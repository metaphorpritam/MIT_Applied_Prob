Q95. ★ · Transition matrices · G5 §1.1

A student proposes the following as the transition matrix of a three-state chain on $\{1,2,3\}$, where $p_{ij}=\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i)$: $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.3&0.3\\0.2&0.5&0.3\\0.4&-0.1&0.7\end{pmatrix}.$$ (a) List every way this fails the definition of a transition matrix. (b) The student also worries that "the columns don't add to 1". Is that a defect? (c) Repair the matrix, touching only the rows that are defective, and confirm your repair.

Solution

Setup. A matrix $P$ is a transition matrix iff (i) $p_{ij}\ge0$ for all $i,j$, and (ii) every row sums to $1$, because row $i$ is the conditional PMF of $X_{n+1}$ given $X_n=i$, and a PMF is nonnegative and sums to $1$.

Step 1 (row sums). Row 1: $0.5+0.3+0.3=1.1$, which exceeds $1$ by $0.1$. Row 2: $0.2+0.5+0.3=1.0\;\checkmark$. Row 3: $0.4+(-0.1)+0.7=1.0$ — it sums correctly, but only because a negative entry cancels an excess.

Step 2 (nonnegativity). $p_{32}=-0.1<0$. A conditional probability cannot be negative, so this is a second, independent violation. So there are exactly two defects: row 1 does not sum to 1, and $p_{32}$ is negative.

Step 3 (part b — the column question). The column sums are $1.1,\;0.7,\;1.3$. This is not a defect. Column $j$ collects $p_{1j},p_{2j},p_{3j}$, which are probabilities of the same event $\{X_{n+1}=j\}$ under three different conditioning events. There is no rule forcing them to add to anything in particular. (Matrices whose columns happen to also sum to 1 are called doubly stochastic and are special — see Q108.)

Step 4 (part c — the repair). Fix row 1 by removing the excess $0.1$ from one entry, say $p_{13}:0.3\to0.2$. Fix row 3 by replacing $p_{32}=-0.1$ with $p_{32}=0.1$ and absorbing the change into $p_{33}:0.7\to0.5$ so the row still sums to 1. The result is $$P'=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.3&0.2\\0.2&0.5&0.3\\0.4&0.1&0.5\end{pmatrix},$$ with row sums $1.0,\;1.0,\;1.0\;\checkmark$ and all entries in $[0,1]\;\checkmark$. Its column sums are $1.1,\;0.9,\;1.0$ — still not 1, and still fine.

Answer. $\;$(a) row 1 sums to $1.1$, and $p_{32}=-0.1<0$; (b) no — column sums are unconstrained; (c) e.g. $p_{13}=0.2$, $p_{32}=0.1$, $p_{33}=0.5$, giving row sums $(1,1,1)$.

Trap. The tempting wrong answer is "row 3 is fine, it sums to 1". Summing to 1 is necessary but not sufficient: nonnegativity is a separate requirement, and a negative entry hidden by a compensating large entry still destroys the probabilistic meaning of the row.

Q96. ★ · $n$-step probabilities · G5 §1.3

A chain on $\{1,2,3\}$ has transition matrix $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.4&0.1\\0.2&0.3&0.5\\0&0.6&0.4\end{pmatrix}.$$ Write $r_{ij}(n)=\mathbf{P}(X_n=j\mid X_0=i)$. Compute (a) $\mathbf{P}(X_1=2,\;X_2=3\mid X_0=1)$, (b) $r_{13}(2)$, and (c) $r_{13}(3)$.

Solution

Setup. Part (a) is a single path, so multiply transition probabilities along it. Parts (b),(c) need the Chapman–Kolmogorov recursion $r_{ij}(n)=\sum_{k}r_{ik}(n-1)\,p_{kj}$, which is total probability over the state at time $n-1$ with the Markov property used to drop the earlier history.

Step 1 (part a). By the multiplication rule and the Markov property, $$\mathbf{P}(X_1=2,X_2=3\mid X_0=1)=p_{12}\,p_{23}=(0.4)(0.5)=0.2 .$$

Step 2 (part b). Sum over the intermediate state $k$ at time 1: $$r_{13}(2)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}p_{1k}p_{k3}=(0.5)(0.1)+(0.4)(0.5)+(0.1)(0.4)=0.05+0.2+0.04=0.29 .$$ The middle term $0.2$ is exactly part (a) — one of three disjoint routes.

Step 3 (the full two-step row). For part (c) we need all of row 1 of $P^2$: $$r_{11}(2)=(0.5)(0.5)+(0.4)(0.2)+(0.1)(0)=0.25+0.08+0=0.33,$$ $$r_{12}(2)=(0.5)(0.4)+(0.4)(0.3)+(0.1)(0.6)=0.20+0.12+0.06=0.38,$$ and $r_{13}(2)=0.29$ from Step 2. Check: $0.33+0.38+0.29=1.00\;\checkmark$.

Step 4 (part c). Apply the recursion once more, now summing over the state at time 2: $$r_{13}(3)=\sum_{k=1}^{3}r_{1k}(2)\,p_{k3}=(0.33)(0.1)+(0.38)(0.5)+(0.29)(0.4)=0.033+0.19+0.116=0.339 .$$ A Monte-Carlo run of $400{,}000$ paths gives $0.2901$ and $0.3372$ for (b) and (c), confirming both.

Answer. $\;$(a) $0.2$, (b) $r_{13}(2)=0.29$, (c) $r_{13}(3)=0.339$.

Trap. A careless reader reports $0.2$ for part (b) as well, having found "the" path $1\to2\to3$. $r_{13}(2)$ is the probability of being at 3 at time 2 by any route, including $1\to1\to3$ and $1\to3\to3$; those add another $0.05+0.04=0.09$.

Q97. ★★ · Building a chain from a story · G5 §1.1–1.3

Every Monday a streaming subscriber is in one of three states: Active, Paused, or Cancelled. From Active, next Monday she is Active with probability $0.80$, Paused with probability $0.15$, and Cancelled with probability $0.05$. From Paused, she is Active with probability $0.30$, Paused with probability $0.55$, and Cancelled with probability $0.15$. Cancellation is permanent. (a) Write the transition matrix with states ordered $(A,P,C)$. (b) Given that she is Active this Monday, what is the probability she is Cancelled three Mondays later? (c) A colleague says "the cancellation rate is $5\%$ per week, so over three weeks it is about $3\times0.05=0.15$." Why is his number wrong, and in which direction?

Solution

Setup. Each sentence of the story is one row of $P$. "Cancellation is permanent" means $C$ is absorbing: $p_{CC}=1$ and $p_{CA}=p_{CP}=0$.

Step 1 (part a). $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.80&0.15&0.05\\0.30&0.55&0.15\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},$$ row sums $0.80+0.15+0.05=1$, $0.30+0.55+0.15=1$, $0+0+1=1\;\checkmark$.

Step 2 (two steps from A). Using $r_{Aj}(2)=\sum_k p_{Ak}p_{kj}$, $$r_{AA}(2)=(0.80)(0.80)+(0.15)(0.30)+(0.05)(0)=0.64+0.045=0.685,$$ $$r_{AP}(2)=(0.80)(0.15)+(0.15)(0.55)+(0.05)(0)=0.12+0.0825=0.2025,$$ $$r_{AC}(2)=(0.80)(0.05)+(0.15)(0.15)+(0.05)(1)=0.04+0.0225+0.05=0.1125 .$$ Check: $0.685+0.2025+0.1125=1.0000\;\checkmark$.

Step 3 (part b — three steps). Recurse once more into column $C$: $$r_{AC}(3)=r_{AA}(2)\,p_{AC}+r_{AP}(2)\,p_{PC}+r_{AC}(2)\,p_{CC}$$ $$=(0.685)(0.05)+(0.2025)(0.15)+(0.1125)(1)=0.034250+0.030375+0.112500=0.177125 .$$ Note the third term: probability already absorbed stays absorbed, which is why $p_{CC}=1$ carries it forward. A Monte-Carlo run of $400{,}000$ subscribers gives $0.1765\;\checkmark$.

Step 4 (part c). The colleague's $0.15$ is wrong for two compounding reasons, which push in opposite directions. Adding $0.05$ three times double-counts: after week 1 the subscriber may already be Cancelled, so the same event is counted again. That inflates the estimate. But he also ignores the Paused state, which cancels at the higher rate $0.15$ and which the subscriber enters with probability $0.15$ per week. Numerically the net effect is that the true value $0.177125$ exceeds $0.15$: the Paused channel more than offsets the double count.

Answer. $\;$(a) as displayed; (b) $\mathbf{P}(X_3=C\mid X_0=A)=0.177125$; (c) $0.15$ neither accounts for absorption already having occurred nor for the higher hazard from Paused, and the true answer is larger.

Trap. Writing $1-(0.95)^3=0.142625$ is the "improved" wrong answer: it fixes the double count but still assumes the cancellation probability is $0.05$ every week regardless of state, which is false as soon as the subscriber pauses.

Q98. ★★ · Initial distributions and running the chain backwards · G5 §1.3

A web server is Up (state 1) or Down (state 2) each hour, with $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.9&0.1\\0.4&0.6\end{pmatrix},$$ and at hour $0$ it is Up with probability $0.3$ and Down with probability $0.7$. (a) Compute $\mathbf{P}(X_2=1)$. (b) Compute $\mathbf{P}(X_0=1\mid X_1=1)$. (c) A classmate says "the Markov property says the past doesn't matter, so $\mathbf{P}(X_0=1\mid X_1=1)=\mathbf{P}(X_0=1)=0.3$." Diagnose the error.

Solution

Setup. Write the time-$n$ distribution as a row vector $\pi^{(n)}=(\mathbf{P}(X_n=1),\mathbf{P}(X_n=2))$. Then $\pi^{(n+1)}=\pi^{(n)}P$, which is the total probability theorem applied to the state one step earlier. Part (b) runs the other way and therefore needs Bayes' rule.

Step 1 (one step). $$\mathbf{P}(X_1=1)=\mathbf{P}(X_0=1)p_{11}+\mathbf{P}(X_0=2)p_{21}=(0.3)(0.9)+(0.7)(0.4)=0.27+0.28=0.55,$$ so $\pi^{(1)}=(0.55,\,0.45)$.

Step 2 (part a — second step). $$\mathbf{P}(X_2=1)=(0.55)(0.9)+(0.45)(0.4)=0.495+0.180=0.675 .$$

Step 3 (part b — Bayes). The joint probability of the numerator event was already computed in Step 1 as the first term: $$\mathbf{P}(X_0=1,\;X_1=1)=\mathbf{P}(X_0=1)\,p_{11}=(0.3)(0.9)=0.27 .$$ Divide by $\mathbf{P}(X_1=1)=0.55$: $$\mathbf{P}(X_0=1\mid X_1=1)=\frac{0.27}{0.55}=0.490909 .$$ (Complement: $0.28/0.55=0.509091$; the two sum to $1\;\checkmark$.) A Monte-Carlo run of $600{,}000$ paths gives $0.4905\;\checkmark$.

Step 4 (part c — the diagnosis). The Markov property says the future is conditionally independent of the past given the present: $\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i,\text{ earlier})=p_{ij}$. It says nothing about the past given the future. Here observing $X_1=1$ is evidence about $X_0$, because state 1 is much easier to reach from state 1 ($0.9$) than from state 2 ($0.4$); that evidence raises the posterior on $X_0=1$ from the prior $0.3$ to $0.490909$. Independence of past and future would require $\mathbf{P}(X_0=1,X_1=1)=\mathbf{P}(X_0=1)\mathbf{P}(X_1=1)=(0.3)(0.55)=0.165$, but the true joint is $0.27$.

Answer. $\;$(a) $\mathbf{P}(X_2=1)=0.675$; (b) $\mathbf{P}(X_0=1\mid X_1=1)=0.490909$; (c) the Markov property conditions on the present and constrains the future, not the past.

Trap. $0.3$ is the tempting answer, and it is the prior, not the posterior. A second trap in part (a) is reading $r_{11}(2)=0.85$ off $P^2$ and reporting it — that is $\mathbf{P}(X_2=1\mid X_0=1)$, which ignores the initial distribution.

Q99. ★★★ · When the obvious state is not a state · G5 §1.1

Two coins sit on a table: coin $A$ lands heads with probability $0.8$, coin $B$ with probability $0.3$. At time $0$ you pick one of the two uniformly at random, you are not told which, and from then on you flip that same coin forever, the flips being conditionally independent given the coin. Let $X_n\in\{H,T\}$ be the outcome of flip $n$, $n=1,2,\dots$ (a) Show that $\{X_n\}$ is not a Markov chain on $\{H,T\}$ by comparing $\mathbf{P}(X_2=H\mid X_1=H)$ with $\mathbf{P}(X_3=H\mid X_2=H,X_1=H)$. (b) Enlarge the state so that the Markov property holds, write the transition matrix, and classify the resulting chain.

Solution

Setup. Let $C\in\{A,B\}$ be the identity of the chosen coin, $\mathbf{P}(C=A)=0.5$. Given $C$, the flips are i.i.d. Bernoulli. Unconditionally the flips are exchangeable but not independent, because every flip carries information about $C$, and $C$ affects every future flip.

Step 1 (the joint probabilities of head runs). Condition on the coin and use conditional independence: $$\mathbf{P}(X_1=H)=(0.5)(0.8)+(0.5)(0.3)=0.40+0.15=0.55,$$ $$\mathbf{P}(X_1=X_2=H)=(0.5)(0.8)^2+(0.5)(0.3)^2=(0.5)(0.64)+(0.5)(0.09)=0.320+0.045=0.365,$$ $$\mathbf{P}(X_1=X_2=X_3=H)=(0.5)(0.8)^3+(0.5)(0.3)^3=(0.5)(0.512)+(0.5)(0.027)=0.2560+0.0135=0.2695 .$$

Step 2 (part a — the two conditionals). $$\mathbf{P}(X_2=H\mid X_1=H)=\frac{0.365}{0.55}=0.663636,\qquad \mathbf{P}(X_3=H\mid X_2=H,X_1=H)=\frac{0.2695}{0.365}=0.738356 .$$ The same "current state" $H$ gives two different predictions, differing by $0.074720$. If $\{X_n\}$ were Markov on $\{H,T\}$ both would equal $p_{HH}$. So the Markov property fails.

Step 3 (why, in words). The extra head in the conditioning event is evidence about the coin. By Bayes' rule, $$\mathbf{P}(C=A\mid X_1=X_2=H)=\frac{(0.5)(0.64)}{0.365}=\frac{0.320}{0.365}=0.876712,$$ and then the predictive probability is the mixture $$(0.876712)(0.8)+(1-0.876712)(0.3)=0.701370+0.036986=0.738356\;\checkmark,$$ matching Step 2. The single symbol $H$ is a lossy summary: it does not record how much evidence has accumulated about $C$.

Step 4 (part b — the repair). Take the state to be the pair $Y_n=(C,X_n)$, four states $(A,H),(A,T),(B,H),(B,T)$. Since $C$ never changes and the next flip depends only on $C$, $$p_{(A,\cdot)\to(A,H)}=0.8,\quad p_{(A,\cdot)\to(A,T)}=0.2,\quad p_{(B,\cdot)\to(B,H)}=0.3,\quad p_{(B,\cdot)\to(B,T)}=0.7,$$ all other entries $0$. Ordering the states $(A,H),(A,T),(B,H),(B,T)$, $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.8&0.2&0&0\\0.8&0.2&0&0\\0&0&0.3&0.7\\0&0&0.3&0.7\end{pmatrix},$$ row sums all $1\;\checkmark$. This is Markov because the transition law depends on the past only through $(C,X_n)$, and in fact only through $C$.

Step 5 (classification). $P$ is block diagonal: $\{(A,H),(A,T)\}$ and $\{(B,H),(B,T)\}$ are two separate recurrent classes, each aperiodic, with no transient states. Consequently the limit remembers the start: $P^{10}$ from $(A,H)$ is $(0.8,\,0.2,\,0,\,0)$, whereas from $(B,H)$ it is $(0,\,0,\,0.3,\,0.7)$. That is exactly the modeling content of the story — you never learn which coin you have with certainty, and the chain never forgets it.

Answer. $\;$(a) $0.663636\ne0.738356$, so $\{X_n\}$ is not Markov on $\{H,T\}$; (b) $Y_n=(C,X_n)$ is Markov, with the block-diagonal $P$ above — two aperiodic recurrent classes, no transient states.

Trap. "The flips are independent tosses of one coin, so of course the process is Markov (indeed i.i.d.)." They are independent only conditionally on $C$. Marginalizing over an unknown, permanent parameter creates dependence that reaches arbitrarily far back — the whole history matters, which is why the repair must put the parameter into the state.

Q100. ★ · Recurrent and transient classes · G5 §2.1–2.3

A chain on $\{1,\dots,6\}$ has the nonzero transitions $$p_{11}=0.2,\;p_{12}=0.3,\;p_{13}=0.5;\qquad p_{21}=0.4,\;p_{25}=0.6;$$ $$p_{34}=1;\qquad p_{43}=0.7,\;p_{44}=0.3;\qquad p_{56}=1;\qquad p_{65}=1 .$$ Identify the recurrent classes, the transient states, and the period of each recurrent class.

Solution

Setup. State $j$ is accessible from $i$ if $r_{ij}(n)>0$ for some $n\ge0$. State $i$ is recurrent if every $j$ accessible from $i$ can reach $i$ back; otherwise it is transient. Recurrent states split into classes of mutually accessible states.

Step 1 (accessible sets). From 3 you go to 4 and from 4 back to 3, and nothing else is reachable: the accessible set of 3 is $\{3,4\}$. Likewise 5 goes to 6 and 6 to 5, so the accessible set of 5 is $\{5,6\}$. Both sets are closed (no arrow leaves them), so $\{3,4\}$ and $\{5,6\}$ are recurrent classes.

Step 2 (the transient states). From 1 you can reach 3 (probability $0.5$), and from 3 there is no path back to 1, since $\{3,4\}$ is closed. Hence 1 is transient. From 2 you can reach 5 with probability $0.6$, and $\{5,6\}$ is closed, so 2 is transient too. States 1 and 2 communicate with each other ($1\to2$ and $2\to1$), so they form one transient class $\{1,2\}$.

Step 3 (periods). Class $\{3,4\}$: state 4 has a self-transition $p_{44}=0.3>0$, so a return to 4 is possible in $1$ step and also in $2$ steps ($4\to3\to4$); $\gcd(1,2)=1$, so the class is aperiodic. Class $\{5,6\}$: every arrow goes $5\to6$ or $6\to5$, so returns to 5 occur only at even times; $\gcd$ of the possible return times is $2$, so this class has period $2$.

Step 4 (what this predicts). The chain has two recurrent classes, so the limit of $r_{ij}(n)$ depends on the starting state, and one of the classes is periodic, so for starts leading into $\{5,6\}$ the limit fails to exist at all. Numerically, from state 1, $r_{1\cdot}(200)=(0,\,0,\,0.302768,\,0.432526,\,0.215686,\,0.049020)$ while $r_{1\cdot}(201)=(0,\,0,\,0.302768,\,0.432526,\,0.049020,\,0.215686)$ — the $\{3,4\}$ entries have settled, the $\{5,6\}$ entries oscillate forever.

Answer. $\;$Recurrent classes $\{3,4\}$ (aperiodic) and $\{5,6\}$ (period 2); transient states $1$ and $2$.

Trap. Calling $\{1,2\}$ a recurrent class because 1 and 2 communicate with each other. Communication is not enough — the class must also be closed, and $\{1,2\}$ leaks out through $p_{13}$ and $p_{25}$.

Q101. ★★ · Periodicity · G5 §2.3

A chain on $\{1,2,3,4\}$ has $p_{12}=1$, $p_{23}=1$, $p_{31}=0.6$, $p_{34}=0.4$, $p_{41}=1$, and no other nonzero entries. (a) Is the chain irreducible (one recurrent class)? (b) Find its period. (c) True or false, with reason: "a chain in which no state has a self-transition must be periodic." (d) For which $n\le12$ is $r_{11}(n)=0$?

Solution

Setup. The period of a recurrent class containing state $i$ is $d=\gcd\{n\ge1:r_{ii}(n)>0\}$; every state in a class has the same period. The quickest route to $d$ is to find the lengths of the cycles through one state and take their gcd.

Step 1 (part a). From 1 you reach 2, 3, then 1 again, and also 4 via $3\to4$; from 4 you return to 1. So all four states are mutually accessible and the chain is irreducible — one recurrent class, no transient states.

Step 2 (part b — the cycles through state 1). There are exactly two simple cycles through 1: $$1\to2\to3\to1\quad\text{(length }3),\qquad 1\to2\to3\to4\to1\quad\text{(length }4).$$ Every return to 1 is a concatenation of these, so the set of possible return times is $\{3a+4b:a,b\ge0,\;a+b\ge1\}=\{3,4,6,7,8,9,10,\dots\}$. Its gcd is $\gcd(3,4)=1$, so the chain is aperiodic, $d=1$.

Step 3 (part c). False, and this chain is the counterexample: no state has a self-transition, yet the chain is aperiodic. Aperiodicity needs only two return times whose gcd is 1; a self-transition is one convenient way to get that (a return time of 1 forces $d=1$) but it is not necessary.

Step 4 (part d). The impossible return times are the positive integers not representable as $3a+4b$: $n=1,2,5$. Direct computation of $r_{11}(n)$ for $n=1,\dots,12$ gives $$0,\;0,\;0.6,\;0.4,\;0,\;0.36,\;0.48,\;0.16,\;0.216,\;0.432,\;0.288,\;0.1936,$$ zero exactly at $n=1,2,5$ and strictly positive for every $n\ge6\;\checkmark$ — the hallmark of an aperiodic chain. Consistently, $r_{1\cdot}(100)$ and $r_{1\cdot}(101)$ both equal $(0.294118,\,0.294118,\,0.294118,\,0.117647)=(5/17,5/17,5/17,2/17)$: the limit exists and does not oscillate.

Answer. $\;$(a) yes, irreducible; (b) period $d=\gcd(3,4)=1$, aperiodic; (c) false — this chain has no self-transitions and is aperiodic; (d) $r_{11}(n)=0$ exactly for $n=1,2,5$.

Trap. Reading the "obvious" loop $1\to2\to3\to1$ and answering "period 3". The period is the gcd over all return times, and the branch $3\to4$ supplies a return of length 4, which collapses the gcd to 1.

Q102. ★★ · The two ways convergence fails · G5 §2.4

The steady-state convergence theorem says: if a chain has a single recurrent class and that class is aperiodic, then $r_{ij}(n)\to\pi_j$ for every $i$. For each of the two chains below, say which hypothesis fails, and describe precisely what $r_{ij}(n)$ actually does.

Chain A on $\{1,2\}$: $p_{12}=p_{21}=1$.    Chain B on $\{1,2,3,4\}$: $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.7&0.3&0&0\\0.4&0.6&0&0\\0&0&0.5&0.5\\0&0&0.1&0.9\end{pmatrix}.$$

Solution

Setup. Two hypotheses, two failure modes. Periodicity destroys the existence of the limit; multiple recurrent classes destroy its independence of $i$.

Step 1 (Chain A: the aperiodicity hypothesis fails). The chain is irreducible — a single recurrent class $\{1,2\}$ — so that hypothesis holds. But every return to 1 takes an even number of steps, so $d=2$. Concretely $$r_{11}(n)=\begin{cases}1,&n\text{ even},\\0,&n\text{ odd},\end{cases}$$ so $r_{11}(10)=1$ and $r_{11}(11)=0$: no limit exists. What is still true is that the balance equations $\pi_1=\pi_2$, $\pi_1+\pi_2=1$ have the unique solution $\pi=(0.5,0.5)$, and the frequency interpretation survives: the long-run fraction of time in state 1 is $0.5$. Averaging the first $2000$ powers of $P$ gives row 1 equal to $(0.5,\,0.5)$ to six decimals $\checkmark$. So $\pi$ is still meaningful as a time-average, just not as a limit of $r_{ij}(n)$.

Step 2 (Chain B: the single-class hypothesis fails). $P$ is block diagonal, so $\{1,2\}$ and $\{3,4\}$ are two separate closed classes, each recurrent and each aperiodic (self-transitions $p_{11},p_{22},p_{33},p_{44}>0$). Every $r_{ij}(n)$ converges — but to a limit that depends on where you started.

Step 3 (the two limits). Solve balance inside each block. Block $\{1,2\}$: $0.3\pi_1=0.4\pi_2$, so $\pi_1/\pi_2=4/3$ and with $\pi_1+\pi_2=1$, $$(\pi_1,\pi_2)=(4/7,\,3/7)=(0.571429,\,0.428571).$$ Block $\{3,4\}$: $0.5\pi_3=0.1\pi_4$, so $\pi_3/\pi_4=1/5$ and $$(\pi_3,\pi_4)=(1/6,\,5/6)=(0.166667,\,0.833333).$$ Numerically $P^{200}$ has rows 1 and 2 equal to $(0.571429,\,0.428571,\,0,\,0)$ and rows 3 and 4 equal to $(0,\,0,\,0.166667,\,0.833333)\;\checkmark$. In particular $r_{13}(n)\to0$ while $r_{33}(n)\to0.166667$: the "steady state" is not one vector but two, selected by the initial state.

Answer. $\;$Chain A fails aperiodicity ($d=2$): $r_{11}(n)$ oscillates $1,0,1,0,\dots$ and has no limit, though the time-average frequencies are still $(0.5,0.5)$. Q103. ★★★ · True or false, with reason · G5 §2.1–2.4

Decide true or false and justify each. (a) Every finite-state Markov chain has at least one recurrent state. (b) If a chain has exactly one recurrent class, then $r_{ij}(n)$ converges to a limit that does not depend on $i$. (c) If a chain has a single aperiodic recurrent class together with some transient states, then $\pi_j=0$ for every transient $j$.

Solution

Setup. Recall: $i$ is recurrent iff every state accessible from $i$ can reach $i$ back; $\pi_j$ is the unique solution of the balance equations $\pi_j=\sum_k\pi_kp_{kj}$ with $\sum_j\pi_j=1$, and equals the long-run fraction of time spent in $j$.

Step 1 (part a — TRUE). Suppose every state were transient. Start at any state $i_1$. Because $i_1$ is transient, there is a state $i_2$ accessible from $i_1$ that cannot reach $i_1$ back. Then the accessible set of $i_2$ is a strict subset of that of $i_1$: it is contained in it (anything reachable from $i_2$ is reachable from $i_1$) and it omits $i_1$. Repeat from $i_2$. Each step strictly shrinks a subset of a finite state space, so the process must terminate — and it terminates exactly at a state that is not transient. Hence at least one recurrent state exists. (This argument uses finiteness essentially; an infinite chain such as the unbiased random walk on $\mathbb{Z}$ modified to drift right can have all states transient.)

Step 2 (part b — FALSE). The single-class hypothesis is not sufficient; aperiodicity is also needed. Counterexample: the two-state chain $p_{12}=p_{21}=1$ is irreducible, hence has exactly one recurrent class $\{1,2\}$, yet $$r_{11}(200)=1,\qquad r_{11}(201)=0,$$ so $r_{11}(n)$ has no limit at all. The correct statement adds "and that class is aperiodic".

Step 3 (part c — TRUE). $\pi_j$ is the long-run fraction of time in $j$. A transient state is visited only finitely many times with probability 1, so the number of visits in $n$ steps stays bounded while $n\to\infty$, and the fraction tends to $0$. Algebraically the balance equations force the same conclusion, since the unique normalized solution puts all mass on the recurrent class. Example: with $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.5&0\\0&0.6&0.4\\0&0.3&0.7\end{pmatrix}$$ state 1 is transient (it can reach the closed class $\{2,3\}$ but not return) and the balance equations give $$\pi=(0,\;3/7,\;4/7)=(0,\;0.428571,\;0.571429),$$ confirmed by $r_{1\cdot}(200)=(0,\,0.428571,\,0.571429)$.

Answer. $\;$(a) True; (b) False — aperiodicity is also required, e.g. $p_{12}=p_{21}=1$; (c) True, $\pi_j=0$ for transient $j$.

Trap. In (c), reading $\pi_j=0$ as "the chain never visits $j$". It does visit $j$ — possibly many times, and $\mathbf{P}(X_0=j)$ may even be 1 — but the number of visits is finite, so its share of an infinitely long run is zero. $\pi_j$ measures frequency, not possibility.

Q105. ★★ · Balance equations, three states · G5 §3.2–3.3

Answer. $\;\pi=(49/120,\,5/12,\,7/40)=(0.408333,\,0.416667,\,0.175)$; deliveries on $40.8333\%$ of days, about $149$ per year.

Q107. ★★ · Spot the error: local balance · G5 §3.4

A chain on $\{0,1,2,3\}$ has $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.4&0.4&0.2&0\\0.3&0.4&0.3&0\\0&0.5&0.2&0.3\\0&0&0.6&0.4\end{pmatrix}.$$ A student writes "this is a birth–death chain, so I can use local balance": $\pi_0p_{01}=\pi_1p_{10}$, i.e. $0.4\pi_0=0.3\pi_1$, giving $\pi_1=\frac43\pi_0$. (a) Identify the error. (b) Write the correct cut equation across the boundary separating $\{0\}$ from $\{1,2,3\}$ and deduce the true ratio $\pi_1/\pi_0$. (c) The true steady state is $\pi=(5/27,\,10/27,\,8/27,\,4/27)$; verify it satisfies the cut equation separating $\{0,1\}$ from $\{2,3\}$.

Solution

Setup. Cut (or "flow balance") equations are always valid for any partition of the state space: in steady state, the long-run rate of transitions from $S$ to $S^{c}$ equals the rate from $S^{c}$ to $S$, because every crossing one way must eventually be matched by a crossing back. The local balance shortcut $\pi_ip_{i,i+1}=\pi_{i+1}p_{i+1,i}$ is the special case where the only way across the cut $\{0,\dots,i\}\mid\{i+1,\dots\}$ is the single pair of arrows $i\leftrightarrow i+1$ — i.e. a genuine birth–death chain.

Step 1 (part a — the error). This chain is not birth–death: $p_{02}=0.2>0$ is a jump of two states at once. So the cut between $\{0\}$ and $\{1,2,3\}$ is crossed not only by $0\to1$ but also by $0\to2$, and the student's equation leaves out that flow. The chain also lacks the return arrow $2\to0$, so the two sides of his equation are not even the same cut.

Step 2 (part b — the correct cut). Flow out of $\{0\}$: $\pi_0(p_{01}+p_{02})=\pi_0(0.4+0.2)=0.6\,\pi_0$. Flow into $\{0\}$: $\pi_1p_{10}+\pi_2p_{20}+\pi_3p_{30}=0.3\,\pi_1+0+0=0.3\,\pi_1$. Equating, $$0.6\,\pi_0=0.3\,\pi_1\;\Longrightarrow\;\pi_1=2\,\pi_0 .$$ The true ratio is $2$, not the student's $4/3=1.333333$ — a $50\%$ error, and it propagates. Carrying his pairwise ratios all the way up and normalizing gives $\pi^{\text{student}}=(0.283019,\,0.377358,\,0.226415,\,0.113208)$ against the truth $(0.185185,\,0.370370,\,0.296296,\,0.148148)$: he overstates $\pi_0$ by $0.097834$, because he has ignored an escape route out of state 0 and so made state 0 look stickier than it is.

Step 3 (part c — verify the other cut). Across $\{0,1\}\mid\{2,3\}$ the crossing arrows are $0\to2$ ($0.2$), $1\to2$ ($0.3$) going out, and $2\to1$ ($0.5$) coming back ($p_{30}=p_{31}=0$). With $\pi_0=5/27=0.185185$, $\pi_1=10/27=0.370370$, $\pi_2=8/27=0.296296$: $$\text{out}=\pi_0(0.2)+\pi_1(0.3)=0.037037+0.111111=0.148148,$$ $$\text{in}=\pi_2(0.5)=(0.296296)(0.5)=0.148148\;\checkmark .$$ The two match exactly, confirming both $\pi$ and the principle: cut equations hold in general, pairwise local balance does not.

Answer. $\;$(a) the chain is not birth–death ($p_{02}=0.2$), so local balance does not apply; (b) $0.6\pi_0=0.3\pi_1$, hence $\pi_1=2\pi_0$; (c) both sides of the $\{0,1\}\mid\{2,3\}$ cut equal $0.148148\;\checkmark$.

Trap. Concluding "cut equations are unreliable here, use the full balance equations". Cut equations are perfectly reliable — what fails is the assumption that a cut is crossed by exactly one arrow in each direction. Write the cut with all crossing arrows and it works on any chain.

In the interior, moving up requires an arrival and no departure and moving down requires a departure and no arrival, so for $i=1,2,3$ $$p_{i,i+1}=p(1-q)=0.1,\qquad p_{i,i-1}=q(1-p)=0.4 .$$ At the boundaries the mechanism changes: from $0$ no departure is possible, so $p_{01}=p=0.2$; at $4$ an arrival is blocked and lost, so $p_{43}=q=0.5$. The full matrix, with the self-transition probabilities filling each row to 1, is $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.8&0.2&0&0&0\\0.4&0.5&0.1&0&0\\0&0.4&0.5&0.1&0\\0&0&0.4&0.5&0.1\\0&0&0&0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix},$$ row sums all $1\;\checkmark$.

Step 1 (part a). $$\rho=\frac{p(1-q)}{q(1-p)}=\frac{(0.2)(0.5)}{(0.5)(0.8)}=\frac{0.1}{0.4}=0.25 .$$ Since $\rho<1$ the queue is stable: in the interior, going down is four times as likely as going up, so the distribution decays geometrically by a factor $0.25$ per ticket.

Step 2 (part b — the four cut equations). The boundary cuts do not use $\rho$: $$\pi_0(0.2)=\pi_1(0.4)\;\Longrightarrow\;\pi_1=\tfrac{0.2}{0.4}\pi_0=0.5\,\pi_0,$$ $$\pi_1(0.1)=\pi_2(0.4)\;\Longrightarrow\;\pi_2=0.25\,\pi_1=0.125\,\pi_0,$$ $$\pi_2(0.1)=\pi_3(0.4)\;\Longrightarrow\;\pi_3=0.25\,\pi_2=0.03125\,\pi_0,$$ $$\pi_3(0.1)=\pi_4(0.5)\;\Longrightarrow\;\pi_4=0.2\,\pi_3=0.00625\,\pi_0 .$$

Step 3 (normalize). The relative weights are $1,\;0.5,\;0.125,\;0.03125,\;0.00625$, summing to $1.6625$. Hence $\pi_0=1/1.6625=0.601504$ and $$\pi=(0.601504,\;0.300752,\;0.075188,\;0.018797,\;0.003759).$$ Solving the full $5\times5$ balance system numerically reproduces this exactly, and $200{,}000$ simulated chains give $(0.6022,\,0.3005,\,0.0745,\,0.0192,\,0.0037)\;\checkmark$.

Step 4 (part c). $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{i=0}^{4}i\,\pi_i=0.300752+2(0.075188)+3(0.018797)+4(0.003759)$$ $$=0.300752+0.150376+0.056391+0.015038=0.522556\ \text{tickets}.$$

Step 5 (part d — unlimited buffer). Now $\pi_i=0.5\,\rho^{\,i-1}\pi_0$ for every $i\ge1$, so $$\frac{1}{\pi_0}=1+0.5\sum_{i\ge1}\rho^{\,i-1}=1+\frac{0.5}{1-0.25}=1+\frac{0.5}{0.75}=1.666667\;\Longrightarrow\;\pi_0=0.6 .$$ For the mean, use $\sum_{i\ge1}i\,r^{\,i-1}=1/(1-r)^2$ with $r=\rho=0.25$: $$\mathbb{E}[X]=0.6(0.5)\sum_{i\ge1}i\,\rho^{\,i-1}=\frac{0.3}{(0.75)^2}=\frac{0.3}{0.5625}=\frac{8}{15}=0.533333 ,$$ matching a direct 400-term summation $\checkmark$. So capping the buffer at 4 costs almost nothing: $0.522556$ versus $0.533333$, because $\pi_4=0.003759$ is already tiny. Truncation only bites when $\rho\to1$, where $\mathbb{E}[X]$ diverges and the buffer becomes the binding constraint.

Answer. $\;\rho=0.25$; $\pi=(0.601504,\,0.300752,\,0.075188,\,0.018797,\,0.003759)$; $\mathbb{E}[X]=0.522556$; with an unlimited buffer $\pi_0=0.6$ and $\mathbb{E}[X]=8/15=0.533333$.

Trap. Writing $\pi_i=\pi_0\rho^{\,i}$ straight down from $i=0$. That ignores the boundary row: at the empty state there is no departure to cancel the arrival, so the up-probability there is $p=0.2$, not $p(1-q)=0.1$, and the first ratio is $0.5$, not $0.25$. The pure-geometric shortcut returns $\pi_0=0.750733$ and $\mathbb{E}[X]=0.328446$ — a $37\%$ underestimate of the queue length. A second trap is $\rho=p/q=0.4$: it forgets that an arrival and a departure in the same slot cancel.

Q111. ★★ · Which system of equations? · G5 §5.1

A chain on $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ has $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.2&0.5&0.1&0.2&0\\0.3&0.1&0.4&0&0.2\\0&0.4&0.2&0.1&0.3\\0&0&0&1&0\\0&0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$ For each question below, name the system of equations you would set up, write its unknowns and boundary conditions, and give the answer. (i) $\mathbf{P}(X_3=2\mid X_0=1)$. (ii) Starting from 1, the probability of ending up absorbed in state 4. (iii) Starting from 1, the expected number of steps until absorption. (iv) The long-run fraction of time spent in state 2.

Solution

Setup. States 4 and 5 are absorbing; $\{1,2,3\}$ is a transient class, since from 1 you can reach 4 and never return. Each of the four questions is a different linear system — recognizing which is the entire skill.

Step 1 (i) — $n$-step recursion. Unknowns $r_{ij}(n)$; recursion $r_{ij}(n)=\sum_kr_{ik}(n-1)p_{kj}$ with initial condition $r_{ij}(0)=\delta_{ij}$. No boundary conditions and no solving — just iterate (equivalently, read off $P^3$). Iterating gives $$\mathbf{P}(X_3=2\mid X_0=1)=0.21 ,$$ confirmed by $400{,}000$ simulated paths ($0.2100$).

Step 2 (ii) — absorption equations. Unknowns $a_i=\mathbf{P}(\text{absorbed at }4\mid X_0=i)$ for the transient $i$; equations $a_i=\sum_{j}p_{ij}a_j$ with the boundary conditions $a_4=1$, $a_5=0$. That is $$a_1=0.2a_1+0.5a_2+0.1a_3+0.2,\quad a_2=0.3a_1+0.1a_2+0.4a_3,\quad a_3=0.4a_2+0.2a_3+0.1 .$$ Solving the $3\times3$ system: $(a_1,a_2,a_3)=(0.446203,\,0.262658,\,0.256329)$, so the answer is $a_1=0.446203$. Simulation gives $0.4461\;\checkmark$. Note there is no "$1+$" here — these are probabilities, and time does not enter.

Step 3 (iii) — expected-time equations. Unknowns $\mu_i=\mathbb{E}[\text{steps to absorption}\mid X_0=i]$; equations $\mu_i=1+\sum_{j\text{ transient}}p_{ij}\mu_j$ with $\mu_4=\mu_5=0$. The "$1+$" counts the step you are about to take. Solving gives $$(\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_3)=(4.082278,\,3.892405,\,3.196203),$$ so the answer is $\mu_1=4.082278$ steps. Same coefficient matrix $I-Q$ as in (ii), different right-hand side (all ones instead of the absorption column).

Step 4 (iv) — balance equations, and a warning. The relevant system is $\pi_j=\sum_k\pi_kp_{kj}$, $\sum_j\pi_j=1$. But state 2 is transient, so it is visited only finitely often and $\pi_2=0$: the long-run fraction of time in state 2 is $0$. Moreover this chain has two recurrent classes $\{4\}$ and $\{5\}$, so the balance system does not have a unique solution — every vector $(0,0,0,\alpha,1-\alpha)$ satisfies it. The correct long-run answer conditional on the start is $(0,0,0,0.446203,\,0.553797)$ from state 1, which is exactly the absorption probabilities from Step 2. This is the sense in which question (iv) is really question (ii) in disguise.

Answer. $\;$(i) $n$-step recursion, $0.21$; (ii) absorption equations with $a_4=1,a_5=0$, $a_1=0.446203$; (iii) expected-time equations with the $1+$ and $\mu_4=\mu_5=0$, $\mu_1=4.082278$; (iv) balance equations — but $\pi_2=0$ because 2 is transient, and the balance system is not uniquely solvable here.

Trap. Setting up the absorption system with a "$1+$" (that is question iii) or the time system without it (that would give $\mu_i=0$). A second trap is expecting a nonzero $\pi_2$: a chain with absorbing states has all its steady-state mass on the absorbing states, so "what fraction of time in state 2?" always answers $0$, however long it lingers there first.

Q112. ★ · Absorption probability by first-step analysis · G5 §4.1

A token sits on one of four squares $1,2,3,4$. Squares 1 and 4 end the game. From square 2 the token moves to 1 with probability $0.3$ and to 3 with probability $0.7$; from square 3 it moves to 2 with probability $0.5$ and to 4 with probability $0.5$. Starting on square 2, what is the probability that the game ends on square 4?

Solution

Setup. Let $a_i=\mathbf{P}(\text{absorbed at }4\mid X_0=i)$. The boundary conditions are $a_4=1$ and $a_1=0$. First-step analysis conditions on the first move and uses the Markov property to restart the same problem from the new state: $a_i=\sum_jp_{ij}a_j$ for the transient $i=2,3$.

Step 1 (the two equations). $$a_2=0.3\,a_1+0.7\,a_3=0.3(0)+0.7a_3=0.7\,a_3,$$ $$a_3=0.5\,a_2+0.5\,a_4=0.5\,a_2+0.5(1)=0.5\,a_2+0.5 .$$

Step 2 (substitute). Put the first into the second: $$a_3=0.5(0.7a_3)+0.5=0.35\,a_3+0.5\;\Longrightarrow\;0.65\,a_3=0.5\;\Longrightarrow\;a_3=\frac{0.5}{0.65}=\frac{10}{13}=0.769231 .$$

Step 3 (back-substitute). $$a_2=0.7\,a_3=0.7\cdot\frac{10}{13}=\frac{7}{13}=0.538462 .$$ Check the first equation directly: $0.3(0)+0.7(0.769231)=0.538462\;\checkmark$. Monte Carlo with $300{,}000$ games gives $0.5382$ from square 2 and $0.7689$ from square 3 $\checkmark$.

Answer. $\;a_2=7/13=0.538462$ (so the game ends on square 1 with probability $6/13=0.461538$).

Trap. Guessing $0.5$ "by symmetry" because square 2 sits between the two ends. The chain is not symmetric: from 2 the token heads toward 4 with probability $0.7$ but toward 1 with only $0.3$, while square 3 is an even coin flip. That bias is what pushes $a_2$ above $0.5$.

Q113. ★★ · Expected time to absorption · G5 §4.2

A beetle wanders among three chambers $1,2,3$ and an exit, state $4$, moving once per minute according to $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.4&0.6&0&0\\0.3&0.2&0.5&0\\0&0.6&0&0.4\\0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$ Starting in chamber 1, how long on average until the beetle exits? Give $\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_3$.

Solution

Setup. Let $\mu_i=\mathbb{E}[\text{minutes until state 4}\mid X_0=i]$, with $\mu_4=0$. First-step analysis: take one step (costing 1 minute), then face the same problem from wherever you land: $$\mu_i=1+\sum_{j=1}^{4}p_{ij}\,\mu_j,\qquad i=1,2,3 .$$

Step 1 (write the three equations). $$\mu_1=1+0.4\mu_1+0.6\mu_2,\qquad \mu_2=1+0.3\mu_1+0.2\mu_2+0.5\mu_3,\qquad \mu_3=1+0.6\mu_2+0.4(0)=1+0.6\mu_2 .$$

Step 2 (eliminate $\mu_1$). From the first, $0.6\mu_1=1+0.6\mu_2$, so $$\mu_1=\frac{1}{0.6}+\mu_2=\frac53+\mu_2=1.666667+\mu_2 .$$

Step 3 (substitute both into the middle equation). $$\mu_2=1+0.3\left(\tfrac53+\mu_2\right)+0.2\mu_2+0.5\left(1+0.6\mu_2\right)$$ $$=1+0.5+0.3\mu_2+0.2\mu_2+0.5+0.3\mu_2=2+0.8\,\mu_2 .$$ Hence $0.2\,\mu_2=2$ and $\mu_2=10$.

Step 4 (back-substitute). $$\mu_3=1+0.6(10)=7,\qquad \mu_1=\tfrac53+10=\tfrac{35}{3}=11.666667 .$$ Check the middle equation: $1+0.3(11.666667)+0.2(10)+0.5(7)=1+3.5+2+3.5=10=\mu_2\;\checkmark$. A Monte-Carlo run of $200{,}000$ beetles started in chamber 1 gives a mean exit time of $11.6482$ minutes $\checkmark$.

Answer. $\;\mu_1=35/3=11.666667$ minutes, $\mu_2=10$, $\mu_3=7$.

Trap. Dropping the "$+1$" and solving $\mu_i=\sum_jp_{ij}\mu_j$. That homogeneous system has only the solution $\mu_1=\mu_2=\mu_3=0$ — a clear signal that you have written the equations for an absorption probability (Q112) rather than an expected time. The $+1$ is the minute spent on the step you just took.

Q114. ★★★ · Gambler's ruin · G5 §4.3

A player starts with $4$ chips and plays rounds until she holds either $0$ chips (she stops, ruined) or $10$ chips (she stops, satisfied). Each round she gains one chip with probability $p=0.45$ and loses one with probability $0.55$, independently. (a) What is the probability she reaches $10$? (b) What is the expected number of rounds she plays? (c) Compare both answers with the fair-game case $p=0.5$ and comment.

Solution

Setup. This is a birth–death chain on $\{0,1,\dots,10\}$ with $0$ and $m=10$ absorbing. Write $a_i$ for the probability of reaching $m$ before $0$ from $i$ chips, and $D_i$ for the expected number of rounds from $i$. The standard results for $p\ne1/2$, with $$\rho=\frac{1-p}{p},$$ are $$a_i=\frac{1-\rho^{\,i}}{1-\rho^{\,m}},\qquad D_i=\frac{i-m\,a_i}{1-2p}.$$ Both come from first-step analysis; $a_i$ from telescoping the differences $a_{i+1}-a_i=\rho^{\,i}(a_1-a_0)$, and $D_i$ from a particular solution linear in $i$ plus the homogeneous solution $A(1-\rho^{\,i})$.

Step 1 (the ratio). $$\rho=\frac{1-0.45}{0.45}=\frac{0.55}{0.45}=\frac{11}{9}=1.222222 .$$ Because $\rho>1$ the walk drifts downward: unfavorable odds.

Step 2 (part a). With $i=4$, $m=10$: $$\rho^{4}=(1.222222)^4=2.231520,\qquad \rho^{10}=(1.222222)^{10}=7.438781,$$ $$a_4=\frac{1-2.231520}{1-7.438781}=\frac{-1.231520}{-6.438781}=0.191266 .$$ So she reaches 10 chips with probability about $19.13\%$, and is ruined with probability $1-0.191266=0.808734$. Monte Carlo over $300{,}000$ sessions gives $0.1914\;\checkmark$.

Step 3 (part b). With $1-2p=1-0.90=0.10$ and $m\,a_4=10(0.191266)=1.912660$: $$D_4=\frac{4-1.912660}{0.10}=\frac{2.087340}{0.10}=20.873404\ \text{rounds}.$$ Simulation gives $20.931\;\checkmark$. Sanity check on the sign: the numerator $i-m\,a_i$ is the expected loss in chips, $4-1.912660=2.087340$, and $1-2p=0.10$ is the expected loss per round, so $D_i$ is total expected loss divided by loss per round — a Wald-style identity, and both quantities are positive, so $D_4>0\;\checkmark$.

Step 4 (part c — the fair game). For $p=0.5$ the formulas degenerate ($\rho=1$ makes both numerator and denominator zero) and the limits are $a_i=i/m$ and $D_i=i(m-i)$: $$a_4=\frac{4}{10}=0.4,\qquad D_4=4(10-4)=24\ \text{rounds}.$$ Comment: shaving the win probability from $0.50$ to $0.45$ — a $5$ percentage-point edge for the house — cuts her success probability from $0.4$ to $0.191266$, less than half. The reason is that the edge compounds: it is the ratio $\rho^{\,i}$ that appears, so the disadvantage is exponentiated by the distance she must travel. The expected duration falls only mildly, from $24$ to $20.873404$ rounds, because most of her sessions end in ruin either way — she just gets there a little faster.

Answer. $\;$(a) $a_4=0.191266$; (b) $D_4=20.873404$ rounds; (c) the fair game would give $0.4$ and $24$ rounds.

Trap. Reporting $4/10=0.4$ on the grounds that "she needs 6 more chips and can afford to lose 4, so it is roughly proportional". That reasoning is exactly the fair-game formula and is off by more than a factor of two here. A second trap is writing $\rho=p/(1-p)=0.818182$ instead of $(1-p)/p$; that inverts the drift and returns $a_4=0.637586$, i.e. it makes an unfavorable game look favorable.
