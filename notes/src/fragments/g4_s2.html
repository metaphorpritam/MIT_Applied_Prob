§2   Random sums and the Bernoulli process

Section 1 turned $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)$ from numbers into random variables, and gave you two machines for using them: the law of iterated expectations $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]$ and the law of total variance $\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big] +\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)$. This section spends that capital twice.

First on a problem those laws were practically invented for: the sum $Y=X_1+X_2+\cdots+X_N$ in which the number of terms is itself random. You visit a random number $N$ of stores and spend a random amount $X_i$ in store $i$ (L12 slide 7); a crate holds a random number $N$ of boxes, each holding a random number $X_i$ of widgets (rec13 P3). No formula from G2 or G3 covers this — $Y$ is not a sum of a fixed number of things, and it is not a function of a single random variable either. Conditioning on $N$ dissolves the difficulty in one stroke, and out fall two of the most-used formulas in the course: $$\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[N]\,\mathbb{E}[X],\qquad \operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)+(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N).$$

Then we start the second half of 6.041: stochastic processes. Until now a probabilistic model produced one outcome, or a handful of random variables. A process produces an infinite sequence of them, indexed by time, and the questions change character — not "what is $\mathbf{P}(X=3)$?" but "how long until the first arrival?", "when does the $k$th arrival happen?", "if I start watching in the middle, what do I see?". The Bernoulli process (L13) is the simplest such model: independent coin flips, one per time slot. Everything about it reduces to the binomial, geometric and Pascal PMFs you already own — and the reason it is worth a whole lecture is that it is the discrete-time rehearsal for the Poisson process of §3–§4, and for the Markov chains of §5 and G5.

Interpretation. The two halves of this section are the same idea seen twice. A random sum is "condition on the count, then un-condition"; the Bernoulli process is "condition on the past, and discover the past does not matter". Both are exercises in choosing what to condition on so that the conditional world is one you already understand.

2.1 Warm-up: the two laws, sharpened (rec13 P1–P2)

Recitation 13 opens by pushing the two laws of §1 slightly past the form in which the lecture stated them. Its first problem — the generalization of the tower property to two conditioning variables — is used constantly later, so we do it first; its second problem is the two-break stick, which §1 has already worked in full, so it appears here only as a pointer.

Example 2.1 (rec13 P1 — generalizations of the law of iterated expectations). Let $X$, $Y$, $Z$ be discrete random variables. Show that (a) $\mathbb{E}[Z]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X,Y]\big]$; (b) $\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X,Y]\mid X\big]$; (c) $\mathbb{E}[Z]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X,Y]\mid X\big]\Big]$.
Solution

Setting up the objects. $\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X=x,Y=y]$ is an ordinary number: the mean of $Z$ computed under the conditional PMF $p_{Z\mid X,Y}(\cdot\mid x,y)$, $$\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X=x,Y=y]=\sum_z z\,p_{Z\mid X,Y}(z\mid x,y).$$ The object $\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X,Y]$ is the random variable that takes the value $\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X=x,Y=y]$ whenever the pair $(X,Y)$ happens to equal $(x,y)$ — i.e. it is the function $g(x,y)=\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X=x,Y=y]$ evaluated at the random pair $(X,Y)$. Exactly as in §1, but with a two-dimensional conditioning variable.

(a). Since $\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X,Y]=g(X,Y)$ is a function of $(X,Y)$, the expected value rule for functions of two random variables (G2 §4) says we may compute its mean by weighting each value $g(x,y)$ by the joint PMF $p_{X,Y}(x,y)$: $$\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X,Y]\big]=\sum_x\sum_y \mathbb{E}[Z\mid X=x,Y=y]\,p_{X,Y}(x,y).$$ Substitute the definition of the inner conditional expectation: $$=\sum_x\sum_y\Big(\sum_z z\,p_{Z\mid X,Y}(z\mid x,y)\Big)p_{X,Y}(x,y) =\sum_x\sum_y\sum_z z\,p_{Z\mid X,Y}(z\mid x,y)\,p_{X,Y}(x,y),$$ where the finite factor $p_{X,Y}(x,y)$ was pushed inside the $z$-sum (it does not depend on $z$). By the multiplication rule for PMFs, $p_{Z\mid X,Y}(z\mid x,y)\,p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_{X,Y,Z}(x,y,z)$, so $$\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X,Y]\big]=\sum_x\sum_y\sum_z z\,p_{X,Y,Z}(x,y,z).$$ [This is where the official solution stops; here is the missing step.] Interchange the order of summation — legitimate for absolutely convergent sums, which is the standing assumption whenever $\mathbb{E}[Z]$ exists — and pull $z$ out of the inner sums: $$=\sum_z z\Big(\sum_x\sum_y p_{X,Y,Z}(x,y,z)\Big)=\sum_z z\,p_Z(z)=\mathbb{E}[Z],$$ because summing a joint PMF over all values of the other two variables is the definition of the marginal $p_Z$ (G2 §4). That proves (a).

(b). Same computation, run inside the conditional universe $\{X=x\}$. In that universe the relevant "prior" on $Y$ is $p_{Y\mid X}(\cdot\mid x)$, so the expected value rule gives $$\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X,Y]\;\big|\;X=x\big] =\sum_y \mathbb{E}[Z\mid X=x,Y=y]\,p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x) =\sum_y\sum_z z\,p_{Z\mid X,Y}(z\mid x,y)\,p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x).$$ The multiplication rule, applied inside the conditional model given $X=x$, says $p_{Z\mid X,Y}(z\mid x,y)\,p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=p_{Y,Z\mid X}(y,z\mid x)$, hence $$=\sum_y\sum_z z\,p_{Y,Z\mid X}(y,z\mid x).$$ [Second gap filled.] Swap the sums, pull out $z$, and marginalize over $y$: $$=\sum_z z\Big(\sum_y p_{Y,Z\mid X}(y,z\mid x)\Big)=\sum_z z\,p_{Z\mid X}(z\mid x) =\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X=x].$$ This holds for every value $x$ with $p_X(x)>0$, and two random variables that agree for every value of $X$ are the same random variable; therefore $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X,Y]\mid X\big]=\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X]$.

(c). Take the (unconditional) expectation of both sides of (b). The left side becomes $\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X,Y]\mid X\big]\Big]$; the right side becomes $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X]\big]$, which is $\mathbb{E}[Z]$ by the ordinary law of iterated expectations of §1. Equating the two gives (c).

Note on the printed solution. The recitation solution writes the left-hand side of (b) as $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X,Y=y]\mid X=x\big]$, mixing the random variable $Y$ with a realized value $Y=y$ inside the same expression; the intended object is $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X,Y]\mid X=x\big]$, as written above.

Interpretation. Part (b) is the useful one, and it has a slogan: conditioning on less, later, is the same as conditioning on more, first, and then averaging out the extra information. $\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X,Y]$ is what you would guess about $Z$ knowing both clues; if you then average over the clue $Y$ you did not actually get, you are back to $\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X]$. Part (a) is the special case where you throw away both clues, and part (c) says the tower can be climbed down one rung at a time or all at once — the answer is the same. This "tower property" is what makes the random-sum derivation of §2.2 legitimate.
Example 2.2 (rec13 P2 — breaking a stick twice; B&T Example 4.17, p. 223). Start with a stick of length $\ell$. Break it at a point chosen uniformly at random over its length and keep the piece containing the left end. Repeat the process on the piece you are left with. (a) Find the expected length after two breaks. (b) Find its variance.

Already done — see §1. This is verbatim the experiment of Example 1.2, and the recitation's two questions are answered there: with $Y$ the length after the first break and $X$ the length after the second, Example 1.2 derives $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=Y/2$ (the step the official solution asserts), Example 1.4 gives $\mathbb{E}[X]=\ell/4$, and Example 1.9 gives $\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)=\ell^2/48$, $\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]=\ell^2/36$ and hence $\operatorname{var}(X)=7\ell^2/144\approx0.048611\,\ell^2$, together with the numerical checks. The third break — the natural follow-up — is Practice 1.6, with $\mathbb{E}[X_3]=\ell/8$ and $\operatorname{var}(X_3)=37\ell^2/1728$.

What rec13 adds. Only the framing: the recitation places this problem immediately after P1 to make the point that the two laws are used together — the same conditioning variable $Y$ serves the iterated-expectation calculation and both terms of the total-variance calculation, so one modelling decision ("condition on the length after the first break") answers both questions. That is the habit §2.2 now industrializes for random sums, where conditioning on the count $N$ does the same double duty.

Practice 2.1. Practice 1.6 breaks the stick a third time and obtains $\mathbb{E}[X_3]=\ell/8$ and $\operatorname{var}(X_3)=37\ell^2/1728$ by the within/between decomposition. Confirm both without any conditioning at all, by writing $X_3$ as a product of independent uniform fractions.
Solution

Each break keeps a uniformly random fraction of whatever is currently held, and the fractions are independent of each other and of the earlier lengths, so $$X_3=\ell\,U_1U_2U_3,\qquad U_1,U_2,U_3\ \text{i.i.d.}\ U(0,1).$$ Independence lets the expectation factor across the product (G2 §4), and $\mathbb{E}[U]=\tfrac12$, $\mathbb{E}[U^2]=\int_0^1u^2\,du=\tfrac13$, so $$\mathbb{E}[X_3]=\ell\big(\tfrac12\big)^3=\frac{\ell}{8}=0.125\,\ell,\qquad \mathbb{E}[X_3^2]=\ell^2\big(\tfrac13\big)^3=\frac{\ell^2}{27}\approx0.037037\,\ell^2,$$ (stick3_EX_over_ell, stick3_EX2_over_ell2) whence $$\operatorname{var}(X_3)=\frac{\ell^2}{27}-\Big(\frac{\ell}{8}\Big)^2 =\frac{64\ell^2-27\ell^2}{1728}=\frac{37\ell^2}{1728}\approx0.021412\,\ell^2$$ (stick3_var_over_ell2), matching Practice 1.6. ✓ The same trick reproves Example 2.2's $7\ell^2/144$ from $X=\ell U_1U_2$. Worth noticing why the shortcut exists here and not in general: the conditional distribution of each stage is uniform on $[0,\text{previous length}]$, which is exactly "previous length times an independent $U(0,1)$" — a multiplicative structure most conditioning problems do not have, which is why §1's within/between route is the one that generalizes.

2.2 The sum of a random number of random variables

Now the main event of L12's second half.

Definition (random sum; L12 slide 7, B&T §4.5). Let $N$ be a random variable taking nonnegative integer values — the count. Let $X_1,X_2,\ldots$ be identically distributed random variables with common mean $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and common variance $\operatorname{var}(X)$ — the summands. Assume $N,X_1,X_2,\ldots$ are all independent (any finite subcollection is independent). The random sum is $$Y=X_1+X_2+\cdots+X_N,\qquad\text{with the convention }Y=0\text{ when }N=0 .$$ L12's story: $N$ is the number of stores you visit, $X_i$ the money you spend in store $i$, $Y$ the total spending.
Gotcha. $Y$ is not a function of $N$ alone, and not a sum of a fixed number of terms — the upper limit $N$ in "$X_1+\cdots+X_N$" is a random variable, so the very number of things being added changes from outcome to outcome. Watch the capital/lowercase distinction throughout: $N$ (random) in the definition of $Y$, lowercase $n$ inside a conditioning event $\{N=n\}$. Losing track of which is which is the single most common error here (L12 note 2).
Fig. 2.1 — The two-track recipe for a random sum: condition on $N=n$, un-fix $n$, then apply the appropriate law (L12 slides 7–8; B&T §4.5).

The mean, derived in full

Step 1: condition on $\{N=n\}$. Fix a nonnegative integer $n$. Inside the conditional universe $\{N=n\}$ the sum has exactly $n$ terms, so $$\mathbb{E}[Y\mid N=n]=\mathbb{E}[X_1+X_2+\cdots+X_N\mid N=n] =\mathbb{E}[X_1+X_2+\cdots+X_n\mid N=n].$$ The first equality is only a rewriting of $Y$; the second replaces the random upper limit $N$ by $n$, which is legitimate because on the event $\{N=n\}$ the two expressions are literally the same number.

Step 2: drop the conditioning. The random variable $X_1+\cdots+X_n$ is a function of $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ only, and these are independent of $N$; hence $X_1+\cdots+X_n$ is independent of the event $\{N=n\}$, and conditioning on that event does not change its distribution: $$\mathbb{E}[X_1+\cdots+X_n\mid N=n]=\mathbb{E}[X_1+\cdots+X_n].$$ (This is the justification L12 slide 7 leaves implicit; it is the step that fails when $N$ and the $X_i$ are dependent — see the Gotcha below.)

Step 3: linearity. Expectation of a sum is the sum of expectations — no independence needed for this step — and each $X_i$ has the same mean $\mathbb{E}[X]$: $$\mathbb{E}[X_1+\cdots+X_n]=\mathbb{E}[X_1]+\cdots+\mathbb{E}[X_n] =\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[X]+\cdots+\mathbb{E}[X]}_{n\text{ terms}}=n\,\mathbb{E}[X].$$

Step 4: un-fix $n$. Steps 1–3 hold for every nonnegative integer $n$. The random variable $\mathbb{E}[Y\mid N]$ is by definition the one taking the value $\mathbb{E}[Y\mid N=n]$ when $N=n$; since that value is $n\,\mathbb{E}[X]$ for each $n$, $$\boxed{\;\mathbb{E}[Y\mid N]=N\,\mathbb{E}[X].\;}$$ Note what kind of object this is: $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is a fixed number, $N$ is random, so $\mathbb{E}[Y\mid N]$ is a random variable — a constant multiple of $N$.

Step 5: iterate. By the law of iterated expectations and then linearity (pulling the constant $\mathbb{E}[X]$ out of the outer expectation), $$\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid N]\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[N\,\mathbb{E}[X]\big] =\mathbb{E}[N]\,\mathbb{E}[X].$$

The variance, derived in full

Step 1$'$: condition on $\{N=n\}$. Exactly as before, $$\operatorname{var}(Y\mid N=n)=\operatorname{var}(X_1+\cdots+X_n\mid N=n) =\operatorname{var}(X_1+\cdots+X_n),$$ the last equality again because $X_1+\cdots+X_n$ is independent of the event $\{N=n\}$.

Step 2$'$: variance of a sum of independent terms. Here — unlike for the mean — independence is required: for independent random variables the variance of a sum is the sum of the variances (G2 §3), so $$\operatorname{var}(X_1+\cdots+X_n)=\operatorname{var}(X_1)+\cdots+\operatorname{var}(X_n) =n\operatorname{var}(X).$$ (L12 slide 8 states $\operatorname{var}(Y\mid N=n)=n\operatorname{var}(X)$ without this justification.)

Step 3$'$: un-fix $n$, giving the random variable $\operatorname{var}(Y\mid N)=N\operatorname{var}(X)$, and take its expectation: $$\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(Y\mid N)\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[N\operatorname{var}(X)\big] =\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X),$$ pulling out the constant $\operatorname{var}(X)$.

Step 4$'$: the other term. From the mean derivation, $\mathbb{E}[Y\mid N]=N\mathbb{E}[X]$, a constant multiple of $N$, so by $\operatorname{var}(aN)=a^2\operatorname{var}(N)$ with the constant $a=\mathbb{E}[X]$, $$\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid N]\big)=\operatorname{var}\big(N\,\mathbb{E}[X]\big) =(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N).$$

Step 5$'$: add. The law of total variance assembles the pieces: $$\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(Y\mid N)\big] +\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid N]\big) =\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)+(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N).$$

Property (random sums; L12 slides 7–8, B&T §4.5 Properties box, p. 242). For $Y=X_1+\cdots+X_N$ with $N,X_1,X_2,\ldots$ independent and the $X_i$ identically distributed, $$\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[N]\,\mathbb{E}[X],\qquad \operatorname{var}(Y)=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)}_{\text{randomness in the sizes}} +\underbrace{(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N)}_{\text{randomness in the count}} .$$ (B&T also derives the transform $M_Y(s)=M_N(\log M_X(s))$; transforms are outside the scope of L12, which reads "mean and variance only; no transforms".)
Interpretation. The mean formula is the one anybody would guess: average count times average size. The variance formula is the one nobody guesses, and the two terms have clean meanings, inherited from the "within/between" reading of the law of total variance in §1. Even if you knew $N$ exactly, the sum would still wobble because the $X_i$ wobble — that is $\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)$, the average within-group variance. On top of that, changing $N$ by one shifts the whole sum by about $\mathbb{E}[X]$, so fluctuations in $N$ get amplified by $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and squared into the variance — that is $(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N)$, the between-group variance. When the individual items are large, uncertainty about how many there are dominates uncertainty about how big each is.
Gotcha (the term everyone drops). Writing $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)$ — as if $N$ were the constant $\mathbb{E}[N]$ — is the classic error. In rec13 P3 below it would give $160$ instead of the correct $1760$ (crate_wrong_varT versus crate_varT): too small by a factor of $11$, with the missing term ten times larger than the one kept (crate_ratio $=10$).
Gotcha (independence is not decoration). Both formulas need $N$ independent of the $X_i$. Counterexample: let $N$ be $1$ or $2$ with probability $1/2$ each and set every $X_i=N$ — so $\mathbb{E}[X]=1.5$, $\mathbb{E}[N]=1.5$, and the formula would predict $\mathbb{E}[Y]=2.25$. But $Y=N\cdot N=N^2$ takes the values $1$ and $4$ with probability $1/2$ each, so $\mathbb{E}[Y]=2.5\ne2.25$ (gotcha_EY_true, gotcha_EY_formula). Step 2 of the derivation is exactly what breaks. Insurance claims (a bad month brings both more claims and bigger ones) are the standard real-world violation.
Example 2.3 (L12 slide 7, made numeric — the shopping trip). You visit $N$ stores, where $N$ is equally likely to be $1,2,3,4$ or $5$. In each store you spend an amount $X_i$ with mean $\mathbb{E}[X]=\$100$ and variance $\operatorname{var}(X)=400$ (i.e. standard deviation $\$20$), independently of everything else. Find the mean and standard deviation of the total spend $Y$, and say which source of randomness dominates.

Solution. First the count. For $N$ uniform on $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ each value has probability $1/5=0.2$, so $$\mathbb{E}[N]=\tfrac15(1+2+3+4+5)=\tfrac{15}{5}=3,\qquad \mathbb{E}[N^2]=\tfrac15(1+4+9+16+25)=\tfrac{55}{5}=11,$$ $$\operatorname{var}(N)=\mathbb{E}[N^2]-(\mathbb{E}[N])^2=11-9=2$$ (shop_EN, shop_EN2, shop_varN). Then $$\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[N]\mathbb{E}[X]=3\cdot100=\$300,$$ $$\operatorname{var}(Y)=\underbrace{3\cdot400}_{=1200}+\underbrace{100^2\cdot2}_{=20\,000}=21\,200, \qquad \sigma_Y=\sqrt{21\,200}\approx\$145.60$$ (shop_EY, shop_term_within, shop_term_between, shop_varY, shop_sdY). The count term supplies $20\,000/21\,200=94.34\%$ of the variance (shop_between_share): almost all the uncertainty about your bill comes from not knowing how many stores you will enter. A $2\,000\,000$-trip simulation returns $\mathbb{E}[Y]\approx300.156$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y)\approx21\,198.6$ (shop_mc_EY, shop_mc_varY).

Example 2.4 (rec13 P3 — widgets in boxes in a crate). Widgets are stored in boxes, and the boxes are assembled in a crate. Let $X$ be the number of widgets in a particular box and $N$ the number of boxes in a crate. $X$ and $N$ are independent integer-valued random variables, each with expected value $10$ and variance $16$. Evaluate the expected value and variance of the total number of widgets $T$ in a crate.
Solution

Model. Let $X_i$ be the number of widgets in the $i$th box, so that $T=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_i$ is a random sum with count $N$ and summands $X_i$. The problem says $X$ and $N$ are independent; as the derivation needs, we also read the physical description as saying the box contents $X_1,X_2,\ldots$ are i.i.d. copies of $X$ and independent of $N$ — boxes are filled separately and then counted into a crate. [This is the assumption the official solution uses without stating it; see the note at the end.]

The mean, step by step (this is the official chain with every link spelled out): $$\mathbb{E}[T]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_i\;\Big|\;N\Big]\Big] \quad\text{(law of iterated expectations)}$$ $$\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_i\;\Big|\;N=n\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i\Big] =\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_i]\quad\text{(fixed number of terms, then linearity)}$$ $$=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X]=n\,\mathbb{E}[X] \quad\text{(each }X_i\text{ has the distribution of }X\text{, and is independent of }N).$$ [First gap filled: the replacement $\mathbb{E}[X_i\mid N]=\mathbb{E}[X]$ uses both the independence of $X_i$ from $N$ and the fact that all $X_i$ are distributed like $X$.] Un-fixing $n$ gives $\mathbb{E}[T\mid N]=N\,\mathbb{E}[X]$, and $$\mathbb{E}[T]=\mathbb{E}\big[N\,\mathbb{E}[X]\big]=\mathbb{E}[N]\,\mathbb{E}[X] =10\cdot10=100$$ [second gap filled: the constant $\mathbb{E}[X]$ comes out of the outer expectation] (crate_ET). On average a crate holds $100$ widgets.

The variance, step by step. $$\operatorname{var}(T)=\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(T\mid N)\big] +\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[T\mid N]\big)\quad\text{(law of total variance)}.$$ For the first term: given $N=n$, $T$ is the sum of the $n$ independent random variables $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, so its variance is the sum of their variances, $\operatorname{var}(T\mid N=n)=n\operatorname{var}(X)$, i.e. $\operatorname{var}(T\mid N)=N\operatorname{var}(X)$, and $$\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(T\mid N)\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[N\operatorname{var}(X)\big] =\operatorname{var}(X)\,\mathbb{E}[N]=16\cdot10=160 .$$ [Third gap filled: this step needs the $X_i$ independent of each other — the problem statement only says "$X$ and $N$ are independent", so the mutual independence of the box contents is an extra (and physically natural) modeling assumption. Without it the cross-covariance terms $2\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{cov}(X_i,X_j)$ would survive.] For the second term: $\mathbb{E}[T\mid N]=N\mathbb{E}[X]$ is $N$ scaled by the constant $\mathbb{E}[X]=10$, and scaling a random variable by a constant $a$ multiplies its variance by $a^2$, so $$\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[T\mid N]\big)=\operatorname{var}\big(N\,\mathbb{E}[X]\big) =(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N)=10^2\cdot16=100\cdot16=1600 .$$ Adding, $\operatorname{var}(T)=160+1600=1760$ (crate_varT), so the standard deviation is $\sqrt{1760}\approx41.95$ widgets (crate_sdT).

Reading the answer. The count term ($1600$) is ten times the size term ($160$) (crate_ratio). With boxes averaging ten widgets each, one box more or less moves the crate total by about ten — far more than the wobble in a single box's contents. A crate is "$100\pm42$" widgets, and the $\pm42$ is essentially uncertainty about the number of boxes.

Example 2.5 (B&T Example 4.35 — a geometric number of exponential times, mean and variance). Jane looks for a book in one bookstore after another. Each store carries it with probability $p$, independently; she keeps going until she buys it. In each store she spends an exponentially distributed time with parameter $\lambda$, independent of everything else. Find the mean and variance of her total time $Y$ in bookstores, with $p=0.2$ and $\lambda=0.5$ per hour.

Solution. The number $N$ of stores visited is geometric with parameter $p$ (the trial index of the first success), so from G2 §3, $$\mathbb{E}[N]=\frac1p=\frac{1}{0.2}=5,\qquad \operatorname{var}(N)=\frac{1-p}{p^2}=\frac{0.8}{0.04}=20 .$$ The time in a store is exponential with parameter $\lambda$, so from G3 §1, $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac1\lambda=\frac{1}{0.5}=2\ \text{hours},\qquad \operatorname{var}(X)=\frac{1}{\lambda^2}=\frac{1}{0.25}=4 .$$ $N$ counts stores and $X_i$ measures time in store $i$; they are independent, so the random-sum formulas apply: $$\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[N]\mathbb{E}[X]=5\cdot2=10\ \text{hours}=\frac{1}{p\lambda},$$ $$\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)+(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N) =5\cdot4+4\cdot20=20+80=100=\frac{1}{(p\lambda)^2}$$ (bk_EY, bk_varY). The general identity behind the numbers: $$\operatorname{var}(Y)=\frac1p\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda^2}+\frac{1}{\lambda^2}\cdot\frac{1-p}{p^2} =\frac{1}{\lambda^2}\Big(\frac{p}{p^2}+\frac{1-p}{p^2}\Big) =\frac{1}{\lambda^2}\cdot\frac{1}{p^2}=\frac{1}{(p\lambda)^2}.$$ Why that is remarkable. $\mathbb{E}[Y]=1/(p\lambda)$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y)=1/(p\lambda)^2$ are exactly the mean and variance of an exponential random variable with parameter $p\lambda=0.1$ (bk_rate) — and B&T shows with transforms that $Y$ really is exponential with that parameter. A geometric number of exponentials is exponential; a fixed number ($n\ge2$) of exponentials is not (it is Erlang, which §3.4 will build). Randomizing the count restores the memorylessness that adding a fixed number of stages destroys.

Recipe (is this a random sum?).
  1. Identify the count and the summand. Is the quantity of interest a total over a collection whose size is uncertain? Name the size $N$ and the per-item contribution $X_i$.
  2. Check independence. Are the $X_i$ i.i.d.? Is $N$ independent of them? If a big $N$ tends to come with big $X_i$'s, stop — the formulas do not apply and you must work with the joint model directly.
  3. Get four numbers: $\mathbb{E}[N],\operatorname{var}(N),\mathbb{E}[X], \operatorname{var}(X)$, from the named distributions of G2/G3.
  4. Assemble: $\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[N]\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)+(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N)$. Sanity-check the units: in the variance, the first term is (count)$\times$(size$^2$) and the second (size$^2$)$\times$(count$^2$-ish) — both come out in size$^2$.
  5. If you need the full distribution, not just two moments, you must condition and sum: $p_Y(y)=\sum_n p_N(n)\,\mathbf{P}(X_1+\cdots+X_n=y)$, using the convolutions of G3 §4.
Practice 2.2. A website receives $N$ visitors in an hour, where $N$ is Poisson with $\lambda=4$ (so $\mathbb{E}[N]=\operatorname{var}(N)=4$). Each visitor buys, independently, with probability $q=0.25$; let $Y$ be the number of purchases. Find $\mathbb{E}[Y]$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y)$, and comment.
Solution

Mean equals variance — the signature of a Poisson random variable, and indeed $Y$ is Poisson with parameter $\lambda q=1$. Thinning a Poisson count by an independent coin gives a Poisson count; §4 proves this properly when it splits the Poisson process.

Practice 2.4. Show directly from the formulas that if $N$ is not random — say $\mathbf{P}(N=n_0)=1$ — the random-sum results collapse to the familiar fixed-sum ones. Then explain in one sentence what happens if instead the $X_i$ are non-random, $X_i\equiv c$.

2.3 The Bernoulli process: definition and first properties

We now switch subjects. A stochastic process is a probabilistic model of a phenomenon evolving in time: not one random variable but an infinite sequence of them. L13 opens the study of processes with the simplest possible example.

Definition (Bernoulli process; L13 slide 2, B&T §6.1). A Bernoulli process with parameter $p\in(0,1)$ is a sequence $X_1,X_2,X_3,\ldots$ of independent Bernoulli random variables with, for every trial index $i$, $$\mathbf{P}(X_i=1)=\mathbf{P}(\text{success at trial } i)=p,\qquad \mathbf{P}(X_i=0)=\mathbf{P}(\text{failure at trial } i)=1-p .$$ Time is discrete: trial $i$ occupies the $i$th time slot. When the context is arrivals of customers or packets we say arrival instead of "success", and a slot with $X_i=1$ is a slot in which an arrival occurred.

L13 slide 2 lists what such a sequence can model: a run of lottery wins and losses; the daily ups and downs of the Dow Jones; customers arriving (each second) at a bank; jobs arriving (in each time slot) at a server. In the queueing reading, time is chopped into periods and "success at trial $i$" means "at least one customer arrived during period $i$" — the model is a coarse but extremely tractable picture of an arrival stream.

Fig. 2.3 — Three sample paths of a Bernoulli process over $n=30$ slots. Each path is one outcome of the experiment. The first three interarrival times $T_1,T_2,T_3$ of §2.4–§2.5 are marked (L13 slides 2, 5; B&T §6.1, its Figure 6.2).

Two views of a random process (L13 slide 3)

First view: a sequence of random variables. The process is the list $X_1,X_2,\ldots$, and you may ask ordinary questions about individual entries. The slide leaves the answers blank; they are filled in during lecture, and they are the Bernoulli facts of G2 §2–§3. Each $X_t$ takes the value $1$ with probability $p$ and $0$ with probability $1-p$, so $$\mathbb{E}[X_t]=1\cdot p+0\cdot(1-p)=p,$$ $$\mathbb{E}[X_t^2]=1^2\cdot p+0^2\cdot(1-p)=p \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname{var}(X_t)=\mathbb{E}[X_t^2]-(\mathbb{E}[X_t])^2=p-p^2=p(1-p).$$ For $p=0.3$: $\mathbb{E}[X_t]=0.3$ and $\operatorname{var}(X_t)=0.21$ (bp_EXt, bp_varXt). Note $\mathbb{E}[X_t]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X_t)$ do not depend on $t$ — the process is time-homogeneous, the same statistics in every slot.

Second view: what is the right sample space? The honest sample space for the whole process is the set of infinite binary sequences $(x_1,x_2,\ldots)$ — one outcome is an entire realization, one complete path like a row of Fig. 2.3. The slide makes the point with a deliberately extreme question, again left blank: $$\mathbf{P}(X_t=1\text{ for all }t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}(X_1=\cdots=X_n=1) =\lim_{n\to\infty}p^{\,n}=0\quad(p<1),$$ using independence for $\mathbf{P}(X_1=\cdots=X_n=1)=p^n$ and $0<p<1$ for the limit. Concretely with $p=0.3$: five consecutive successes have probability $0.3^5=0.00243$, twenty have probability $0.3^{20}\approx3.487\times10^{-11}$, and the limit is $0$ (bp_all_ones_5, bp_all_ones_20, bp_all_ones_limit).

Gotcha. "Probability zero" is not "impossible". The all-successes sequence is a perfectly legitimate element of the sample space; it simply has zero probability, exactly as every individual point of a continuous sample space did in G3. This is why an infinite sample space needs the machinery of §1's axioms (countable additivity) and why one cannot define the process by assigning probabilities to individual infinite sequences.

L13 slide 3 also names the three processes the course studies: the Bernoulli process (memoryless, discrete time — this section), the Poisson process (memoryless, continuous time — §3–§4), and Markov chains (with dependence across time — §5 and G5). The organizing question for all three is the same: how much does the past tell you about the future?

The number of successes in $n$ slots (L13 slide 4)

Let $S=X_1+X_2+\cdots+X_n$ be the number of successes in the first $n$ slots — a fixed number of i.i.d. Bernoulli terms, so this is the binomial of G2 §1. The three blanks on the slide are:

The PMF. A particular sequence with $k$ successes and $n-k$ failures has probability $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ by independence, and there are $\binom{n}{k}$ such sequences (choose which slots hold the successes), so $$\mathbf{P}(S=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n .$$

The mean, by linearity (no independence needed): $\mathbb{E}[S]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_i]=np$.

The variance, by independence (variance of a sum of independent terms is the sum of variances): $\operatorname{var}(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{var}(X_i)=np(1-p)$.

With $p=0.3$ and $n=20$: $\mathbb{E}[S]=6$, $\operatorname{var}(S)=4.2$, $\sigma_S\approx2.049$, and for instance $\mathbf{P}(S=0)=0.000798$, $\mathbf{P}(S=3)=0.071604$, $\mathbf{P}(S=6)=0.191639$, $\mathbf{P}(S=10)=0.030817$ (bp_ES, bp_varS, bp_sdS, bp_pS_0, bp_pS_3, bp_pS_6, bp_pS_10).

Independence over disjoint sets of trials

Property (disjoint trials; B&T §6.1, p. 298). If $U$ is a function of the trials in a set $A$ of time indices and $V$ is a function of the trials in a set $B$, and $A\cap B=\varnothing$, then $U$ and $V$ are independent. This is the process-level version of the G2 §4 fact that functions of independent random variables are independent.
Example 2.6 (B&T Example 6.1). (a) Let $U$ be the number of successes in trials $1$ through $5$ and $V$ the number in trials $6$ through $10$. Then $U=X_1+\cdots+X_5$ and $V=X_6+\cdots+X_{10}$ are built from the disjoint index sets $\{1,\ldots,5\}$ and $\{6,\ldots,10\}$, so $U$ and $V$ are independent. (b) Let $U$ be the first odd time at which a success occurs and $V$ the first even time at which a success occurs. $U$ is determined by $X_1,X_3,X_5,\ldots$ and $V$ by $X_2,X_4,X_6,\ldots$; the two index sets are disjoint, so $U$ and $V$ are independent. Note this is true even though both are "first success" times of the same process and might feel entangled — what matters is only which trials they look at.
Interpretation. A Bernoulli process carries no structure beyond "independent, identical, one flip per slot" — which is exactly why every question about it reduces to a distribution you already met in G2. Counting in a window gives the binomial; the disjoint-trials property says that windows that do not overlap give independent counts, so a Bernoulli process is just a supply of independent binomials, one per window. Everything harder in this section (§2.4–§2.5) comes from asking timing questions instead of counting questions.

2.4 Interarrival times, memorylessness, and the fresh-start property

Counting successes in a window is one question; timing is the other. Define $T_1$ to be the number of trials up to and including the first success (L13 slide 5).

Property (first arrival is geometric; L13 slide 5, B&T §6.1 summary box, p. 298). $$p_{T_1}(t)=\mathbf{P}(T_1=t)=(1-p)^{t-1}p,\quad t=1,2,\ldots,\qquad \mathbb{E}[T_1]=\frac1p,\qquad \operatorname{var}(T_1)=\frac{1-p}{p^2}.$$

Derivation of the PMF. The event $\{T_1=t\}$ says: trials $1$ through $t-1$ are failures and trial $t$ is a success. These are $t$ statements about $t$ different, independent trials, so their probabilities multiply: $$\mathbf{P}(T_1=t)=\underbrace{(1-p)\cdots(1-p)}_{t-1\text{ failures}}\cdot p=(1-p)^{t-1}p .$$ This is the geometric PMF of G2 §3, where its mean $1/p$ and variance $(1-p)/p^2$ were derived (the mean by the same conditioning trick we are using here, the variance by the total-variance argument). For $p=0.3$: $\mathbb{E}[T_1]=1/0.3\approx3.3333$, $\operatorname{var}(T_1)=0.7/0.09\approx7.7778$, $\sigma_{T_1}\approx2.789$, and $\mathbf{P}(T_1=1)=0.3$, $\mathbf{P}(T_1=2)=0.21$, $\mathbf{P}(T_1=3)=0.147$, $\mathbf{P}(T_1=5)=0.07203$ (geo_ET1, geo_varT1, geo_sdT1, geo_pT1_1–geo_pT1_5).

Property (fresh start and memorylessness; L13 slides 5–6, B&T §6.1 independence-properties box, p. 299).
  1. Fresh start. For any fixed time $n$, the sequence $X_{n+1},X_{n+2},\ldots$ is itself a Bernoulli process with the same parameter $p$, and it is independent of $X_1,\ldots,X_n$.
  2. Memorylessness. If no success has occurred in the first $n$ trials, the number of further trials needed is geometric with parameter $p$ all over again: $$\mathbf{P}(T_1-n=t\mid T_1>n)=(1-p)^{t-1}p=\mathbf{P}(T_1=t),\qquad t=1,2,\ldots$$
  3. Fresh start at a random time. The same conclusion holds if you start watching at a random time $N$, provided $N$ is determined by the past only and carries no information about the future.

Why fresh start holds. The variables $X_{n+1},X_{n+2},\ldots$ are, by the definition of the process, independent Bernoulli($p$) variables — that is literally the definition of a Bernoulli process, so the shifted sequence is one. Independence of the future from the past is the same statement as the independence of the whole family. Nothing needs proving; the content is in realizing how much this buys you.

Memorylessness, derived algebraically. The intuitive argument is the fresh-start property: given $\{T_1>n\}$ the first $n$ trials were all failures, and the process from slot $n+1$ onward is a brand-new Bernoulli process, so the wait from there is again geometric. Here is the same statement by direct computation. First, $$\mathbf{P}(T_1>n)=\mathbf{P}(X_1=\cdots=X_n=0)=(1-p)^n,$$ by independence. Next, for $t\ge1$ the event $\{T_1-n=t\}\cap\{T_1>n\}$ is just $\{T_1=n+t\}$ (if the first success is at slot $n+t$ with $t\ge1$, then automatically $T_1>n$), so $$\mathbf{P}(T_1-n=t\mid T_1>n)=\frac{\mathbf{P}(T_1=n+t)}{\mathbf{P}(T_1>n)} =\frac{(1-p)^{n+t-1}p}{(1-p)^{n}}=(1-p)^{t-1}p=\mathbf{P}(T_1=t),$$ because $(1-p)^{n+t-1}/(1-p)^n=(1-p)^{t-1}$. Numerically with $p=0.3$, $n=5$, $t=3$: $\mathbf{P}(T_1>5)=0.7^5=0.16807$ and $\mathbf{P}(T_1=8\mid T_1>5)=0.7^{7}\cdot0.3/0.16807=0.147=\mathbf{P}(T_1=3)$ (geo_tail_5, geo_cond_check, geo_pT1_3_again).

Fig. 2.4 — Fresh start (top) and memorylessness (bottom): after any number of observed failures, the PMF of the remaining wait is the original geometric PMF, unshifted (L13 slides 5–6; B&T §6.1, p. 299).
Interpretation. Memorylessness says a Bernoulli process has no fatigue and no momentum. A slot machine that has not paid out in $40$ pulls is not "due"; a component that has survived $40$ days is, in this model, as good as new. Formally, the conditional distribution of the remaining wait does not depend on how long you have already waited — the geometric is the only discrete distribution on $\{1,2,\ldots\}$ with this property, just as the exponential is the only continuous one (a fact §3 leans on heavily).
Gotcha (the two shapes of "the wait"). Memorylessness is about the additional wait $T_1-n$ given $T_1>n$, which is geometric with mean $1/p$. It is not the claim that $T_1$ itself is unaffected: $\mathbb{E}[T_1\mid T_1>n]=n+1/p$, which grows with $n$. Nothing was gained by waiting, but nothing was lost either — you have simply used up $n$ slots.
Example 2.7 (L13 slide 5, the unsolved lottery question — solved). "If you buy a lottery ticket every day, what is the distribution of the length of the first string of losing days?"

Setting up. Model the days as a Bernoulli process where "success" means the ticket wins, with $\mathbf{P}(\text{win})=p$ on any given day, independently. The first string of losing days is the run of consecutive losses that begins on day $1$ and ends the day before the first win. Call its length $L$. If day $1$ is already a win, the string is empty and $L=0$.

Solution. Relate $L$ to the first-success time: the first win occurs on day $T_1$, and the losing days before it are days $1,\ldots,T_1-1$, so $$L=T_1-1 .$$ Therefore, for $\ell=0,1,2,\ldots$, $$\mathbf{P}(L=\ell)=\mathbf{P}(T_1=\ell+1)=(1-p)^{(\ell+1)-1}p=(1-p)^{\ell}p .$$ This is the shifted (or "number of failures") geometric distribution, supported on $\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ rather than $\{1,2,\ldots\}$. Its mean and variance follow from those of $T_1$ by the shift rules $\mathbb{E}[T_1-1]=\mathbb{E}[T_1]-1$ and $\operatorname{var}(T_1-1)=\operatorname{var}(T_1)$ (adding a constant moves the mean and leaves the variance alone, G2 §2–§3): $$\mathbb{E}[L]=\frac1p-1=\frac{1-p}{p},\qquad \operatorname{var}(L)=\frac{1-p}{p^2}.$$ With $p=0.3$: $\mathbf{P}(L=0)=0.3$, $\mathbf{P}(L=1)=0.21$, $\mathbf{P}(L=2)=0.147$, $\mathbf{P}(L=5)=0.050421$, $\mathbb{E}[L]=0.7/0.3\approx2.3333$ and $\operatorname{var}(L)\approx7.7778$ (lot_pL_0, lot_pL_1, lot_pL_2, lot_pL_5, lot_EL, lot_varL); a $2\,000\,000$-run simulation gives $\mathbb{E}[L]\approx2.3326$ (lot_mc_EL).

Postscript (all the strings, not just the first). By the fresh-start property the process restarts after the first win, so the second string of losing days has the same distribution and is independent of the first — and so on. The lengths of successive losing strings are i.i.d. shifted geometrics.

Example 2.8 (B&T Example 6.2 — busy and idle periods of a processor). A computer works in discrete slots. A priority task arrives at the beginning of each slot with probability $p$, independently across slots, and occupies the whole slot; when no priority task is present the machine runs a background task. Call a slot busy if a priority task runs in it and idle otherwise. A busy period is a maximal run of busy slots and an idle period a maximal run of idle slots. Find the distributions and means of the busy-period length $B$ and the idle-period length $I$.

Solution. Treat "priority task arrives" as a success, so the slots form a Bernoulli process with parameter $p$. Suppose a busy period has just started, so its first slot is busy. By the fresh-start property the slots after it form an independent Bernoulli process, and the busy period runs until the first failure among them. Let $G$ be the number of those following slots up to and including that first failure; since failures have probability $1-p$ per slot, $G$ is geometric with parameter $1-p$, i.e. $\mathbf{P}(G=k)=p^{\,k-1}(1-p)$. And $B=G$: the busy period consists of its first slot together with the $G-1$ busy slots that follow. Hence $$p_B(k)=p^{\,k-1}(1-p),\quad k=1,2,\ldots,\qquad \mathbb{E}[B]=\frac{1}{1-p},\qquad \operatorname{var}(B)=\frac{p}{(1-p)^2}.$$ By the mirror-image argument (swap the roles of success and failure), an idle period ends at the first success, so $$p_I(k)=(1-p)^{k-1}p,\quad k=1,2,\ldots,\qquad \mathbb{E}[I]=\frac1p,\qquad \operatorname{var}(I)=\frac{1-p}{p^2}.$$ With $p=0.3$: $\mathbb{E}[B]=1/0.7\approx1.4286$ slots, $\operatorname{var}(B)\approx0.6122$, $\mathbb{E}[I]=1/0.3\approx3.3333$ slots, $\operatorname{var}(I)\approx7.7778$ (cpu_EB, cpu_varB, cpu_EI, cpu_varI). Busy periods are short and idle periods long, as they must be when arrivals are rare. The same PMFs apply to the second, third, … busy or idle period, again by fresh start.

Example 2.9 (B&T Example 6.3 — fresh start at a random time). Let $N$ be the first time at which a success immediately follows another success (the first $i$ with $X_{i-1}=X_i=1$). Find $\mathbf{P}(X_{N+1}=X_{N+2}=0)$.

Solution — the intuitive route. Once the pattern "two successes in a row" has completed at time $N$, the process from time $N+1$ on is a fresh Bernoulli process, so the next two trials are failures with probability $(1-p)^2$. With $p=0.3$ that is $0.7^2=0.49$ (fresh_p2).

Solution — the rigorous route, which shows what "$N$ is determined by the past" is doing. $N$ is a random variable, so condition on its value and use the total probability theorem. Note first the range of the sum: a consecutive pair needs two trials, so $N\ge2$ with probability $1$ and $\mathbf{P}(N=1)=0$ — the decomposition therefore runs over $n=2,3,\ldots$: $$\mathbf{P}(X_{N+1}=X_{N+2}=0)=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}(N=n)\, \mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=X_{n+2}=0\mid N=n).$$ The key observation: by the way $N$ was defined, the event $\{N=n\}$ is determined entirely by $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ (it says $X_{n-1}=X_n=1$ and no earlier consecutive pair occurred). Those variables are independent of $X_{n+1}$ and $X_{n+2}$, so conditioning on $\{N=n\}$ changes nothing: $$\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=X_{n+2}=0\mid N=n)=\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=X_{n+2}=0)=(1-p)^2 ,\qquad n\ge2 .$$ Substituting the constant $(1-p)^2$ back into the sum and using $\sum_{n\ge2}\mathbf{P}(N=n)=1$, $$\mathbf{P}(X_{N+1}=X_{N+2}=0)=(1-p)^2\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}(N=n)=(1-p)^2=0.49 .$$ (That $\sum_{n\ge2}\mathbf{P}(N=n)=1$ — i.e. that $N$ is finite with probability $1$ — is itself worth a word: the pairs of trials $(1,2),(3,4),(5,6),\ldots$ are independent, each is a double success with probability $p^2>0$, so with probability $1$ one of them eventually is, and $N$ is at most the index of that pair.)

Gotcha (random times that peek ahead). Fresh start at a random time needs $N$ to depend only on the past. Let $N$ be "the trial just before the first success". Then by construction $X_{N+1}=1$ with probability $1$, not $p$ — the "future" after $N$ is not a fresh Bernoulli process at all, because $N$ was defined using information about it. In Example 2.9 the definition of $N$ used only $X_1,\ldots,X_N$; that is exactly the licence needed.

2.5 The time of the $k$th success: the Pascal PMF

Having timed the first arrival, time all of them. Following L13 slide 6, let $T_1$ be the number of trials up to and including the first success, $T_2$ the number of additional trials from there up to and including the second success, $T_3$ the additional trials from there to the third, and so on; $T_k$ is called the $k$th interarrival time. Let $$Y_k=T_1+T_2+\cdots+T_k$$ be the trial index of the $k$th success — the $k$th arrival time. (The interarrival times $T_1,T_2,T_3$ are marked on Fig. 2.3; $Y_3$ is where the third stem stands.)

Property (i.i.d. geometric interarrivals; L13 slide 6, B&T §6.1, p. 302). $T_1,T_2,T_3,\ldots$ are independent, each geometric with parameter $p$.

Why. The slide gives the argument for $T_2$ and says "continuing similarly". Given that the first arrival occurred at time $t$, i.e. $\{T_1=t\}$, the fresh-start property says that $X_{t+1},X_{t+2},\ldots$ is a new Bernoulli process independent of $X_1,\ldots,X_t$. Now $T_2$ is the index of the first success in that process, so (i) $T_2$ is geometric with parameter $p$, exactly as $T_1$ was, and (ii) $T_2$ is a function of $X_{t+1},X_{t+2},\ldots$ only, while $T_1$ is a function of $X_1,\ldots,X_t$, so the two are independent. Since the conditional distribution of $T_2$ given $T_1=t$ is the same geometric for every $t$, $T_2$ is unconditionally geometric and independent of $T_1$. Repeating the argument at the second arrival gives $T_3$, and so on inductively.

Property (alternative description of the Bernoulli process; B&T §6.1, p. 302). Instead of specifying the trials $X_1,X_2,\ldots$, one may specify the process by its interarrival times: start with a sequence $T_1,T_2,\ldots$ of independent geometric($p$) random variables and declare arrivals at times $T_1$, $T_1+T_2$, $T_1+T_2+T_3$, …. The two descriptions define the same process.
Interpretation. Two dual pictures of the same object: the slot-by-slot picture (flip a coin every slot) and the gap-by-gap picture (draw i.i.d. geometric waits and place arrivals at the running totals). Whichever makes a given question easy is the one to use — and being able to switch is the whole point of Example 2.10 below. The same duality reappears in §3, where a Poisson process can be described either by counts in intervals or by i.i.d. exponential interarrival times.
Example 2.10 (B&T Example 6.4 — rain). After a rainy day, the number of days until it next rains is geometric with parameter $p$, independent of the past. Find the probability that it rains on both the $5$th and the $8$th day of the month.

Solution. Attacking this by manipulating geometric PMFs is painful: you would have to sum over all the ways rain could fall before day $5$, and between days $5$ and $8$. Instead recognize the hypothesis as exactly the alternative description above — i.i.d. geometric gaps between "arrivals" (rainy days) — which means the rainy days form a Bernoulli process with parameter $p$. In the slot-by-slot picture the answer is immediate: each day is rainy with probability $p$, independently of other days, so $$\mathbf{P}(\text{day 5 rainy and day 8 rainy})=\mathbf{P}(X_5=1)\mathbf{P}(X_8=1)=p^2 .$$ With $p=0.3$ this is $0.09$ (rain_p2). The moral is that translating between the two descriptions can turn a messy calculation into a one-liner.

The Pascal PMF, derived

Property (the $k$th arrival time; L13 slide 6, B&T §6.1 properties box, p. 303). For $k=1,2,\ldots$, $$\mathbb{E}[Y_k]=\frac kp,\qquad \operatorname{var}(Y_k)=\frac{k(1-p)}{p^2},$$ $$p_{Y_k}(t)=\mathbf{P}(Y_k=t)=\binom{t-1}{k-1}p^k(1-p)^{t-k},\qquad t=k,k+1,k+2,\ldots$$ The last is the Pascal PMF of order $k$ (also called the negative binomial).

Mean and variance. $Y_k=T_1+\cdots+T_k$ is a sum of a fixed number $k$ of independent geometric($p$) random variables, so by linearity and by additivity of variance under independence, $$\mathbb{E}[Y_k]=\mathbb{E}[T_1]+\cdots+\mathbb{E}[T_k]=k\cdot\frac1p=\frac kp,$$ $$\operatorname{var}(Y_k)=\operatorname{var}(T_1)+\cdots+\operatorname{var}(T_k) =k\cdot\frac{1-p}{p^2}=\frac{k(1-p)}{p^2}.$$ For $p=0.3$, $k=3$: $\mathbb{E}[Y_3]=3/0.3=10$, $\operatorname{var}(Y_3)=3\cdot0.7/0.09 \approx23.3333$, $\sigma\approx4.83$ (pas_EY, pas_varY, pas_sdY).

The PMF, by a combinatorial argument (the slide leaves it blank; this is B&T's derivation, p. 303–304, with each step justified). First, $Y_k\ge k$ always — you cannot collect $k$ successes in fewer than $k$ trials — so $p_{Y_k}(t)=0$ for $t<k$. Now fix $t\ge k$ and observe that the event $\{Y_k=t\}$ — "the $k$th success comes at trial $t$" — occurs if and only if both of these happen:

  • Event $A$: trial $t$ is a success. (If trial $t$ were a failure, no success at all lands on trial $t$, so the $k$th one certainly does not.)
  • Event $B$: exactly $k-1$ successes occur in the first $t-1$ trials. (Fewer, and the $k$th success is still ahead after trial $t$; more, and it already happened before $t$.)

Their probabilities are, respectively, $\mathbf{P}(A)=p$ (one trial, one success), and — by the binomial PMF applied to the $t-1$ trials before $t$ — $$\mathbf{P}(B)=\binom{t-1}{k-1}p^{k-1}(1-p)^{(t-1)-(k-1)}=\binom{t-1}{k-1}p^{k-1}(1-p)^{t-k}.$$ $A$ concerns trial $t$ alone and $B$ concerns trials $1,\ldots,t-1$; the index sets are disjoint, so by the disjoint-trials property $A$ and $B$ are independent and their probabilities multiply: $$p_{Y_k}(t)=\mathbf{P}(A\cap B)=\mathbf{P}(A)\mathbf{P}(B) =p\cdot\binom{t-1}{k-1}p^{k-1}(1-p)^{t-k}=\binom{t-1}{k-1}p^{k}(1-p)^{t-k}.$$

Numerical check of the derivation. With $p=0.3$, $k=3$, $t=8$: $\mathbf{P}(A)=0.3$ and $\mathbf{P}(B)=\binom72(0.3)^2(0.7)^5=21\cdot0.09\cdot0.16807=0.317652$, whose product is $0.095296$ — matching the direct evaluation $\binom72(0.3)^3(0.7)^5=0.095296$ (pas_pA, pas_pB, pas_product, pas_p_8). Other values: $\mathbf{P}(Y_3=3)=0.027$, $\mathbf{P}(Y_3=5)=0.07938$, $\mathbf{P}(Y_3=10)=0.080048$; the PMF sums to $1.0$ over $t=3,4,\ldots$ (pas_norm_check), and its most likely value is $t=7$ (pas_mode_t) — note the mode $7$ sits below the mean $10$, because the PMF is right-skewed.

Fig. 2.5 — The Pascal family. Left: order $k$ varying at $p=0.3$ ($k=1$ is the geometric). Right: $k=3$ with $p$ varying. Dotted lines mark the means $k/p$ (L13 slide 6; B&T §6.1, p. 303).
Gotcha (Pascal vs binomial). These two look alike and answer different questions. The binomial fixes the number of trials $n$ and makes the number of successes random: $\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$, with $\binom nk$ out front. The Pascal fixes the number of successes $k$ and makes the number of trials random: $\binom{t-1}{k-1}p^k(1-p)^{t-k}$, with $\binom{t-1}{k-1}$ out front — the last trial is pinned to be a success, so only the earlier $t-1$ slots are free to be arranged. Concretely with $p=0.2$: $\mathbf{P}(S_6=2)=0.24576$ (two successes somewhere in six trials) versus $\mathbf{P}(Y_2=6)=0.08192$ (the second success exactly at trial six) — the latter is smaller because it also demands that trial $6$ be one of the two (prac_bin_S2_6, prac_pas_val).
Example 2.11 (B&T Example 6.5 — Alicia fouls out). In each minute of play Alicia commits a single foul with probability $p$ and no foul with probability $1-p$, independently across minutes. She fouls out on her sixth foul, and otherwise plays the full $30$ minutes. Find the PMF of her playing time $Z$, and evaluate it for $p=0.05$.

Solution. Model fouls as a Bernoulli process with parameter $p$; then the minute of her sixth foul is $Y_6$, with Pascal PMF of order $6$, $$p_{Y_6}(t)=\binom{t-1}{5}p^6(1-p)^{t-6},\qquad t=6,7,\ldots$$ Her playing time is $Z=\min\{Y_6,30\}$: she leaves at minute $Y_6$ if that is before the end, and otherwise plays $30$ minutes. Hence for $z$ in the range $6,7,\ldots,29$ the events $\{Z=z\}$ and $\{Y_6=z\}$ are the same event, so $$p_Z(z)=\binom{z-1}{5}p^6(1-p)^{z-6},\qquad z=6,7,\ldots,29,$$ and the remaining probability piles up at $30$ (the value $Z$ takes whenever $Y_6\ge30$): $$p_Z(30)=1-\sum_{z=6}^{29}p_Z(z).$$ Numbers for $p=0.05$. $\mathbb{E}[Y_6]=6/0.05=120$ minutes — far beyond a $30$-minute game, so fouling out should be rare, and it is. The smallest and largest of the $24$ Pascal terms are $p_Z(6)=(0.05)^6\approx1.56\times10^{-8}$ and $p_Z(29)=\binom{28}{5}(0.05)^6(0.95)^{23}\approx0.00047198$; their total is $\sum_{z=6}^{29}p_Z(z)\approx0.002741$, so $$p_Z(30)=1-0.002741=0.997259$$ (alicia_EY6, alicia_pZ_6, alicia_pZ_29, alicia_sum_6_29, alicia_pZ_30). She plays the full game about $99.7\%$ of the time, and her expected playing time is $\mathbb{E}[Z]\approx29.9866$ minutes (alicia_EZ).

Gotcha embedded here. $Z$ is a mixed-looking discrete random variable: an ordinary Pascal tail on $6,\ldots,29$ plus an atom of mass $0.997259$ at $30$. Truncation always works this way — you cannot just renormalize the Pascal PMF over $6..30$; the leftover mass belongs at the truncation point.

Practice 2.7. A quality inspector tests items one at a time; each is defective with probability $p=0.2$, independently. (a) What is the probability that the second defective item is the sixth item tested? (b) What are the mean and variance of the trial index of the second defective? (c) Compare (a) with the probability that exactly two of the first six items are defective, and explain the difference.
Solution

(a) This is $\mathbf{P}(Y_2=6)$ with $k=2$, $t=6$: $$\binom{6-1}{2-1}(0.2)^2(0.8)^{6-2}=\binom51(0.04)(0.4096)=5\cdot0.04\cdot0.4096=0.08192$$ (prac_pas_val).

(b) $\mathbb{E}[Y_2]=k/p=2/0.2=10$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y_2)=k(1-p)/p^2 =2\cdot0.8/0.04=40$, so $\sigma\approx6.32$ (prac_pas_EY2, prac_pas_varY2).

(c) $\mathbf{P}(S_6=2)=\binom62(0.2)^2(0.8)^4=15\cdot0.04\cdot0.4096=0.24576$ (prac_bin_S2_6), three times larger. The binomial event allows the two defectives anywhere among the six positions ($\binom62=15$ arrangements); the Pascal event insists that one of them be the sixth item, leaving only $\binom51=5$ arrangements for the other. Indeed $0.08192/0.24576=5/15=1/3\approx0.333333$ (prac27_ratio).

Practice 2.8. Show that the Pascal PMF of order $1$ is the geometric PMF, and that $\mathbf{P}(Y_k>t)=\mathbf{P}(S_t\le k-1)$ where $S_t$ is the number of successes in the first $t$ trials. Interpret the second identity.
Solution

For $k=1$: $\binom{t-1}{0}p^1(1-p)^{t-1}=(1-p)^{t-1}p$, since $\binom{m}{0}=1$ — the geometric PMF, as it must be, because $Y_1=T_1$.

For the identity, read both sides as statements about the same experiment. "The $k$th success has not happened by the end of trial $t$" ($Y_k>t$) says precisely that fewer than $k$ successes occurred in trials $1,\ldots,t$, i.e. $S_t\le k-1$. The two events are literally equal, so their probabilities are. This is the discrete ancestor of the Poisson-process duality of §3, where $\mathbf{P}(Y_k>t)=\mathbf{P}(N_t\le k-1)$ converts an Erlang tail into a Poisson sum — the same sentence with continuous time.

2.6 Merging and splitting Bernoulli processes

L13 closes with two operations that build new Bernoulli processes from old ones. Both are easy here, and both have exact analogues for the Poisson process in §4 — where they become genuinely powerful.

Fig. 2.6 — Splitting (left) and merging (right), redrawn from L13 slides 7–8 with the same arrival pattern (slots 2, 4, 7, 8, 12) on the central axis. In splitting the arrows point away from the original; in merging they point into the output (B&T §6.1, its Figures 6.3 and 6.4).
Property (splitting; L13 slide 7, B&T §6.1, p. 304). Take a Bernoulli process with parameter $p$. Whenever an arrival occurs, flip an independent coin: with probability $q$ send the arrival to stream 1, with probability $1-q$ to stream 2. Then stream 1 is a Bernoulli process with parameter $pq$ and stream 2 is a Bernoulli process with parameter $p(1-q)$.

Why. Fix a slot. A stream-1 arrival occurs in that slot exactly when two independent things happen: an arrival occurs ($p$) and the routing coin says "stream 1" ($q$). So the per-slot probability is $pq$. Different slots involve different, independent trials and different, independent coin flips, so the indicator variables of stream-1 arrivals are independent across slots — which is the definition of a Bernoulli process. Same argument for stream 2 with $1-q$. With $p=0.3$, $q=0.6$: streams of parameter $0.18$ and $0.12$, and $0.18+0.12=0.3=p$ (split_p1, split_p2, split_sum).

Property (merging; L13 slide 8, B&T §6.1, p. 305). Take two independent Bernoulli processes with parameters $p$ and $q$. Declare an arrival in the merged process in every slot in which at least one of the two has an arrival (collisions count as one arrival). The merged process is Bernoulli with parameter $$p+q-pq=1-(1-p)(1-q).$$

Why. In a fixed slot, "no merged arrival" means neither process had one; by independence that has probability $(1-p)(1-q)$, so a merged arrival has probability $1-(1-p)(1-q)=1-(1-p-q+pq)=p+q-pq$. Slots are again independent because they involve disjoint, independent trials of both processes. With $p=0.3$, $q=0.2$: $\mathbf{P}(\text{no arrival}) =0.7\cdot0.8=0.56$ and the merged parameter is $0.3+0.2-0.06=0.44$ (merge_none, merge_p).

Gotcha (the $-pq$ is not optional). The merged parameter is $p+q-pq$, not $p+q$: the two processes can arrive in the same slot, and that slot still contributes only one merged arrival. With $p=q=0.6$, "$p+q$" would give $1.2$, which is not a probability. The collision probability is $pq=0.06$ in the example above, and given that the merged process has an arrival, the chance it was a collision is $pq/(p+q-pq)=0.06/0.44\approx0.136364$ (merge_collision, merge_pcoll_given_arr). This overlap term is exactly what disappears in continuous time: §4's Poisson merging gives the clean $\lambda_1+ \lambda_2$, because two arrivals at the identical instant have probability zero.
Interpretation. Merging and splitting say the Bernoulli family is closed under the two operations you actually perform on arrival streams: pooling several sources into one queue, and routing one stream to several servers. Because the output is again Bernoulli, you can re-apply every formula in this section to it — count with the binomial, wait with the geometric, time the $k$th arrival with the Pascal — instead of building a new model. Note also that splitting and merging are inverses in the picture (Fig. 2.6 uses the same central pattern for both), but not as operations: splitting and then re-merging the two sub-streams returns the original process, while merging and then splitting does not generally recover the two inputs, because the merged stream has forgotten which source each arrival came from.
Practice 2.9. Two independent sensors report events, each as a Bernoulli process, with parameters $p=0.4$ and $q=0.25$ per slot. (a) What is the parameter of the merged alarm stream? (b) The merged stream is then filtered: each merged alarm is forwarded with probability $0.7$, independently. What is the parameter of the forwarded stream, and what is the expected number of forwarded alarms in $100$ slots?
Solution

(a) $p+q-pq=0.4+0.25-0.4\cdot0.25=0.65-0.10=0.55$ (prac_merge).

(b) Splitting the merged Bernoulli($0.55$) process with $q_{\text{keep}}=0.7$ gives a Bernoulli process with parameter $0.55\cdot0.7=0.385$. Over $n=100$ slots the count is binomial, so the expected number is $np=100\cdot0.385=38.5$ alarms, with variance $np(1-p)=100\cdot0.385\cdot0.615=23.6775$ (prac29_forward, prac29_E100, prac29_var100). For reference, splitting a Bernoulli($0.4$) stream with $0.7$ alone would give $0.28$ (prac_split_keep.)

Practice 2.10. A Bernoulli process with parameter $p$ is split by an independent fair coin into streams 1 and 2. Let $M$ be the number of stream-1 arrivals in $n$ slots and $M'$ the number of stream-2 arrivals. Are $M$ and $M'$ independent? (Careful — the general "disjoint trials" property does not apply directly here.)
Solution

They are not independent in general, because both are driven by the same original trials: $M+M'=S$, the total number of arrivals, and $S\le n$ because a slot holds at most one arrival. That cap couples the two counts — a large $M$ leaves fewer slots in which $M'$ could grow, so they are negatively correlated. Concretely, for $n=1$: $\mathbf{P}(M=1,M'=1)=0$ while $\mathbf{P}(M=1)\mathbf{P}(M'=1)=(p/2)^2>0$ — for $p=0.3$, $0$ versus $0.0225$ (prac210_pM1M1, prac210_product) — so independence fails. Each of $M$ and $M'$ is individually binomial($n,p/2$), by the splitting property plus the binomial count formula. (Contrast §4: for a split Poisson process the two counts are independent — a genuinely surprising fact that has no Bernoulli analogue.)
