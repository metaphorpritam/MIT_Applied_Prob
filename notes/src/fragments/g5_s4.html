Section 3 answered the long-run question: if a chain has a single aperiodic recurrent class, where is it after a very long time? This section answers the two short-run questions that the long-run answer throws away. Suppose the chain starts in a transient state and there is more than one place it can get stuck. Which trap does it fall into, and how long does the fall take? Steady-state analysis cannot tell you: the steady-state probability of every transient state is $0$ no matter how long the chain lingers there, and if there are two absorbing states the limiting distribution depends on where you started, which is exactly the information the balance equations discard.

Both questions are answered by the same idea, and the idea is one you already own. In G4 §1 you computed $\mathbb{E}[X]$ by conditioning on a first coin flip; in G2 §3 you got the geometric mean $1/p$ from the recursion $\mathbb{E}[X]=1+(1-p)\mathbb{E}[X]$. Do the same here — condition on the first transition — and the Markov property guarantees that what follows the first transition is a fresh copy of the same problem started at the new state. One unknown per state, one equation per state, and a linear system to solve. The technique is called first-step analysis, and it is the whole of L18 slides 5–7 and the whole of rec19.

Interpretation. Every formula in this section has the shape "$(\text{answer at }i)=\sum_j p_{ij}\,(\text{answer at }j)\;+\;(\text{cost of the step})$". For a probability the step costs nothing, so $a_i=\sum_j p_{ij}a_j$. For an expected time the step costs one time unit, so $\mu_i=1+\sum_j p_{ij}\mu_j$. If you remember the shape, you can rebuild every equation on this page from scratch, including ones the course never writes down (expected total reward, expected number of visits to a marked state, expected number of ice cream cones — rec19(d)).

4.1 Absorbing states and absorption probabilities

Definition (absorbing state; B&T §7.4). A state $k$ is absorbing if $$p_{kk}=1,\qquad p_{kj}=0\ \text{ for all } j\ne k .$$ Once the chain enters $k$ it never leaves, so $\{k\}$ is a recurrent class all by itself. Here $p_{kj}$ is the one-step transition probability $\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=k)$ of §1.

Why we may always assume the recurrent states are absorbing. The questions of this section are "which recurrent class is entered, and when". The instant the chain enters a recurrent class it can never leave it (§2), so everything that happens afterwards is irrelevant to both questions. We may therefore doctor the chain: take each recurrent class, merge its states into a single new state, and make that state absorbing. This changes nothing about which class is entered or how long that takes, and it leaves us with a chain in which every state is either transient or absorbing (B&T §7.4 does exactly this in its Figure 7.17, where states 4 and 5 are lumped into a single absorbing state 6). All of §4 is written for such a chain.

Definition (absorption probability; B&T §7.4). Fix one absorbing state $s$. For every state $i$ define $$a_i=\mathbf{P}\big(X_n \text{ eventually equals } s \;\big|\; X_0=i\big),$$ the probability that the chain is ultimately absorbed at $s$ given that it starts at $i$. The subscript $i$ is the starting state; $s$ is fixed once and for all before you write a single equation.
Fig. 4.1 — First-step analysis, the picture behind every equation in this section. Condition on the first transition; by the Markov property the rest of the trip is the same problem started at the new state. The only difference between the two equations is whether the step itself is charged (L18 slides 5–6, B&T §7.4).
Theorem (absorption probability equations; L18 slide 5, B&T §7.4). In a chain in which every state is transient or absorbing, and with $s$ a fixed absorbing state, the numbers $a_1,\ldots,a_m$ are the unique solution of $$a_s=1,\qquad a_i=0 \ \text{ for every absorbing } i\ne s,\qquad a_i=\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{ij}\,a_j\ \text{ for every transient } i .$$

Derivation, every step. The two boundary conditions are immediate from the definition: if $X_0=s$ the chain is already absorbed at $s$, so the event has probability $1$; if $X_0=i$ is some other absorbing state, the chain never moves, so it never reaches $s$ and the probability is $0$. For a transient $i$, let $A$ be the event "$X_n$ eventually equals $s$". Condition on the first transition: $$a_i=\mathbf{P}(A\mid X_0=i) =\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{P}(A\mid X_0=i,\,X_1=j)\,\mathbf{P}(X_1=j\mid X_0=i),$$ which is the total probability theorem (G1 §2) applied to the partition of the sample space by the value of $X_1$, everything conditioned on $X_0=i$. The second factor is by definition $p_{ij}$. For the first factor, note that $A$ is an event about $X_1,X_2,\ldots$; by the Markov property, once we know $X_1=j$, the extra conditioning on $X_0=i$ tells us nothing more about the future, so $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid X_0=i,\,X_1=j)=\mathbf{P}(A\mid X_1=j)=a_j ,$$ the last equality because the chain is time-homogeneous — the probability of eventually hitting $s$ starting from $j$ at time $1$ is the same as starting from $j$ at time $0$. Substituting both factors, $$a_i=\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{ij}\,a_j .\qquad\blacksquare$$

Why the solution is unique (the gap L18 slide 5 leaves open). The slide asserts "unique solution" and stops. Here is the argument (B&T Problem 7.33). Order the states so that the transient ones come first, and let $Q$ be the square block of $p_{ij}$ with both $i$ and $j$ transient, $b$ the column vector of $\sum_{j\text{ absorbing}}p_{ij}a_j$ (a known number for each transient $i$, since $a_j$ is $1$ at $s$ and $0$ elsewhere). The transient equations read $x=Qx+b$, i.e. $(I-Q)x=b$. Now $(Q^n)_{ik}=\mathbf{P}(X_n=k,\ X_1,\ldots,X_{n-1}\text{ all transient} \mid X_0=i)$, so the row sums of $Q^n$ equal $\mathbf{P}(\text{not yet absorbed by time } n\mid X_0=i)$, which tends to $0$ because absorption happens with probability $1$ from any transient state (§2). Hence $Q^n\to0$, the series $\sum_{n\ge0}Q^n$ converges, and $(I-Q)\sum_{n\ge0}Q^n=I$; so $I-Q$ is invertible and $x=(I-Q)^{-1}b$ is the one and only solution. The identical argument covers the $\mu_i$ and $t_i$ systems below.
Gotcha. $a_i$ is defined relative to one particular target $s$. Change the target and every number changes. If a chain has absorbing states $4$ and $5$, the vector $(a_1,a_2,a_3)$ for target $4$ and the vector for target $5$ are different solutions of different systems — though of course they must add up to $1$ componentwise, because absorption somewhere is certain. Use that as a free check on your arithmetic.

The transient rows are $$p_{14}=0.2,\quad p_{12}=0.5,\quad p_{13}=0.3;\qquad p_{21}=0.4,\quad p_{23}=0.6;\qquad p_{32}=0.8,\quad p_{35}=0.2 ,$$ each summing to $1$ as it must. Step 1 — boundary conditions. The target is $s=4$, so $a_4=1$; the other absorbing state gives $a_5=0$. These are the two blanks the slide leaves for lecture.

Step 2 — one equation per transient state. Apply $a_i=\sum_j p_{ij}a_j$ to $i=1,2,3$, substituting the known boundary values as we go: $$a_1=0.2\,a_4+0.5\,a_2+0.3\,a_3=0.2+0.5a_2+0.3a_3,$$ $$a_2=0.4\,a_1+0.6\,a_3,$$ $$a_3=0.8\,a_2+0.2\,a_5=0.8a_2 .$$

Step 3 — eliminate. The third equation already gives $a_3$ in terms of $a_2$. Put it into the second: $$a_2=0.4a_1+0.6(0.8a_2)=0.4a_1+0.48a_2 \;\Longrightarrow\; (1-0.48)a_2=0.4a_1 \;\Longrightarrow\; 0.52\,a_2=0.4\,a_1 ,$$ so $a_2=\frac{0.4}{0.52}a_1=\frac{10}{13}a_1$ and therefore $a_3=0.8\cdot\frac{10}{13}a_1=\frac{8}{13}a_1$.

Step 4 — close the loop on $a_1$. Substitute both into the first equation: $$a_1=0.2+0.5\cdot\tfrac{10}{13}a_1+0.3\cdot\tfrac{8}{13}a_1 =0.2+\frac{5+2.4}{13}a_1=0.2+\frac{7.4}{13}a_1 .$$ Move the $a_1$ term across: $\left(1-\frac{7.4}{13}\right)a_1=0.2$, i.e. $\frac{5.6}{13}a_1=0.2$, hence $$a_1=\frac{0.2\cdot13}{5.6}=\frac{2.6}{5.6}=\frac{13}{28}=0.464286 .$$

Step 5 — back-substitute. $$a_2=\frac{10}{13}\cdot\frac{13}{28}=\frac{10}{28}=\frac{5}{14}=0.357143,\qquad a_3=\frac{8}{13}\cdot\frac{13}{28}=\frac{8}{28}=\frac{2}{7}=0.285714 .$$ (Keys L18_a1, L18_a2, L18_a3; a $400{,}000$-run simulation started at state $1$ lands in state $4$ a fraction $0.463990$ of the time, L18_a1_mc.)

Sanity checks. All three are between $0$ and $1$, as probabilities must be. They are ordered $a_1>a_2>a_3$, which is right: state $1$ is the only state with a direct arc into $4$, and state $3$ is the only state with a direct arc into the rival absorbing state $5$. And the absorption probabilities for the target $5$ are the complements $15/28$, $9/14$, $5/7$.

Interpretation. Notice what the answer is not. It is not "the fraction of arcs pointing toward $4$", and it is not determined by the one-step probabilities of state $i$ alone: $a_2=5/14$ even though state $2$ has no arc to either absorbing state. The system of equations is doing the work of summing over infinitely many paths, each weighted by its probability. Solving three linear equations replaces an infinite sum — that is the entire payoff of first-step analysis.

4.2 Expected time to absorption

Definition (expected time to absorption; L18 slide 6, B&T §7.4). $$\mu_i=\mathbb{E}\big[\text{number of transitions until absorption}\mid X_0=i\big] =\mathbb{E}\big[\min\{n\ge0:X_n\text{ is recurrent}\}\mid X_0=i\big].$$ Note $\mu_i=0$ whenever $i$ is itself absorbing (the minimum is achieved at $n=0$), and $\mu_i$ does not care which absorbing state is reached — only when.
Theorem (expected-time equations; L18 slide 6). The numbers $\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_m$ are the unique solution of $$\mu_i=0\ \text{ for every absorbing } i,\qquad \mu_i=1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{ij}\,\mu_j\ \text{ for every transient } i .$$

Derivation, every step (L18 slide 6 states the recursion but derives nothing). Let $T$ be the number of transitions until absorption. Starting from a transient $i$ the chain certainly makes at least one transition, so write $T=1+T'$, where $T'$ is the number of further transitions needed after the first one. Apply the total expectation theorem (G4 §1), conditioning on $X_1$: $$\mu_i=\mathbb{E}[T\mid X_0=i]=1+\mathbb{E}[T'\mid X_0=i] =1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{P}(X_1=j\mid X_0=i)\;\mathbb{E}[T'\mid X_0=i,\,X_1=j].$$ The first equality splits off the deterministic $1$ using linearity of expectation. In the last term, $T'$ counts transitions of the chain from time $1$ onward; by the Markov property and time homogeneity, given $X_1=j$ that count has exactly the distribution of $T$ for a chain started at $j$, and the extra conditioning on $X_0=i$ is irrelevant. Hence $\mathbb{E}[T'\mid X_0=i,X_1=j]=\mu_j$, and with $\mathbf{P}(X_1=j\mid X_0=i)=p_{ij}$, $$\mu_i=1+\sum_{j=1}^m p_{ij}\,\mu_j .\qquad\blacksquare$$ Uniqueness follows from the same $Q^n\to0$ argument as in §4.1: the system is $(I-Q)\mu=\mathbf{1}$.

Gotcha — the two most common slips. (i) Forgetting the leading $1$. Without it the system reads $\mu=Q\mu$, whose unique solution is $\mu=0$: the chain would be absorbed instantly. The $1$ is the cost of the step you just took. (ii) Writing $\mu_i=1+\sum_j p_{ij}\mu_j$ for an absorbing $i$ too. That is wrong — an absorbing $i$ has $\mu_i=0$, not $1$. Equivalently: the sum on the right runs over all $j$, but the terms with $j$ absorbing contribute $p_{ij}\cdot0=0$, so in practice you only ever carry the transient terms.

Step 1 — boundary condition. $\mu_5=0$ (the blank on the slide).

Step 2 — one equation per transient state. $$\mu_1=1+0.5\mu_2+0.5\mu_3,$$ $$\mu_2=1+0.4\mu_1+0.6\mu_3,$$ $$\mu_3=1+0.8\mu_2+0.2\mu_5=1+0.8\mu_2 .$$

Step 3 — eliminate $\mu_3$. Substitute $\mu_3=1+0.8\mu_2$ into the second equation: $$\mu_2=1+0.4\mu_1+0.6(1+0.8\mu_2)=1.6+0.4\mu_1+0.48\mu_2 \;\Longrightarrow\;0.52\,\mu_2=1.6+0.4\,\mu_1 .$$

Step 4 — eliminate $\mu_2$ and $\mu_3$ from the first equation. $$\mu_1=1+0.5\mu_2+0.5(1+0.8\mu_2)=1.5+0.5\mu_2+0.4\mu_2=1.5+0.9\,\mu_2 .$$ Now use $\mu_2=(1.6+0.4\mu_1)/0.52$: $$\mu_1=1.5+0.9\cdot\frac{1.6+0.4\mu_1}{0.52}.$$ Multiply both sides by $0.52$: $$0.52\mu_1=0.78+0.9(1.6+0.4\mu_1)=0.78+1.44+0.36\mu_1 ,$$ so $(0.52-0.36)\mu_1=2.22$, i.e. $0.16\,\mu_1=2.22$ and $$\mu_1=\frac{2.22}{0.16}=13.875=\frac{111}{8}.$$

Step 5 — back-substitute. $$\mu_2=\frac{1.6+0.4(13.875)}{0.52}=\frac{1.6+5.55}{0.52}=\frac{7.15}{0.52}=13.75=\frac{55}{4}, \qquad \mu_3=1+0.8(13.75)=1+11=12 .$$ (Keys L18_mu1, L18_mu2, L18_mu3; simulation from state $1$ gives a mean of $13.828920$ transitions, L18_mu1_mc.)

Why so long? Only one arc leaves the transient triangle, the $0.2$ from state $3$ to state $5$, and the chain reaches state $3$ only intermittently. A rough sanity estimate: the chain spends roughly a third of its transient time at state $3$ and escapes with probability $0.2$ per visit, so absorption should take on the order of $3/0.2\approx15$ steps — the exact $13.875$ is in the right neighborhood.

Recipe — choosing the right linear system.
  1. Classify first. Identify the recurrent classes and the transient states (§2). Lump every recurrent class into one absorbing state unless the question asks something about behavior inside a class.
  2. Decide what the unknown is. "Which trap?" $\Rightarrow$ absorption probabilities $a_i$, one target at a time. "How long until any trap?" $\Rightarrow$ expected times $\mu_i$. "How long until one specific state $s$?" $\Rightarrow$ mean first passage times $t_i$ (§4.4): make $s$ absorbing and re-use the $\mu$ machinery.
  3. Write the boundary conditions first. $a_s=1$ and $a_i=0$ at the other absorbing states; or $\mu_i=0$ at every absorbing state; or $t_s=0$.
  4. Write one equation per transient state by first-step analysis, substituting the boundary values immediately. Charge $+1$ if and only if you are counting time.
  5. Solve and check. Probabilities must lie in $[0,1]$ and the $a_i$ over all absorbing targets must sum to $1$; expected times must be positive and at least $1$ from any transient state.

Example 4.3 (spider and fly — B&T Example 7.12, on the chain of B&T Example 7.2 and its Figure 7.19, with $m=4$). A fly moves along a line in unit increments: at each time period it moves one unit left with probability $0.3$, one unit right with probability $0.3$, and stays put with probability $0.4$, independently of everything else. Spiders sit at positions $1$ and $m$; if the fly lands on a spider it is caught and the process ends. Take $m=4$, so the states are the fly's positions $1,2,3,4$ with $1$ and $4$ absorbing and $$p_{21}=p_{23}=0.3,\ p_{22}=0.4;\qquad p_{32}=p_{34}=0.3,\ p_{33}=0.4 .$$ Find (i) the expected time until the fly is caught, and (ii) the probability it is caught by the right-hand spider.
Fig. 4.5 — The spider-and-fly chain for $m=4$ (B&T Example 7.2, its Figure 7.19). Self-loops of probability $0.4$ are the fly standing still.

(i) Expected time. Boundary: $\mu_1=\mu_4=0$. For the two transient states, $$\mu_2=1+0.3\mu_1+0.4\mu_2+0.3\mu_3=1+0.4\mu_2+0.3\mu_3,$$ $$\mu_3=1+0.3\mu_2+0.4\mu_3+0.3\mu_4=1+0.3\mu_2+0.4\mu_3 .$$ The chain is symmetric under $2\leftrightarrow3$ (relabel positions by reflection and the transition probabilities are unchanged), so $\mu_2=\mu_3=:\mu$. Both equations then collapse to the same one: $$\mu=1+0.4\mu+0.3\mu=1+0.7\mu\;\Longrightarrow\;0.3\,\mu=1\;\Longrightarrow\;\mu=\frac{10}{3}=3.333333 .$$ (Keys spider_mu2, spider_mu3; simulation gives $3.331443$.) If you do not want to invoke symmetry, eliminate directly. The second equation rearranges to $0.6\mu_3=1+0.3\mu_2$, i.e. $\mu_3=(1+0.3\mu_2)/0.6$. The first rearranges to $0.6\mu_2=1+0.3\mu_3$, and substituting, $$0.3\mu_3=0.3\cdot\frac{1+0.3\mu_2}{0.6}=0.5\left(1+0.3\mu_2\right)=0.5+0.15\mu_2 ,$$ so $0.6\mu_2=1+0.5+0.15\mu_2$, giving $0.45\mu_2=1.5$ and $\mu_2=10/3$ as before.

Interpretation of $10/3$. From either interior state the fly escapes the pair $\{2,3\}$ only by the $0.3$ arc into a spider; the self-loops and the $2\leftrightarrow3$ moves waste time. Because the two interior states are interchangeable, the effective escape probability per step is exactly $0.3$, and the time to absorption is geometric with parameter $0.3$ — mean $1/0.3=10/3$ (G2 §3). The linear system just rediscovered the geometric mean.

(ii) Which spider. Take $s=4$, so $a_4=1$, $a_1=0$, and $$a_2=0.3(0)+0.4a_2+0.3a_3\;\Longrightarrow\;0.6a_2=0.3a_3 ,$$ $$a_3=0.3a_2+0.4a_3+0.3(1)\;\Longrightarrow\;0.6a_3=0.3a_2+0.3 .$$ From the first, $a_3=2a_2$. Substituting into the second, $0.6(2a_2)=0.3a_2+0.3$, i.e. $1.2a_2-0.3a_2=0.3$, so $0.9a_2=0.3$ and $$a_2=\frac{1}{3}=0.333333,\qquad a_3=\frac{2}{3}=0.666667 .$$ (Keys spider_a2, spider_a3; simulation from state $2$ gives $0.332742$.) These are the linear interpolation $a_i=(i-1)/(m-1)$ — the signature of a symmetric random walk, and exactly the fair-game gambler's ruin formula of §4.3. The self-loop probability $0.4$ cancels out of the absorption probabilities entirely: standing still cannot change where you eventually end up, only how long you take to get there.

4.3 Gambler's ruin

The single most important absorption problem in the course, because it is the one with a closed form. B&T give it as Example 7.11.

Example 4.4 (gambler's ruin; B&T Example 7.11 and its Figure 7.18). A gambler bets \$1 on each of a sequence of independent rounds, winning with probability $p$ and losing with probability $1-p$. She plays until she either accumulates a target fortune of \$$m$ or runs out of money. Starting with \$$i$, what is the probability $a_i$ that she reaches \$$m$, and what is the expected number $D_i$ of rounds played?
Fig. 4.6 — The gambler's ruin chain (B&T Example 7.11, its Figure 7.18). The state is the current fortune; $0$ (ruin) and $m$ (target) are absorbing.

Setting up. The state $X_n$ is the fortune after $n$ rounds, taking values $0,1,\ldots,m$. States $0$ and $m$ are absorbing; every other state has $p_{i,i+1}=p$ and $p_{i,i-1}=1-p$. Fix the target $s=m$, so $a_i$ is the probability of winning. First-step analysis gives $$a_0=0,\qquad a_m=1,\qquad a_i=p\,a_{i+1}+(1-p)\,a_{i-1},\quad i=1,\ldots,m-1 .$$

The trick that makes it solvable. Because $p+(1-p)=1$ we may write the left-hand side as $a_i=p\,a_i+(1-p)\,a_i$ — this is the only clever step in the whole derivation. Subtracting, $$p\,a_i+(1-p)a_i=p\,a_{i+1}+(1-p)a_{i-1} \;\Longrightarrow\; p\,(a_{i+1}-a_i)=(1-p)\,(a_i-a_{i-1}).$$ Introduce the successive differences $$\delta_i=a_{i+1}-a_i,\qquad i=0,1,\ldots,m-1,$$ and the odds ratio $$\rho=\frac{1-p}{p},$$ the ratio of the probability of losing a round to the probability of winning one. The relation above becomes simply $\delta_i=\rho\,\delta_{i-1}$ for $i=1,\ldots,m-1$, so by induction $$\delta_i=\rho^{\,i}\delta_0,\qquad i=0,\ldots,m-1 .$$

Pin down $\delta_0$. The differences telescope: $$\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\delta_i=(a_1-a_0)+(a_2-a_1)+\cdots+(a_m-a_{m-1})=a_m-a_0=1-0=1 .$$ Substituting $\delta_i=\rho^i\delta_0$, $$\delta_0\left(1+\rho+\rho^2+\cdots+\rho^{\,m-1}\right)=1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \delta_0=\frac{1}{1+\rho+\cdots+\rho^{\,m-1}} .$$

Assemble $a_i$. Since $a_0=0$, $$a_i=a_0+\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\delta_k=\delta_0\left(1+\rho+\cdots+\rho^{\,i-1}\right) =\frac{1+\rho+\cdots+\rho^{\,i-1}}{1+\rho+\cdots+\rho^{\,m-1}} .$$ Now evaluate the two finite geometric sums (G2 §3). If $\rho\ne1$, i.e. $p\ne\tfrac12$, the numerator is $(1-\rho^{\,i})/(1-\rho)$ and the denominator is $(1-\rho^{\,m})/(1-\rho)$, so the common factor $1/(1-\rho)$ cancels; if $\rho=1$ each sum is just its number of terms. Therefore $$\boxed{\;a_i=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1-\rho^{\,i}}{1-\rho^{\,m}}, & p\ne\tfrac12,\\[2ex] \dfrac{i}{m}, & p=\tfrac12,\end{cases}\qquad \rho=\frac{1-p}{p}. \;}$$

Expected duration. The same chain, now counting time: $D_0=D_m=0$ and $$D_i=1+p\,D_{i+1}+(1-p)\,D_{i-1},\qquad i=1,\ldots,m-1 .$$ This is the $a$-recursion with an extra constant $1$, so its solution is any particular solution plus the general solution of the homogeneous problem. For a particular solution try $D_i=ci$ (linear in $i$): $$ci=1+p\,c(i+1)+(1-p)\,c(i-1)=1+ci+c\big(p-(1-p)\big)=1+ci+c(2p-1),$$ so $0=1+c(2p-1)$ and $c=1/(1-2p)$, valid when $p\ne\tfrac12$. The homogeneous equation $h_i=p\,h_{i+1}+(1-p)h_{i-1}$ is the one we just solved: its solutions are $h_i=A+B\rho^{\,i}$. Hence $D_i=\dfrac{i}{1-2p}+A+B\rho^{\,i}$. Impose $D_0=0$: $A+B=0$, so $B=-A$ and $D_i=\dfrac{i}{1-2p}+A\left(1-\rho^{\,i}\right)$. Impose $D_m=0$: $$\frac{m}{1-2p}+A\left(1-\rho^{\,m}\right)=0\;\Longrightarrow\;A=-\frac{m}{(1-2p)\left(1-\rho^{\,m}\right)} .$$ Substituting and recognizing $a_i=(1-\rho^i)/(1-\rho^m)$, $$\boxed{\;D_i=\begin{cases}\dfrac{i-m\,a_i}{1-2p}, & p\ne\tfrac12,\\[2ex] i\,(m-i), & p=\tfrac12.\end{cases}\;}$$ For $p=\tfrac12$ the linear trial fails (the coefficient $1-2p$ vanishes) and one tries a quadratic: $D_i=i(m-i)$ works, since $1+\tfrac12\big[(i+1)(m-i-1)+(i-1)(m-i+1)\big]=1+\tfrac12\big[2im-2i^2-2\big]=i(m-i)$. Every one of these closed forms is checked in computes/g5_s4.py against a direct numpy.linalg.solve of the $(m-1)\times(m-1)$ system, for $(p,m)\in\{(0.5,10),(0.4,10),(0.6,10),(0.45,20)\}$: agreement to $3\times10^{-13}$ or better.

Practice 4.6. Show that in a fair game ($p=1/2$) the expected duration $D_i=i(m-i)$ can be read off from a single conservation statement, without solving any recursion.
Solution

In a fair game the fortune is a martingale: $\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}\mid X_n]=X_n$, so $\mathbb{E}[X_n]=i$ for all $n$, and at absorption $\mathbb{E}[X_\infty]=m\,a_i+0\cdot(1-a_i)=i$, giving $a_i=i/m$ — the fair-game absorption formula with no algebra. For the duration, apply the same idea to $X_n^2-n$: since each step changes $X$ by $\pm1$ with equal probability, $\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}^2\mid X_n]=\tfrac12(X_n+1)^2+\tfrac12(X_n-1)^2=X_n^2+1$, so $\mathbb{E}[X_n^2]-n$ is constant, equal to $i^2$. At absorption $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=m^2a_i=m^2(i/m)=mi$, so $mi-D_i=i^2$ and $D_i=mi-i^2=i(m-i)$. (This martingale argument is not required by 6.041 — it is offered as a cross-check on the algebra above, and it reproduces $D_{10}=10\cdot10=100$ for $m=20$.)

4.4 Mean first passage and mean recurrence times

L18 slide 7 takes the $\mu$ machinery and points it at a different question: not "when do I get stuck" but "when do I first reach a particular state $s$", in a chain that need not have any absorbing states at all.

Definition (L18 slide 7, B&T §7.4). Consider a chain with a single recurrent class and fix a recurrent state $s$.
  • The mean first passage time from $i$ to $s$ is $$t_i=\mathbb{E}\big[\min\{n\ge0:X_n=s\}\mid X_0=i\big].$$ Note the $n\ge0$: starting at $s$ costs nothing, so $t_s=0$.
  • The mean recurrence time of $s$ is $$t_s^{*}=\mathbb{E}\big[\min\{n\ge1:X_n=s\}\mid X_0=s\big],$$ the expected number of steps to return to $s$. The $n\ge1$ is what makes it interesting.
Theorem (L18 slide 7). The $t_i$ are the unique solution of $$t_s=0,\qquad t_i=1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{ij}\,t_j\ \ \text{for all } i\ne s ,$$ and then $$t_s^{*}=1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{sj}\,t_j .$$

Derivation. For the $t_i$: the transitions out of $s$ are irrelevant to how long it takes to first reach $s$, so replace the chain by one in which $s$ has been made absorbing ($p_{ss}=1$, $p_{sj}=0$ for $j\ne s$). In the new chain every other state is transient (they were all in one recurrent class, and now every path is eventually swallowed by $s$), and "time to reach $s$" is literally "time to absorption". So the equations are the $\mu$ equations of §4.2, verbatim, with $t_s=0$ playing the role of $\mu_{\text{absorbing}}=0$. Uniqueness is the same $Q^n\to0$ argument.

Derivation of the recurrence formula (L18 slide 7 states it without proof). Starting at $s$, the chain must make at least one transition — that is what $n\ge1$ means — landing at $j$ with probability $p_{sj}$. From $j$ the remaining number of steps to reach $s$ has mean $t_j$ (this is the first passage quantity, and it is legitimate to use it even for $j=s$, where $t_s=0$ correctly records that no further steps are needed). By the total expectation theorem, conditioning on $X_1$, $$t_s^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^m p_{sj}\big(1+t_j\big)=\sum_j p_{sj}+\sum_j p_{sj}t_j=1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{sj}\,t_j ,$$ using $\sum_j p_{sj}=1$. $\blacksquare$

Property (the bridge back to §3; B&T Problem 7.34). For a chain with a single recurrent aperiodic class, $$\pi_s=\frac{1}{t_s^{*}} .$$ Why. By §3, $\pi_s$ is the long-run fraction of time spent at $s$. Over $n$ steps the chain visits $s$ about $n\pi_s$ times, and consecutive visits are separated by an average of $t_s^{*}$ steps, so $n\approx (n\pi_s)\,t_s^{*}$, giving $\pi_s t_s^{*}=1$. (A frequency and its mean gap are always reciprocal — the same statement as "if buses come at rate $\lambda$, the mean headway is $1/\lambda$", G4 §3.)
Interpretation — $\pi_s=1/t_s^{*}$ is the most reusable sentence in this note. Read it as a statement about frequency versus gap, not as a formula to memorize. $\pi_s$ answers "what fraction of my life do I spend at $s$?" and $t_s^{*}$ answers "how long between consecutive visits to $s$?" — and those two questions are the same question asked in opposite units, exactly like "3 buses per hour" and "20 minutes apart". Three consequences worth carrying around: (i) a state you visit one week in four has a mean return time of four weeks, no matter how complicated the rest of the chain is; (ii) a rare state ($\pi_s$ small) is automatically a long-wait state ($t_s^{*}$ large), which is why "it hardly ever happens" and "when it does, you waited ages" are not two independent facts about a chain; (iii) it gives you a free cross-check whenever you have computed a steady state one way and a first-passage time another — as Example 4.5 does three separate times. It also gives a cheap route to a mean recurrence time you would otherwise get by solving a linear system: find $\pi$ from the balance equations of §3 and invert it.

4.5 Conditioning on which absorbing state you reach

Here is the subtlest idea in the section, and rec19(e) is built on it. Suppose you are told after the fact that the chain ended up absorbed at $s$, and you want the expected time it took. This is not $\mu_i$: $\mu_i$ averages over all absorption events, including the ones that ended somewhere else, and those may be systematically faster or slower.

Property (conditional transition probabilities; rec19 P1(e)). Let $A$ be the event that the chain is eventually absorbed at $s$, and let $a_k=\mathbf{P}(A\mid X_0=k)$ as in §4.1. For any $i$ with $a_i>0$, $$\tilde p_{ij}\;:=\;\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i,\,A)\;=\;\frac{a_j\,p_{ij}}{a_i} .$$ The chain conditioned on $A$ is again a Markov chain, with these transition probabilities.

Derivation, every step. Start from the definition and apply Bayes' rule (G1 §2) with the conditioning event $\{X_n=i\}$ carried along throughout: $$\tilde p_{ij}=\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i,A) =\frac{\mathbf{P}(A\mid X_{n+1}=j,\,X_n=i)\;\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i)}{\mathbf{P}(A\mid X_n=i)} .$$ Now handle the three pieces. The middle factor is $p_{ij}$ by definition. The denominator is $a_i$ by definition of the absorption probability. For the numerator's first factor, $A$ is an event about the chain from time $n+1$ onward, so by the Markov property the conditioning on $X_n=i$ adds nothing once $X_{n+1}=j$ is known: $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid X_{n+1}=j,\,X_n=i)=\mathbf{P}(A\mid X_{n+1}=j)=a_j ,$$ the last step by time homogeneity. Putting the three together gives $\tilde p_{ij}=a_jp_{ij}/a_i$. $\blacksquare$

These really are transition probabilities. Nonnegativity is clear. For the row sum, use the absorption equation $a_i=\sum_j p_{ij}a_j$ itself: $$\sum_j \tilde p_{ij}=\sum_j\frac{a_jp_{ij}}{a_i}=\frac{1}{a_i}\sum_j p_{ij}a_j=\frac{a_i}{a_i}=1 .$$ So the absorption equations are exactly the normalization condition for the conditioned chain — a pleasing piece of bookkeeping, and a useful check when you compute the $\tilde p_{ij}$ by hand.

Interpretation — conditioning on the future re-weights the present. The factor $a_j/a_i$ is worth staring at: it says that given you know the ending, every step you take is retro-actively judged by how much it helped you get there. A move to a state $j$ that is friendlier to $s$ than your current state ($a_j>a_i$) becomes more likely than the chain's own $p_{ij}$ said; a move to a less friendly state becomes less likely; a move to a state that can never reach $s$ vanishes. This is the same phenomenon as the survivor's account of a lottery: told only about the winners, the "probability" of each choice they made looks inflated. Two practical consequences. First, it explains why the conditional expected time is not $\mu_i$: you are averaging over a different chain, not merely over a subset of runs of the old one. Second, it turns a hard question ("expected time to absorption given we ended at $s$") into a routine one: build $\tilde P$, then run the ordinary §4.2 recipe on it — no new theory, just new numbers. The recipe box of Fig. 4.4 shows exactly this as the left-hand branch's extra step.
Gotcha — this is not a uniform renormalization. The natural but wrong instinct is: "delete the states that lead to the wrong absorbing state, then scale each row up so it sums to $1$ again". That would multiply every entry of row $i$ by the same constant. The correct weights $a_j/a_i$ depend on $j$ as well as $i$: transitions toward states that are friendly to $s$ get boosted more than transitions toward states that are barely friendly. Two consequences worth memorizing: (i) a self-transition is unchanged, $\tilde p_{ii}=a_ip_{ii}/a_i=p_{ii}$; (ii) any state $j$ with $a_j=0$ disappears entirely, $\tilde p_{ij}=0$. Both are visible in Figure 4.9, inside the part-(e) solution below (expand it to see them).

Solution — (h) expected number of days until she is 6-1 again (two independent methods)

The chain. Same as Fig. 4.8 except $p_{9,6\text{-}1}=p_{15,6\text{-}1}=\tfrac18$ and $p_{9,9}=p_{15,15}=\tfrac78$. Practice 4.10. True or false: if a chain has two absorbing states $s$ and $s'$, then the expected time to absorption conditioned on ending at $s$ is always at least as large as the unconditional expected time. Justify.
Solution

False. The unconditional mean is a weighted average of the two conditional means, $\mu_i=a_i\tilde\mu_i^{(s)}+(1-a_i)\tilde\mu_i^{(s')}$ (total expectation theorem, G4 §1, conditioning on which absorbing state is reached). A weighted average lies between its two ingredients, so whichever conditional mean is larger, the other is smaller than $\mu_i$. rec19 gives both signs at once: from $6\text{-}1$, $\tilde\mu_1=3.6501>\mu_1=3.5217$, but from $6\text{-}2$, $\tilde\mu_2=2.3208<\mu_2=2.7391$. The correct general statement is only that $\min(\tilde\mu^{(s)},\tilde\mu^{(s')})\le\mu\le\max(\tilde\mu^{(s)},\tilde\mu^{(s')})$.

4.7 Random walks on the integers

Every absorption problem in this section so far has been a random walk in disguise: the gambler's fortune of §4.3, the fly of Example 4.3, the queue of §1. The walk is worth isolating and naming, because it is the one Markov chain that turns up on every exam and in every application — a queue length, a stock price, a molecule diffusing along a tube, a lead in a game of tennis. This subsection collects what §4.1–§4.3 already prove, adds the derivation route the exam usually asks for (solve the difference equation directly, rather than by the telescoping trick of §4.3), and finishes the picture with the two boundary conditions the course leaves implicit: reflecting barriers, and no barriers at all.

Definition (simple random walk). Let $Z_1,Z_2,\ldots$ be independent and identically distributed steps with $$\mathbf{P}(Z_n=+1)=p,\qquad \mathbf{P}(Z_n=-1)=q=1-p,$$ and let $X_0=i$ be the (deterministic) starting position. The simple random walk is $$X_{n}=X_0+Z_1+\cdots+Z_n,\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad X_{n+1}=X_n+Z_{n+1}.$$ It is symmetric when $p=q=\tfrac12$. Here $X_n$ is the position after $n$ steps, $p$ is the probability of a step to the right, and $q$ that of a step to the left.

It is a Markov chain, and that is the whole point. The next position $X_{n+1}=X_n+Z_{n+1}$ depends on the past only through the current position $X_n$, because $Z_{n+1}$ is independent of everything before it. So the walk satisfies the Markov property of §1 with transition probabilities $$p_{i,i+1}=p,\qquad p_{i,i-1}=q,\qquad p_{ij}=0\ \text{ otherwise},$$ and every machine built in this note applies to it without modification: the $n$-step recursion of §1, the classification of §2, the birth–death local balance of §3 (it is a birth–death chain — no arc skips a state), and the two linear systems of §4.1 and §4.2. Nothing below is a new theory; it is the old theory with $p_{i,i\pm1}$ substituted in.

Interpretation — drift and spread. One step has mean $$\mathbb{E}[Z_n]=(+1)p+(-1)q=p-q=2p-1,$$ called the drift, and variance $\operatorname{var}(Z_n)=\mathbb{E}[Z_n^2]-(\mathbb{E}[Z_n])^2=1-(p-q)^2=4pq$ (using $Z_n^2=1$ always). Since the steps are independent, expectation is additive and so is variance (G2 §3), giving $$\mathbb{E}[X_n]=i+(p-q)n,\qquad \operatorname{var}(X_n)=4pq\,n,\qquad \text{sd}(X_n)=2\sqrt{pq}\,\sqrt n .$$ The mean moves linearly in $n$ while the spread grows only like $\sqrt n$: after $n=100$ steps a $p=0.55$ walk has moved $10.000000$ units on average with standard deviation $9.949874$ (keys rw_mean100_p55, rw_sd100_p55), so the drift is only just emerging from the noise; by $n=10{,}000$ the mean displacement is $1000.000000$ while the standard deviation is only $99.498744$ (keys rw_mean10k_p55, rw_sd10k_p55), and the drift dominates completely. The symmetric walk has zero mean at every $n$ and spread exactly $\sqrt n$ (rw_sd100_p50). This linear-versus-square-root race is the same one behind the weak law and the central limit theorem (G6 §1–§2), and $X_n$ is precisely a sum of i.i.d. terms, so the CLT says $X_n$ is approximately normal for large $n$.

Two absorbing barriers: hitting probabilities

Put absorbing barriers at $0$ and at $N$: the walk stops the moment it touches either end, so $p_{00}=p_{NN}=1$, and the states $1,\ldots,N-1$ are transient. This is exactly the gambler's ruin chain of §4.3 with $m$ renamed $N$ and the losing probability $1-p$ renamed $q$, so the answers are already ours: $$a_i=\mathbf{P}\big(\text{hit }N\text{ before }0\mid X_0=i\big) =\begin{cases}\dfrac{1-\rho^{\,i}}{1-\rho^{\,N}}, & p\ne\tfrac12,\\[2ex] \dfrac iN, & p=\tfrac12,\end{cases}\qquad \rho=\frac qp .$$ What §4.3 does not do is solve the difference equation as a difference equation; it uses a telescoping trick special to this problem. The general method is worth having, because it is what you fall back on when the trick does not present itself, and because it explains where the two cases come from.

Step 1 — the equation. First-step analysis (§4.1) applied to a transient $i$ gives $$a_i=p\,a_{i+1}+q\,a_{i-1},\qquad i=1,\ldots,N-1,\qquad a_0=0,\ a_N=1,$$ the boundary conditions saying that from $0$ the walk is already absorbed at the wrong end and from $N$ at the right one. Rearranged, this is a homogeneous linear difference equation with constant coefficients: $$p\,a_{i+1}-a_i+q\,a_{i-1}=0 .$$

Step 2 — the characteristic equation. For such an equation, try a geometric solution $a_i=r^{\,i}$ with $r\ne0$ — the same "guess an exponential" move used for linear recursions throughout the course. Substituting, $$p\,r^{\,i+1}-r^{\,i}+q\,r^{\,i-1}=0 ,$$ and dividing through by $r^{\,i-1}$ (legitimate since $r\ne0$), $$p\,r^{2}-r+q=0 .$$ Solve by the quadratic formula, using $q=1-p$ to simplify the discriminant: $$1-4pq=1-4p(1-p)=1-4p+4p^{2}=(1-2p)^{2} \;\Longrightarrow\;\sqrt{1-4pq}=|1-2p| ,$$ so, taking $p>\tfrac12$ so that $|1-2p|=2p-1$ (the case $p<\tfrac12$ only swaps the labels of the two roots), $$r=\frac{1\pm(2p-1)}{2p} \;\Longrightarrow\; r_1=\frac{1+(2p-1)}{2p}=\frac{2p}{2p}=1, \qquad r_2=\frac{1-(2p-1)}{2p}=\frac{2-2p}{2p}=\frac qp=\rho .$$ So the two characteristic roots are always $1$ and $\rho=q/p$, and they coincide exactly when $p=q=\tfrac12$. That is the source of the two cases.

Step 3a — the asymmetric case $p\ne\tfrac12$. Distinct roots give the general solution $$a_i=A\cdot1^{\,i}+B\,\rho^{\,i}=A+B\rho^{\,i},$$ with $A,B$ constants fixed by the boundaries. From $a_0=0$: $A+B=0$, so $B=-A$ and $a_i=A\left(1-\rho^{\,i}\right)$. From $a_N=1$: $A\left(1-\rho^{\,N}\right)=1$, so $A=1/(1-\rho^{\,N})$ — legitimate because $\rho\ne1$ makes $\rho^N\ne1$. Therefore $$a_i=\frac{1-\rho^{\,i}}{1-\rho^{\,N}} ,$$ which is the §4.3 formula, now obtained without the telescoping trick.

Step 3b — the symmetric case $p=q=\tfrac12$. Now $\rho=1$ and the two roots collapse into the single root $r=1$; the pair $1^{\,i}$ and $\rho^{\,i}$ is no longer two independent solutions, it is the same solution twice, and $a_i=A+B\cdot1^{\,i}=A+B$ cannot meet two different boundary conditions. For a repeated root $r$ the second independent solution of a linear difference equation is $i\,r^{\,i}$ — check it directly here, with $r=1$, by substituting $a_i=i$ into the right-hand side $\tfrac12a_{i+1}+\tfrac12a_{i-1}$: $$\tfrac12(i+1)+\tfrac12(i-1)=\tfrac12\big(i+1+i-1\big)=i=a_i .\ \checkmark$$ So the general solution is $a_i=A+Bi$, a straight line. Impose $a_0=0$: $A=0$. Impose $a_N=1$: $BN=1$, so $B=1/N$ and $$\boxed{\;a_i=\frac iN\quad(p=\tfrac12),\qquad a_i=\frac{1-\rho^{\,i}}{1-\rho^{\,N}}\quad(p\ne\tfrac12),\qquad \rho=\frac qp .\;}$$

Interpretation. The symmetric answer $i/N$ is linear interpolation between the two barriers: with no drift, the only thing that matters is how far you are from each end, in proportion. It is also the martingale statement $\mathbb{E}[X_\infty]=\mathbb{E}[X_0]$ of Practice 4.6: $N\cdot a_i+0\cdot(1-a_i)=i$. With drift, $\rho^{\,i}$ replaces $i$ — distance is measured on a geometric rather than arithmetic scale, and a barrier a fixed number of steps downwind becomes overwhelmingly more likely than one the same distance upwind.

Expected duration: how long the walk lasts

The same chain, now counting steps. Write $D_i=\mathbb{E}[\text{steps until }X\text{ hits }0 \text{ or }N\mid X_0=i]$ — the $\mu_i$ of §4.2 for this chain. First-step analysis with the $+1$ charge gives $$D_i=1+p\,D_{i+1}+q\,D_{i-1},\qquad i=1,\ldots,N-1,\qquad D_0=D_N=0 .$$ This is the same homogeneous equation as before plus the constant $1$, so by the standard structure theorem for linear equations its general solution is $$D_i=(\text{any one particular solution})+(\text{general solution of the homogeneous equation}),$$ and we already know the homogeneous solutions: $A+B\rho^{\,i}$ when $p\ne\tfrac12$, and $A+Bi$ when $p=\tfrac12$.

Symmetric case, in full. The homogeneous solutions are $A+Bi$, so a particular solution must be sought one degree higher: try $D_i=ci^{2}$. Substituting it into the right-hand side of the recursion, $$1+\tfrac12c(i+1)^{2}+\tfrac12c(i-1)^{2} =1+\tfrac12c\big(i^{2}+2i+1\big)+\tfrac12c\big(i^{2}-2i+1\big) =1+c\,i^{2}+c ,$$ where the cross terms $\pm2i$ cancel. Setting this equal to $D_i=ci^{2}$ leaves $0=1+c$, so $c=-1$ and $D_i=-i^{2}$ is a particular solution. The general solution is therefore $$D_i=-i^{2}+A+Bi .$$ Impose $D_0=0$: $A=0$. Impose $D_N=0$: $-N^{2}+BN=0$, so $B=N$. Hence $$\boxed{\;D_i=Ni-i^{2}=i\,(N-i)\quad(p=\tfrac12).\;}$$ (§4.3 arrives at $i(m-i)$ by verifying a guessed quadratic; this is the same answer derived rather than guessed.)

Asymmetric case. With $\rho\ne1$ the homogeneous solutions are $A+B\rho^{\,i}$, which contains no term linear in $i$, so a linear trial $D_i=ci$ is available: $$1+p\,c(i+1)+q\,c(i-1)=1+c\,i+c\,(p-q)=c\,i \;\Longrightarrow\;0=1+c(p-q)\;\Longrightarrow\;c=\frac{1}{q-p} ,$$ the middle equality using $p+q=1$ to collect the $ci$ terms. So $D_i=\dfrac{i}{q-p}+A+B\rho^{\,i}$. Impose $D_0=0$: $A+B=0$, i.e. $B=-A$, giving $D_i=\dfrac{i}{q-p}+A\left(1-\rho^{\,i}\right)$. Impose $D_N=0$: $$\frac{N}{q-p}+A\left(1-\rho^{\,N}\right)=0 \;\Longrightarrow\;A=-\frac{N}{(q-p)\left(1-\rho^{\,N}\right)} ,$$ and substituting, with $a_i=(1-\rho^{\,i})/(1-\rho^{\,N})$ recognized in the second term, $$\boxed{\;D_i=\frac{i-N\,a_i}{q-p}\quad(p\ne\tfrac12).\;}$$ Note $q-p=1-2p$, so this is the §4.3 formula written in the walk's notation.

Interpretation — the symmetric walk is quadratically slow. $D_i=i(N-i)$ is the product of the two distances to the barriers, so it is quadratic in the distance scale, not linear. Double the board and double the starting point — from $N=10$, $i=3$ to $N=20$, $i=6$ — and the hitting probability is unchanged at $0.3$ (it depends only on the ratio $i/N$), while the expected duration goes from $21$ to $84$: four times longer, not twice (keys rw_p11_D, rw_p11_D_big, rw_p11_ratio). This is the $\sqrt n$ spread of Fig. 4.11 read backwards: to travel a distance $d$ a driftless walk needs the noise to accumulate to $d$, and since noise accumulates like $\sqrt n$, it takes $n\approx d^{2}$ steps. A drifting walk, by contrast, covers distance $d$ in about $d/|p-q|$ steps — linearly. It is the same phenomenon as the §4.3 Gotcha that the fair game lasts longest.
Example 4.7 (a walk with barriers at $0$ and $12$). A token sits at position $5$ on a board with squares $0,1,\ldots,12$. Each turn it moves one square right with probability $p=0.55$ and one square left with probability $q=0.45$; the game ends when the token reaches square $0$ or square $12$. Find the probability that it ends at $12$, and the expected number of turns. Compare with a fair token ($p=\tfrac12$).

Step 1 — identify the chain. States $0,\ldots,12$; $0$ and $12$ absorbing; interior rows $p_{i,i+1}=0.55$, $p_{i,i-1}=0.45$. This is the two-barrier walk with $N=12$ and $i=5$, so both closed forms apply directly.

Step 2 — the odds ratio. $$\rho=\frac qp=\frac{0.45}{0.55}=\frac{9}{11}=0.818182$$ (key rw_rho). Since $p>\tfrac12$ we have $\rho<1$: the walk is pushed toward the upper barrier.

Step 3 — the hitting probability. Compute the two powers first: $$\rho^{\,5}=\left(\frac{9}{11}\right)^{5}=\frac{59049}{161051}=0.366648,\qquad \rho^{\,12}=\left(\frac{9}{11}\right)^{12}=0.089991$$ (keys rw_ex_rho_i, rw_ex_rho_N). Then $$a_5=\frac{1-\rho^{\,5}}{1-\rho^{\,12}}=\frac{1-0.366648}{1-0.089991} =\frac{0.633352}{0.910009}=0.695984$$ (key rw_ex_a; a $200{,}000$-walk simulation gives $0.696240$, rw_ex_a_mc). A fair token would have $a_5=5/12=0.416667$ (rw_ex_a_sym, simulation $0.416385$): a $5$-point edge per turn raises the chance of finishing at the top from about $42\%$ to about $70\%$.

Step 4 — the expected duration. With $q-p=0.45-0.55=-0.10$, $$D_5=\frac{i-N\,a_i}{q-p}=\frac{5-12(0.695984)}{-0.10}=\frac{5-8.351812}{-0.10} =\frac{-3.351812}{-0.10}=33.518119$$ (key rw_ex_D; simulation $33.437410$, rw_ex_D_mc). Both signs in the fraction are negative, as they must be when $p>\tfrac12$ pushes the walk up: $N a_i>i$ exactly when the walk finishes high more often than a fair one would. The fair token instead takes $$D_5=i(N-i)=5\cdot7=35$$ turns (rw_ex_D_sym, simulation $35.062830$) — longer than the drifting token, in agreement with the §4.3 Gotcha that drift in either direction shortens the game.

Check. Both probabilities lie in $[0,1]$; both durations exceed $1$; and the closed forms agree with a direct numpy.linalg.solve of the $11\times11$ first-step system to better than $10^{-12}$ (asserted in computes/g5_s4.py).

One barrier, or none: recurrence of the walk

Let the upper barrier run away, $N\to\infty$, keeping the absorbing barrier at $0$. Two regimes appear, and they are the walk's version of the transient/recurrent dichotomy of §2.

Property (walk on $\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ with $0$ absorbing). Starting from $i>0$:
  • If $p<q$ (downward drift), the walk hits $0$ with probability $1$, and $$\mathbb{E}[\text{steps to hit }0]=\frac{i}{q-p},$$ finite and linear in $i$.
  • If $p=q=\tfrac12$, the walk still hits $0$ with probability $1$ — but the expected time is $+\infty$. The symmetric walk is recurrent yet null recurrent: it always comes back, and the average wait is infinite.
  • If $p>q$ (upward drift), the walk escapes to $+\infty$ with probability $1-\rho^{\,i}>0$ and hits $0$ only with probability $\rho^{\,i}=(q/p)^{\,i}$.

Derivation from the formulas above. The probability of hitting $0$ before $N$ is $1-a_i=(\rho^{\,i}-\rho^{\,N})/(1-\rho^{\,N})$. If $p<q$ then $\rho>1$, so $\rho^{N}\to\infty$ and, dividing numerator and denominator by $\rho^{N}$, $$1-a_i=\frac{\rho^{\,i}/\rho^{\,N}-1}{1/\rho^{\,N}-1}\longrightarrow\frac{0-1}{0-1}=1 ,$$ since $\rho^{\,i}/\rho^{\,N}=\rho^{\,i-N}\to0$ for fixed $i$. If $p>q$ then $\rho<1$, $\rho^{N}\to0$, and $1-a_i\to\rho^{\,i}$ directly. For the symmetric case $1-a_i=1-i/N\to1$. For the durations, take $p<q$ in $D_i=(i-Na_i)/(q-p)$: we just showed $a_i\to0$, and it does so geometrically fast, so $Na_i\to0$ and $D_i\to i/(q-p)$. In the symmetric case $D_i=i(N-i)\to\infty$ for every fixed $i$ — the limit does not exist as a finite number, and the expected hitting time is infinite.

Gotcha — "probability 1" does not mean "soon". The symmetric walk returns to $0$ with certainty and takes forever on average. Both statements hold at once because the distribution of the hitting time has a very heavy tail, so the mean diverges even though the total probability is $1$. This is the discrete cousin of the infinite-mean densities of G3 §1, and it is why §3's steady-state theory always insists on a finite mean recurrence time $t_s^{*}$ before writing $\pi_s=1/t_s^{*}$ (§4.4). For a chain with finitely many states that finiteness is automatic; on an infinite state space it is not.

Reflecting barriers

Replace absorption at the ends by reflection: at $0$ the walk cannot go down, so the downward move is refused and the walk stays put; at $N$ the upward move is refused. Formally, keeping the interior rows unchanged, $$p_{01}=p,\quad p_{00}=q;\qquad p_{N,N-1}=q,\quad p_{NN}=p;\qquad p_{i,i+1}=p,\ p_{i,i-1}=q\ \ (0<i<N).$$ (Some texts reflect strictly, $p_{01}=1$; the qualitative conclusions below are identical.) The change is small on the page and total in its consequences.

Property (what reflection changes). With reflecting barriers there are no absorbing states: from any state the walk can reach any other, so the chain is a single recurrent class, and it is aperiodic because the self-transitions $p_{00}=q>0$ and $p_{NN}=p>0$ create cycles of length $1$. Hence §3 applies: a unique steady state $\pi$ exists, it does not depend on the starting state, and — the chain being birth–death — local balance gives it in closed form. With $r=p/q$, $$\pi_i\,p=\pi_{i+1}\,q\;\Longrightarrow\;\pi_{i+1}=r\,\pi_i\;\Longrightarrow\;\pi_i=r^{\,i}\pi_0, \qquad \pi_i=\frac{r^{\,i}}{\sum_{k=0}^{N}r^{\,k}} ,$$ the last step from the normalization $\sum_i\pi_i=1$. The absorption questions of §4.1–§4.2 become meaningless (every $a_i$ would be $0$, every $\mu_i$ infinite); the meaningful questions are the §3 ones — long-run fraction of time in a state, and mean recurrence time $t_i^{*}=1/\pi_i$ (§4.4).

Numbers. For $N=5$ and $p=0.55$ we have $r=p/q=11/9=1.222222$ and $$\pi=(0.095231,\;0.116394,\;0.142259,\;0.173872,\;0.212510,\;0.259735)$$ (keys rw_ref_up_pi0–rw_ref_up_pi5; a $2{,}000{,}000$-step simulation spends fractions $0.095628$ and $0.259374$ of its time at states $0$ and $5$, rw_ref_up_pi0_mc, rw_ref_up_pi5_mc). The upward drift piles probability against the upper wall — the distribution is geometric in $i$ with ratio $r$ — and the mean recurrence times follow immediately: $t_5^{*}=1/0.259735=3.850085$ steps between successive visits to the top wall, against $t_0^{*}=1/0.095231=10.500771$ for the bottom (rw_ref_up_tstar_top, rw_ref_up_tstar_bot). Reversing the drift to $p=0.45$ reverses the whole vector, by the relabeling symmetry $i\mapsto N-i$.

Interpretation — this is the checkout counter. A reflecting walk is the queue of §1 and §3: the lower wall is "the queue cannot be shorter than empty", the upper wall is "arrivals at a full buffer are turned away". The geometric steady state $\pi_i\propto r^{\,i}$ is the same one §3 derives for birth–death chains, and the stability condition "$r<1$, i.e. arrival rate below service rate" is exactly the condition for the walk to have a proper steady state when the upper wall is removed ($N\to\infty$): then $\sum_{k\ge0}r^{\,k}$ converges only if $r<1$, giving $\pi_i=(1-r)r^{\,i}$, and for $r\ge1$ the queue grows without bound and no steady state exists.
Recipe — which random-walk question is this?
  1. "What is the probability the walk reaches $A$ before $B$?" (ruin, hitting, "which end") $\Rightarrow$ absorption equations (§4.1). Make $A$ and $B$ absorbing, set $a_A=1$, $a_B=0$, write $a_i=p\,a_{i+1}+q\,a_{i-1}$ inside. Closed form: $i/N$ if symmetric, else $(1-\rho^{\,i})/(1-\rho^{\,N})$ with $\rho=q/p$.
  2. "How long until it stops, or first reaches a wall?" $\Rightarrow$ expected-time equations (§4.2): $D_i=1+p\,D_{i+1}+q\,D_{i-1}$, with $D=0$ at the barriers. Closed form: $i(N-i)$ if symmetric, else $(i-N a_i)/(q-p)$. Never forget the $+1$.
  3. "What fraction of time does it spend at state $i$ in the long run?" or "how often does it revisit $i$?" $\Rightarrow$ steady state (§3), which requires reflecting (or wrapping, or infinite-with-inward-drift) boundaries. Use birth–death local balance $\pi_ip=\pi_{i+1}q$ to get $\pi_i\propto(p/q)^{\,i}$, normalize, and read $t_i^{*}=1/\pi_i$ (§4.4).
  4. "Where is it after $n$ steps?" $\Rightarrow$ no chain machinery needed: $X_n$ is a sum of i.i.d. steps, so $\mathbb{E}[X_n]=i+(p-q)n$, $\operatorname{var}(X_n)=4pq\,n$, and the CLT (G6 §2) gives the approximate distribution.
Do not mix them. If the walk has an absorbing barrier there is no steady state worth solving for — the balance equations return $\pi=1$ on the absorbing state and $0$ everywhere else, a true but useless answer, and with two absorbing states they do not even have a unique solution (§3). Conversely, if the barriers reflect, absorption probabilities and expected times to absorption do not exist. Decide first whether the walls swallow the walk or bounce it.

(b) $a_6=6/20=0.3$, unchanged: the hitting probability is a function of the ratio $i/N$ alone, so rescaling the board leaves it fixed. But $D_6=6\cdot14=84$ steps (rw_p11_D_big), a factor of $84/21=4$ (rw_p11_ratio) — doubling all distances quadruples the time. The reason is the $\sqrt n$ spread of the driftless walk: covering a distance $d$ requires the accumulated noise $\sqrt n$ to reach $d$, i.e. $n\approx d^{2}$, so distances enter the duration squared and the probability not at all.

Practice 4.12. A walk starts at $6$ and has $p=0.4$, with an absorbing barrier at $0$ only (no upper barrier). Find the probability it ever hits $0$ and the expected number of steps to do so. Then answer the same two questions for $p=\tfrac12$.
Solution

Drifting down, $p=0.4$, $q=0.6$. Here $\rho=q/p=0.6/0.4=1.5>1$, so by the limit computed above the walk hits $0$ with probability $1$ and $$\mathbb{E}[\text{steps}]=\frac{i}{q-p}=\frac{6}{0.6-0.4}=\frac{6}{0.2}=30$$ (key rw_p12_T6). Sanity check: each step moves the walk down by $0.2$ on average, so covering $6$ units takes about $6/0.2=30$ steps — the drift term does all the work. The two-barrier formulas confirm the limit numerically: with an upper barrier at $N=200$, $a_6=6.286\times10^{-35}$ and $D_6=30.000000$ (keys rw_p12_a_N200, rw_p12_D_N200); already at $N=50$, $D_6=29.999996$.

Symmetric, $p=\tfrac12$. The probability of hitting $0$ is still $1$ — from $1-a_6=1-6/N\to1$ as $N\to\infty$ — but the expected time is infinite: $D_6=6(N-6)$ equals $264$ at $N=50$, $1164$ at $N=200$ and $5964$ at $N=1000$ (keys rw_p12_Dsym_N50, rw_p12_Dsym_N200, rw_p12_Dsym_N1000), growing without bound. The walk is recurrent but null recurrent. Moral: certainty of return and finiteness of the return time are different questions, and only the second one licenses $\pi_s=1/t_s^{*}$.

Practice 4.13. A walk on $\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$ has $p=0.45$ with reflecting barriers at both ends ($p_{00}=q$ at the bottom, $p_{55}=p$ at the top). What fraction of the time does it spend at state $0$ in the long run, how often does it revisit $0$, and what is its average position? Why is it wrong to ask for the "probability of absorption at $0$"?
Solution

The chain is irreducible (each state reaches every other by a run of $\pm1$ steps) and aperiodic (the self-loops at $0$ and $5$ have positive probability), so §3 applies. It is birth–death, so local balance gives $\pi_ip=\pi_{i+1}q$, i.e. $\pi_{i+1}=r\pi_i$ with $$r=\frac pq=\frac{0.45}{0.55}=\frac{9}{11}=0.818182 .$$ Hence $\pi_i=r^{\,i}\pi_0$, and the normalization $\pi_0\sum_{k=0}^{5}r^{\,k}=1$ fixes $\pi_0$, giving $$\pi=(0.259735,\;0.212510,\;0.173872,\;0.142259,\;0.116394,\;0.095231)$$ (keys rw_ref_dn_pi0–rw_ref_dn_pi5). So the walk sits at $0$ a fraction $\pi_0=0.259735$ of the time — about a quarter — and by the mean-recurrence-time formula of §4.4 its visits to $0$ are separated by $$t_0^{*}=\frac{1}{\pi_0}=\frac{1}{0.259735}=3.850085$$ steps on average (key rw_p13_t0star_f). The long-run average position is $\sum_i i\,\pi_i=1.928760$ (rw_p13_mean_f), below the midpoint $2.5$ because the drift is downward.

Why the absorption question is ill-posed. Absorption probabilities are defined only when some state has $p_{kk}=1$ (§4.1). Here $p_{00}=0.55<1$: a walk touching $0$ bounces back up with probability $0.45$ at the next step, so it visits $0$ infinitely often and is never trapped. There is nothing to be absorbed at, and correspondingly the expected time to absorption is infinite. The right questions for a reflecting walk are the steady-state ones answered above.

§4 cheatsheet — absorption.
  • Setup. Lump every recurrent class into one absorbing state; then every state is transient or absorbing. Fix one target $s$ before writing equations.
  • Absorption probabilities (L18 slide 5, B&T §7.4): $a_s=1$; $a_i=0$ at other absorbing states; $a_i=\sum_j p_{ij}a_j$ at transient states. Unique solution because $Q^n\to0$ makes $I-Q$ invertible.
  • Expected time to absorption (L18 slide 6): $\mu_i=0$ at absorbing states; $\mu_i=1+\sum_j p_{ij}\mu_j$ at transient states. The $+1$ is the step you just took.
  • Mean first passage / recurrence (L18 slide 7): $t_s=0$, $t_i=1+\sum_jp_{ij}t_j$; $t_s^{*}=1+\sum_jp_{sj}t_j$; and $\pi_s=1/t_s^{*}$ for a single aperiodic recurrent class.
  • Conditioning on the ending (rec19 P1(e)): $\tilde p_{ij}=a_jp_{ij}/a_i$. Rows sum to $1$ precisely because $a_i=\sum_jp_{ij}a_j$. Self-loops unchanged; states with $a_j=0$ vanish; then re-solve the $\mu$ system on the new chain.
  • Gambler's ruin (B&T Example 7.11), $\rho=(1-p)/p$: $a_i=\dfrac{1-\rho^i}{1-\rho^m}$ ($p\ne\tfrac12$) or $\dfrac im$ ($p=\tfrac12$); $D_i=\dfrac{i-m\,a_i}{1-2p}$ or $i(m-i)$. Unfavorable odds $\Rightarrow a_i\to0$ as $m\to\infty$; the fair game lasts the longest.
  • Random walk (§4.7), $p_{i,i\pm1}=p,q$: drift $p-q$ per step, spread $\sqrt{4pq\,n}$. The difference equation $p\,a_{i+1}-a_i+q\,a_{i-1}=0$ has characteristic roots $1$ and $\rho=q/p$; distinct roots give $a_i=A+B\rho^{\,i}$, the repeated root $p=\tfrac12$ gives $a_i=A+Bi$. With barriers $0,N$: $a_i=i/N$ or $(1-\rho^{\,i})/(1-\rho^{\,N})$, and $D_i=i(N-i)$ or $(i-Na_i)/(q-p)$ — the symmetric duration is quadratic in distance. Reflecting barriers instead: no absorption, birth–death steady state $\pi_i\propto(p/q)^{\,i}$, $t_i^{*}=1/\pi_i$.
  • Spider and fly (B&T Example 7.12, $m=4$): $\mu_2=\mu_3=10/3$, $a_2=1/3$, $a_3=2/3$.
  • rec19 headline numbers. $a_{6\text{-}1}=105/184=0.5707$; $\mu_{6\text{-}1}=81/23=3.5217$; $\mathbb{E}[\text{cones}]=1140/3721=0.3064$; $\tilde\mu_{6\text{-}1}=1763/483=3.6501$; $\mu^{(f)}_{6\text{-}1}=86/13=6.6154$; $\pi_9=\pi_{15}=1/2$; $\mathbb{E}[R]=265/61=4.3443=1/\pi_{6\text{-}1}$.
