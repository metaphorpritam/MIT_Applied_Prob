G3 §4 teaches one transformation skill and teaches it well: given $X$ with a known density and a function $g$, find the density of $Y=g(X)$, either by the CDF method or by the monotone formula $f_Y(y)=f_X\big(h(y)\big)\,|dh/dy|$. This section pushes that skill in the three directions the course has no time for, and all three are standard equipment everywhere else.

First, the one transformation that works for every continuous random variable: its own CDF. Feeding $X$ into $F_X$ always produces a uniform, and running a uniform backwards through $F_X^{-1}$ always produces $X$ — which is, quite literally, how a computer simulates any distribution at all. Second, the two-dimensional version of the monotone formula, whose correction factor is a determinant rather than a derivative; the notes already use this rule twice, in G3 §1 and G4 §1, under the informal name "the extra factor $r$", and this section finally names it. Third, what happens when you sort a sample: the order statistics, of which the maximum and the minimum — already derived in G3 §1 and put to work in G7 §2 — are just the two extreme cases of a single formula.

2.1 Universality of the uniform

Throughout this subsection $X$ is a continuous random variable whose CDF $F(x)=\mathbf{P}(X\le x)$ is continuous everywhere and strictly increasing on the interval where $0<F(x)<1$. Strict monotonicity is what makes $F$ invertible there, so that $F^{-1}(u)$ — the unique $x$ with $F(x)=u$, called the $u$-quantile of $X$ — is well defined for every $u\in(0,1)$. The exponential, the normal, and the uniform on an interval all qualify; a density with a gap in its support, where $F$ is flat, does not, and is handled by the gotcha below.

Theorem (universality of the uniform; both directions). Let $F$ be as above.
  1. Forward. If $X$ has CDF $F$, then $U:=F(X)$ is uniform on $(0,1)$.
  2. Backward. If $U$ is uniform on $(0,1)$, then $X:=F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$.
So $F$ converts any such random variable into a standard uniform, and $F^{-1}$ converts a standard uniform into any such random variable.

Proof of the forward direction. Fix $u\in(0,1)$ and compute the CDF of $U=F(X)$ by the CDF method of G3 §4 — express the event in terms of $X$, then read off a probability of $X$: $$\mathbf{P}(U\le u)=\mathbf{P}\big(F(X)\le u\big)=\mathbf{P}\big(X\le F^{-1}(u)\big),$$ where the second equality holds because $F$ is strictly increasing, so the inequality $F(x)\le u$ holds for exactly the same $x$ as the inequality $x\le F^{-1}(u)$ (a strictly increasing function preserves order, and so does its inverse). The right-hand probability is by definition the CDF of $X$ evaluated at $F^{-1}(u)$: $$\mathbf{P}\big(X\le F^{-1}(u)\big)=F\big(F^{-1}(u)\big)=u,$$ the last step being the definition of an inverse function. Thus $\mathbf{P}(U\le u)=u$ for every $u\in(0,1)$, and $\mathbf{P}(U\le u)$ is $0$ for $u\le 0$ and $1$ for $u\ge 1$ because $F$ takes values in $[0,1]$. That is exactly the CDF of $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ (G3 §1), and a CDF determines a distribution, so $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$. $\blacksquare$

Proof of the backward direction. Now let $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ and set $X=F^{-1}(U)$. Fix any real $x$ and again take the CDF: $$\mathbf{P}(X\le x)=\mathbf{P}\big(F^{-1}(U)\le x\big)=\mathbf{P}\big(U\le F(x)\big),$$ applying $F$ — increasing, hence order-preserving — to both sides of the inequality inside. Since $F(x)\in[0,1]$ and the uniform CDF is $\mathbf{P}(U\le u)=u$ on that range, $$\mathbf{P}\big(U\le F(x)\big)=F(x).$$ So $X$ has CDF $F$, which is what was claimed. $\blacksquare$

Interpretation. The forward direction says that a CDF is a universal flattener: whatever shape $f_X$ has, plotting $F(X)$ instead of $X$ redistributes the probability perfectly evenly over $(0,1)$, because $F(x)$ is by construction "the fraction of the probability lying to the left of $x$", and a randomly chosen point is equally likely to have any fraction to its left. The backward direction is the same statement read as a manufacturing instruction: all the information in a distribution is packed into the shape of $F^{-1}$, which stretches the parts of $(0,1)$ where the density is low and compresses the parts where it is high. Two consequences worth carrying away. Quantiles: $F^{-1}(0.5)$ is the median, $F^{-1}(0.95)$ the $95$th percentile, and the theorem says a random draw's percentile rank is itself uniform. And, at the edge of G7 §4: the p-value of a test with a continuous test statistic is of the form $F(\text{statistic})$, so under the null hypothesis a p-value is uniform on $(0,1)$ — which is precisely why rejecting when $p\le\alpha$ has false-rejection probability $\alpha$.
Gotcha — both hypotheses are doing work. Continuity is essential in the forward direction. If $X$ is discrete, $F(X)$ takes only finitely or countably many values and cannot be uniform: for a fair coin scored $X\in\{0,1\}$, $F(X)\in\{0.5,1\}$, each with probability $1/2$. Strict monotonicity is essential for $F^{-1}$ to be a function at all; where $F$ is flat — an interval carrying no probability — many $x$ share one value of $u$. The standard repair covers every case at once: define the generalized inverse $$F^{-1}(u)=\min\{x:\ F(x)\ge u\},$$ which exists for any CDF whatever, discrete ones included. The backward direction survives verbatim with this definition (the equivalence $F^{-1}(u)\le x\iff u\le F(x)$ still holds), so inverse-CDF sampling works for discrete distributions too — that is how one simulates a geometric, by walking up the PMF until the cumulative sum passes $U$. The forward direction does not survive, and no repair makes it: no function of a discrete random variable is ever continuous.
Recipe — inverse-CDF (inverse transform) sampling. To generate a draw from a target density $f$ using only a uniform random number generator:
  1. Get the CDF. $F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt$, in closed form.
  2. Invert it. Solve $u=F(x)$ for $x$, giving $x=F^{-1}(u)$. This is algebra, not probability.
  3. Draw and map. Draw $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ from the generator and return $X=F^{-1}(U)$.
  4. Check. The map must be increasing, and it must carry $(0,1)$ onto the support of $f$: $F^{-1}(0^{+})$ should be the left end of the support and $F^{-1}(1^{-})$ the right end. Then compare a simulated mean and variance against the theoretical ones.
If step 1 or step 2 has no closed form — the normal is the standard offender, since $\Phi^{-1}$ is not elementary — this recipe stalls, and one uses a transformation trick instead (Example 2.2 below) or a numerical approximation of $\Phi^{-1}$.
Example 2.1 — inverse-CDF sampling of an exponential.

Statement. Using only a stream of $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ numbers, generate draws from $ ext{Exp}(\lambda)$, and verify both directions of the theorem numerically at $\lambda=1.5$.

Step 1 — the CDF. For the exponential, $f(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ on $x\ge0$, so $$F(x)=\int_0^{x}\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt=\big[-e^{-\lambda t}\big]_0^{x}=1-e^{-\lambda x}, \qquad x\ge 0,$$ which is continuous and strictly increasing on $(0,\infty)$ — the theorem applies (G3 §1 derives this CDF).

Step 2 — invert. Set $u=1-e^{-\lambda x}$ and solve: $e^{-\lambda x}=1-u$, then take logarithms of both sides (legal since $1-u>0$ for $u<1$), giving $-\lambda x=\ln(1-u)$ and $$x=F^{-1}(u)=-\frac{\ln(1-u)}{\lambda}.$$ Sanity check: $u\to0^{+}$ gives $x\to0$, $u\to1^{-}$ gives $x\to\infty$, so $(0,1)$ is carried onto $(0,\infty)$, the correct support.

Step 3 — the sampler. Draw $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ and return $X=-\ln(1-U)/\lambda$. A common simplification: $1-U$ is also uniform on $(0,1)$ (it is the reflection of $U$ about $1/2$), so $X=-\ln(U)/\lambda$ is an equally valid sampler — the two differ draw by draw but not in distribution.

Step 4 — verify by simulation. With $\lambda=1.5$ and $N=2{,}000{,}000$ draws (u_lambda, u_N): the sample mean is $0.667013$ against the theoretical $1/\lambda=0.666667$, the sample variance is $0.445479$ against $1/\lambda^{2}=0.444444$, and the simulated tail probability $\mathbf{P}(X>2)$ is $0.049993$ against the exact $e^{-1.5\cdot2}=0.049787$. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance — the largest vertical gap between the empirical CDF and the true $ ext{Exp}(1.5)$ CDF — is $0.000614$, with p-value $0.438$ (u_ks_stat, u_ks_pvalue): no discrepancy at all. Fig. 2.1(a) is the histogram against the target density.

Step 5 — the forward direction, on the same example. Take a fresh exponential sample $Y\sim ext{Exp}(1.5)$ generated by a different mechanism and push it through its own CDF: $F(Y)=1-e^{-1.5Y}$. The result has sample mean $0.499949$ (target $1/2$) and sample variance $0.083353$ (target $1/12=0.083333$), with KS distance $0.000317$ to $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ and p-value $0.988$ (u_fwd_ks_stat). Fig. 2.1(b). Both directions check out.

Fig. 2.1 — Both directions of the universality theorem, at $\lambda=1.5$ ($200{,}000$ draws per panel; computes/rf_s2_figs.py). (a) Backward: $F^{-1}(U)=-\ln(1-U)/\lambda$ reproduces the exponential density from uniform input. Practice 2.1 — sampling a Cauchy, and a warning about the law of large numbers. The standard Cauchy density is $f(x)=\dfrac{1}{\pi(1+x^{2})}$ on the whole line. (a) Find $F$ and invert it to get a sampler. (b) Simulate and check the median and the interquartile range. (c) What does the sample mean do as $n$ grows, and why?
Solution

(a) Integrate: $\displaystyle F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{dt}{\pi(1+t^{2})} =\frac{1}{\pi}\big[\arctan t\big]_{-\infty}^{x}=\frac{1}{\pi}\Big(\arctan x+\frac{\pi}{2}\Big) =\frac12+\frac{\arctan x}{\pi},$ using $\int dt/(1+t^2)=\arctan t$ and $\arctan(-\infty)=-\pi/2$. Setting $u=F(x)$ and solving: $\arctan x=\pi\big(u-\tfrac12\big)$, so $$X=F^{-1}(U)=\tan\Big(\pi\Big(U-\frac12\Big)\Big).$$ As $U$ approaches $0$ or $1$ the tangent blows up in either direction, which is the heavy tail appearing.

(b) Over $N=2{,}000{,}000$ draws the sample median is $0.000454$ (true median $0$, by symmetry) and the sample interquartile range is $1.998035$ against the exact $F^{-1}(0.75)-F^{-1}(0.25)=\tan(\pi/4)-\tan(-\pi/4)=2$ (c_iqr_sim, c_iqr_true). The tail is genuinely fat: $\mathbf{P}(|X|>10)=2\big(\tfrac12- \arctan(10)/\pi\big)=0.063451$ exactly, simulated $0.063201$ — six percent of draws exceed $10$ in magnitude, where a standard normal would give about $1.5\times10^{-23}$.

(c) The running sample mean never settles. At $10^{4}$, $10^{5}$ and $2\times10^{6}$ draws it reads $0.6030$, $-0.1148$ and $-0.0897$ (c_runmean_1e4 and friends), wandering rather than converging. The reason is that $\mathbb{E}[|X|]=\int|x|f(x)\,dx$ diverges logarithmically, so the Cauchy has no mean — and the weak law of large numbers of G6 §1 assumes a finite mean and variance. Take this as the standing caution about simulation: a simulated average always prints a number, whether or not the quantity it estimates exists.

Practice 2.2. The logistic distribution has CDF $F(x)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$ on the whole line. Give the inverse-CDF sampler and evaluate it at $u=0.9$.
Solution

Solve $u=1/(1+e^{-x})$: invert both sides to get $1+e^{-x}=1/u$, so $e^{-x}=(1-u)/u$, and taking logarithms, $-x=\ln\frac{1-u}{u}$, i.e. $$X=F^{-1}(U)=\ln\frac{U}{1-U},$$ the log-odds, or logit, of $U$. At $u=0.9$ this is $\ln 9=2.197225$ (log_q): the $90$th percentile of the logistic sits at $2.197$. The function is increasing and carries $(0,1)$ onto $(-\infty,\infty)$, as required. (Reading the same identity backwards: applying the logistic CDF to a logistic random variable returns a uniform — the forward direction of the theorem.)

2.2 The two-dimensional change of variables: the Jacobian

G3 §4 transforms one random variable at a time. When two random variables are transformed into two new ones — $(X,Y)\mapsto(U,V)$ — the correction factor for the density is no longer a derivative but a determinant, for the same reason that a substitution in a double integral picks up a determinant: it is the local area-scaling factor of the map.

Theorem (change of variables in two dimensions). Let $X,Y$ have joint density $f_{X,Y}$, and let $(U,V)=g(X,Y)$ be a transformation that is one-to-one and continuously differentiable on the region where $f_{X,Y}>0$, with inverse $x=x(u,v)$, $y=y(u,v)$. Define the Jacobian matrix of the inverse map $$J=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u}&\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v}\\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u}&\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det J=\frac{\partial x}{\partial u}\frac{\partial y}{\partial v} -\frac{\partial x}{\partial v}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}.$$ Then the joint density of $(U,V)$ is $$\boxed{\,f_{U,V}(u,v)=f_{X,Y}\big(x(u,v),\,y(u,v)\big)\;\big|\det J\big|\,}$$ on the image of that region, and $0$ elsewhere. Equivalently, one may compute the Jacobian of the forward map and divide by the absolute value of its determinant, since the two matrices are inverses and $\det(J^{-1})=1/\det J$.
Interpretation — why a determinant. Probability is conserved: the chance of landing in a small rectangle around $(u,v)$ of area $du\,dv$ equals the chance of landing in its preimage around $(x,y)$, a small parallelogram. The linear map $J$ sends the rectangle to that parallelogram, and the absolute determinant of a $2\times2$ matrix is exactly the factor by which it scales area. So $f_{U,V}(u,v)\,du\,dv=f_{X,Y}(x,y)\cdot|\det J|\,du\,dv$. In one dimension the "parallelogram" is an interval and $|\det J|$ collapses to $|dh/dy|$ — which is precisely the formula of G3 §4, $f_Y(y)=f_X(h(y))\,|dh/dy|$. The absolute value plays the same role as there: it merges the increasing and decreasing cases, since a density cannot be negative. The same "probability is conserved" reading explains the Jacobian warnings in G6 §3 and G7 §2 about reparametrizing a posterior: a density transforms with this factor, and a likelihood does not.

The polar case, which the notes already use twice. Take $u=r$, $v=\theta$ with the inverse map $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ for $r\ge0$ and $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$. Differentiating each coordinate with respect to each of $r$ and $\theta$, $$J=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det J=\cos\theta\cdot r\cos\theta-(-r\sin\theta)\cdot\sin\theta =r\big(\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta\big)=r,$$ using the Pythagorean identity in the last step. Hence $$f_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)=f_{X,Y}(r\cos\theta,\,r\sin\theta)\cdot r .$$ That factor $r$ is the whole content of two computations already in these notes: the polar evaluation of the Gaussian integral in G3 §1 ("the extra factor $r$ is what rescues the computation") and the polar decomposition of a standard normal pair in G4 §1 ("the extra factor $r$ is the Jacobian and is the whole content of the computation"). Both are correct; neither names the general rule, because 6.041 never states it. It is the theorem above with $\det J=r$.

Example 2.2 (the Box–Muller transform) — generating normals from uniforms.

Statement. The normal CDF $\Phi$ has no elementary inverse, so the recipe of §2.1 cannot produce normal draws directly. Use the polar Jacobian to build a pair of independent standard normals out of a pair of independent uniforms.

Step 1 — the joint density in polar form. Let $X,Y$ be independent standard normals. By independence their joint density is the product of the marginals (G3 §2), $$f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-y^{2}/2} =\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(x^{2}+y^{2})/2}.$$ Substituting $x^{2}+y^{2}=r^{2}$ and multiplying by the Jacobian $r$, $$f_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-r^{2}/2}\cdot r =\underbrace{\Big(r\,e^{-r^{2}/2}\Big)}_{\text{depends on }r\text{ only}}\cdot \underbrace{\frac{1}{2\pi}}_{\text{depends on }\theta\text{ only}},\qquad r\ge0,\ \theta\in[0,2\pi).$$

Step 2 — read off two facts. The joint density factors into a function of $r$ times a function of $\theta$ on a rectangular region, which by the criterion of G3 §2 means $R$ and $\Theta$ are independent. Moreover $\Theta$ is uniform on $[0,2\pi)$ (its factor is constant), and $R$ has the Rayleigh density $f_R(r)=re^{-r^{2}/2}$ — which integrates to $1.000000$ over $[0,\infty)$ (ray_norm, by numerical quadrature), confirming the bookkeeping. This is the result G4 §1 obtains; here it is one line of the general rule.

Step 3 — invert the radial CDF. $F_R(r)=\int_0^{r}se^{-s^{2}/2}ds=\big[-e^{-s^{2}/2} \big]_0^{r}=1-e^{-r^{2}/2}$ (substituting $w=s^2/2$, $dw=s\,ds$). Setting $u=1-e^{-r^{2}/2}$ and solving as in Example 2.1: $r^{2}=-2\ln(1-u)$, so $R=\sqrt{-2\ln(1-U_1)}$.

Step 4 — the sampler. Draw independent $U_1,U_2\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ and return $$X=\sqrt{-2\ln(1-U_1)}\,\cos(2\pi U_2),\qquad Y=\sqrt{-2\ln(1-U_1)}\,\sin(2\pi U_2),$$ a pair of independent standard normals — two for the price of two uniforms.

Step 5 — verify. Over $M=1{,}000{,}000$ pairs: sample means $-0.001677$ and $-0.000730$ (true $0$), sample variances $1.001003$ and $0.998087$ (true $1$), and sample correlation $-0.000455$ (true $0$, as they are independent). The radius has sample mean $1.253020$ against the exact Rayleigh mean $\sqrt{\pi/2}=1.253314$ (ray_mean_true). The KS distance of $X$ to $N(0,1)$ is $0.001484$; the $5\%$ critical distance at this sample size is $1.36/\sqrt{M}=0.001360$ (bm_ks_crit5), so the test lands marginally on the reject side, with p-value $0.024$ — an ordinary one-in-forty fluctuation of a test that is razor-sharp at a million draws, not evidence of a defect; the moments above are exact to three decimals.

Example 2.3 — splitting a sum from its share: $S=X+Y$ and $V=X/(X+Y)$.

Statement. $X$ and $Y$ are independent $ ext{Exp}(\lambda)$ — say two service times. Find the joint density of the total $S=X+Y$ and the first job's share $V=X/(X+Y)$.

Step 1 — invert the map. From $V=X/S$ we get $x=sv$, and then $y=s-x=s(1-v)$. The map is one-to-one from $\{x>0,\ y>0\}$ onto $\{s>0,\ 0<v<1\}$: the total is any positive number and the share is any fraction.

Step 2 — the Jacobian. Differentiate the inverse map: $$J=\begin{pmatrix}\partial x/\partial s&\partial x/\partial v\\ \partial y/\partial s&\partial y/\partial v\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}v&s\\ 1-v&-s\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det J=v(-s)-s(1-v)=-sv-s+sv=-s,$$ so $|\det J|=s$ (positive on the region of interest).

Step 3 — substitute. By independence, $f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\lambda e^{-\lambda y}=\lambda^{2}e^{-\lambda(x+y)}=\lambda^{2}e^{-\lambda s}$, since $x+y=s$ identically. Therefore $$f_{S,V}(s,v)=\lambda^{2}e^{-\lambda s}\cdot s =\underbrace{\big(\lambda^{2}s\,e^{-\lambda s}\big)}_{\text{Erlang order }2}\cdot \underbrace{1}_{\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)},\qquad s>0,\ 0<v<1.$$

Step 4 — read off the conclusion. The density factors over a rectangular region, so $S$ and $V$ are independent; $S$ is Erlang of order $2$ with parameter $\lambda$ — the sum of two independent exponentials, exactly as G4 §3 has it — and $V$ is uniform on $(0,1)$. The last part is the striking one: the share of the total taken by the first job is uniform, and tells you nothing about how long the total was.

Step 5 — verify. At $\lambda=1$ over $1{,}000{,}000$ pairs: $S$ has sample mean $2.001758$ (true $2/\lambda=2$) and sample variance $2.005691$ (true $2/\lambda^{2}=2$), with KS distance $0.000798$ to the Erlang; $V$ has sample mean $0.499550$ and sample variance $0.083351$ (true $1/12$), with KS distance $0.001376$ to $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$; and the sample correlation between $S$ and $V$ is $+0.000083$, consistent with the independence just proved (gv_corr).

Gotcha — the three ways this rule is misapplied. (i) One-to-one is not optional. If two different $(x,y)$ map to the same $(u,v)$, the contributions must be added, one $|\det J|$ term per branch — the two-dimensional version of the $Y=X^{2}$ trap in G3 §4. (ii) The support must be transformed too. The formula gives the density only on the image region; forgetting to work out that region is the single most common error, and it is what makes Example 2.3 land on $0<v<1$ rather than on all of $\mathbb{R}$. (iii) Right matrix, right direction. $|\det J|$ above is that of the map from the new variables to the old; using the forward map instead means dividing rather than multiplying. Check by integrating the answer to $1$, or by testing a case you already know. Finally, when only one new variable is wanted — the density of $X+Y$, say — the honest routes are convolution (G3 §4) or this theorem with an auxiliary second variable (take $U=X+Y$, $V=X$, then integrate $V$ out).
Practice 2.3. $X,Y$ are independent standard normals. Find the joint density of $U=X+Y$ and $V=X-Y$, and say whether they are independent.
Solution

Invert: adding and subtracting the two equations gives $x=(u+v)/2$ and $y=(u-v)/2$. The map is one-to-one from the plane onto the plane.

Jacobian: $$J=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/2\\ 1/2&-1/2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det J=\Big(\frac12\Big)\Big(-\frac12\Big)-\Big(\frac12\Big)\Big(\frac12\Big)=-\frac12, \qquad|\det J|=\frac12 .$$ The map is a rotation by $45^{\circ}$ combined with a stretch by $\sqrt2$, so the inverse shrinks area by $1/2$ — the determinant agrees with the picture.

Substitute: $x^{2}+y^{2}=\dfrac{(u+v)^{2}+(u-v)^{2}}{4}=\dfrac{2u^{2}+2v^{2}}{4} =\dfrac{u^{2}+v^{2}}{2}$ (the cross terms cancel), so $$f_{U,V}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(u^{2}+v^{2})/4}\cdot\frac12 =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\cdot2}}e^{-u^{2}/(2\cdot2)}\cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\cdot2}}e^{-v^{2}/(2\cdot2)} .$$ This is the product of two $N(0,2)$ densities, so $U$ and $V$ are independent, each normal with mean $0$ and variance $2$. Check: $\operatorname{var}(X\pm Y)=1+1=2$ by independence (G3 §4), and $\operatorname{cov}(U,V)=\operatorname{var}(X)-\operatorname{var}(Y)=0$ — but zero covariance alone would not prove independence; the factored density does. Simulation over $10^{6}$ pairs: variances $1.997709$ and $2.002419$, correlation $-0.000306$ (rot_var_sim, rot_corr).

2.3 Order statistics

Definition (order statistics). Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be random variables on the same experiment. Their order statistics $X_{(1)}\le X_{(2)}\le\cdots\le X_{(n)}$ are the same $n$ numbers rearranged into increasing order: $X_{(1)}=\min_i X_i$, $X_{(n)}=\max_i X_i$, and $X_{(k)}$ is the $k$-th smallest. Each $X_{(k)}$ is itself a random variable — a function of the outcome, like any other. When $n$ is odd, $X_{((n+1)/2)}$ is the sample median, and $X_{(n)}-X_{(1)}$ is the sample range.
Gotcha. Even when $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are i.i.d., the order statistics are never independent and never identically distributed: $X_{(1)}\le X_{(3)}$ holds with probability $1$, which is about as dependent as two random variables get. Everything below is about the marginal distribution of a single $X_{(k)}$. Also, for continuous $X_i$ ties have probability zero, so the inequalities are strict almost surely and the sorted list is unambiguous — the formulas below all assume this.

Assume from here that $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are i.i.d. continuous with common CDF $F$ and density $f$.

Property (the CDF of the $k$-th order statistic). For every $x$, $$F_{X_{(k)}}(x)=\mathbf{P}\big(X_{(k)}\le x\big) =\sum_{j=k}^{n}\binom{n}{j}F(x)^{j}\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n-j}.$$

Derivation. Fix $x$ and count how many of the samples fall at or below it: $N(x)=\#\{i:X_i\le x\}$. Each sample independently lands at or below $x$ with the same probability $F(x)$, so $N(x)$ is the count of successes in $n$ independent identical trials — that is, $N(x)\sim\text{Binomial}\big(n,F(x)\big)$, by the indicator decomposition of G2 §4. Now the key translation, and the only idea in the whole subsection: $$\{X_{(k)}\le x\}=\{N(x)\ge k\},$$ in words, "the $k$-th smallest is at or below $x$" says exactly the same thing as "at least $k$ of the samples are at or below $x$". Taking probabilities and summing the binomial PMF over $j=k,\dots,n$ gives the stated formula. $\blacksquare$

Two special cases are already in the notes. For $k=n$ only the $j=n$ term survives and $F_{X_{(n)}}(x)=F(x)^{n}$; for $k=1$ the complementary form is cleaner, $$\mathbf{P}\big(X_{(1)}>x\big)=\mathbf{P}\big(N(x)=0\big)=\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_{X_{(1)}}(x)=1-\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n}.$$ Those are exactly the max and min rules of the recipe in G3 §1 ("multiply the CDFs" and "multiply the tails"), which that section derives directly and applies to exponentials in its Examples 1.7 and 1.8. The theorem above says they are the two ends of one family.

Property (the density of the $k$-th order statistic). If the $X_i$ are i.i.d. with density $f$ and CDF $F$, then $$f_{X_{(k)}}(x)=\frac{n!}{(k-1)!\,(n-k)!}\;F(x)^{k-1}\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n-k}f(x).$$

Derivation (the direct route). For $X_{(k)}$ to lie in the tiny interval $[x,x+\delta]$, three things must happen: one of the samples lands in that interval, exactly $k-1$ of the others land below $x$, and the remaining $n-k$ land above. Count the ways: $n$ choices for which sample sits in the interval, then $\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ choices of which of the remaining $n-1$ go below. Multiply by the probabilities, which are independent across samples: $f(x)\delta$ for the one in the interval, $F(x)^{k-1}$ for those below, $\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n-k}$ for those above. So $$f_{X_{(k)}}(x)\,\delta\approx n\binom{n-1}{k-1}F(x)^{k-1}\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n-k}f(x)\,\delta,$$ and $n\binom{n-1}{k-1}=n\cdot\frac{(n-1)!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!}=\frac{n!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!}$. Dividing by $\delta$ and letting $\delta\to0$ makes it exact. (Differentiating the CDF sum term by term gives the same answer, after a telescoping cancellation.) $\blacksquare$

Interpretation. Read the three factors as a story: $f(x)$ places one observation at $x$, $F(x)^{k-1}$ demands that $k-1$ observations fell to its left, $\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n-k}$ that $n-k$ fell to its right, and the multinomial coefficient counts which observations played which role. Every order-statistic formula in this subsection is that one sentence with different bookkeeping.

The uniform case, and the Beta family

Now specialize to $U_1,\dots,U_n$ i.i.d. $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$, where $F(x)=x$ and $f(x)=1$ on $(0,1)$. The density formula collapses to $$f_{U_{(j)}}(x)=\frac{n!}{(j-1)!\,(n-j)!}\,x^{j-1}(1-x)^{n-j},\qquad 0<x<1,$$ which is the density of a Beta$(j,\,n-j+1)$ random variable: the Beta$(a,b)$ density is $x^{a-1}(1-x)^{b-1}/B(a,b)$, and with $a=j$, $b=n-j+1$ the normalizing constant is $B(a,b)=\frac{(a-1)!(b-1)!}{(a+b-1)!}=\frac{(j-1)!(n-j)!}{n!}$, matching the coefficient exactly. So $$U_{(j)}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(j,\ n-j+1),\qquad \mathbb{E}\big[U_{(j)}\big]=\frac{j}{n+1},\qquad \operatorname{var}\big(U_{(j)}\big)=\frac{j\,(n-j+1)}{(n+1)^{2}(n+2)},$$ using the standard Beta moments $\mathbb{E}=\frac{a}{a+b}$ and $\operatorname{var}=\frac{ab}{(a+b)^{2}(a+b+1)}$ with $a+b=n+1$.

Intuition — the mean without any algebra. The $n$ sample points cut the interval $(0,1)$ into $n+1$ gaps. By symmetry all $n+1$ gaps have the same expected length, and they sum to $1$, so each has expected length $1/(n+1)$. The $j$-th smallest point sits at the end of the $j$-th gap, so $\mathbb{E}[U_{(j)}]=j/(n+1)$ — the sample points are, on average, evenly spaced, but pushed inward: for $n=5$ the largest sits at $5/6=0.833$, not at $1$. This inward bias is the whole content of the estimator bias in G7 §2. The Beta family itself is not new to these notes: G3 §3 meets it as the posterior for a Bernoulli parameter under a Beta prior. The same functional form arises here for a completely different reason, which is a good reminder that a density is a shape, not a story.

Example 2.4 — a tail probability of the sample median, three ways.

Statement. Five points are dropped independently and uniformly on $(0,1)$. What is the probability that the middle one exceeds $0.7$?

Route 1 — the Beta law. $U_{(3)}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(3,3)$ with density $30x^{2}(1-x)^{2}$ (the coefficient is $\frac{5!}{2!\,2!}=\frac{120}{4}=30$; it integrates to $1.000000$, os_f3_norm). Then $$\mathbf{P}\big(U_{(3)}>0.7\big)=\int_{0.7}^{1}30x^{2}(1-x)^{2}\,dx=0.163080$$ (os_p3_beta_tail).

Route 2 — the counting translation, which needs no calculus. By the identity $\{X_{(k)}\le x\}=\{N(x)\ge k\}$, the complement is $\{U_{(3)}>0.7\}=\{N(0.7)\le2\}$: fewer than three of the five points fall below $0.7$. Since $N(0.7)\sim\text{Binomial}(5,0.7)$, $$\mathbf{P}(N\le2)=\sum_{i=0}^{2}\binom{5}{i}(0.7)^{i}(0.3)^{5-i} =0.002430+0.028350+0.132300=0.163080,$$ term by term for $i=0,1,2$ (os_p3_terms) — the same number to six decimals, obtained by arithmetic instead of integration. This equivalence between a Beta tail and a binomial tail is worth remembering; it is the reason the two families keep appearing together.

Route 3 — simulation. Sorting $400{,}000$ samples of five uniforms and counting gives $0.162477$ (os_p3_sim). The standard error of such a fraction is $\sqrt{0.1631\cdot0.8369/400000}\approx0.00058$, so the gap of $0.0006$ from the exact value is about one standard error — a clean agreement, not a discrepancy.

Interpretation. Roughly one time in six the median of five uniform points lands in the top $30\%$ of the interval. Compare a single draw, which exceeds $0.7$ with probability $0.3$: taking a median of five has pulled the distribution sharply toward the center, which is the whole reason sample medians are used.

Exponential order statistics, and the two cross-checks

For $X_1,\dots,X_n$ i.i.d. $ ext{Exp}(\lambda)$ there is a memoryless shortcut that avoids the density formula entirely. The minimum is the first of $n$ competing exponential clocks, so $X_{(1)}\sim ext{Exp}(n\lambda)$ — "rates add", exactly as G3 §1 derives it — with mean $1/(n\lambda)$. By the memorylessness of the exponential (G4 §3), once the first has fired the remaining $n-1$ clocks start fresh, so the additional wait $X_{(2)}-X_{(1)}$ is $ ext{Exp}\big((n-1)\lambda\big)$, independent of the first, and so on. Summing the gaps, $$\mathbb{E}\big[X_{(k)}\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\lambda\,(n-i+1)} =\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\frac1n+\frac{1}{n-1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n-k+1}\right).$$ At $\lambda=1$, $n=5$ this gives $0.200000,\ 0.450000,\ 0.783333,\ 1.283333,\ 2.283333$ against simulated $0.200014,\ 0.450311,\ 0.783701,\ 1.284314,\ 2.284697$ (eo_true, eo_sim). Note the last entry: $\mathbb{E}[X_{(n)}]=\frac1\lambda\big(1+\frac12+\cdots+ \frac1n\big)$, the harmonic number $H_5=2.283333$ — which is exactly the "harmonic pattern" G3 §1 finds for the maximum of three exponentials in its Example 1.7 and its Practice 1.14.

The second cross-check runs into G7 §2. There, $n=5$ observations are drawn uniformly on $[0,\theta]$ and the maximum likelihood estimate of $\theta$ is $\hat\Theta=\max_i X_i=X_{(5)}$. Scaling the uniform result above, $X_{(n)}/\theta\sim\mathrm{Beta}(n,1)$, so $$\mathbb{E}\big[\hat\Theta\big]=\theta\cdot\frac{n}{n+1}=8\cdot\frac56=6.666667$$ (xcheck_max_mean) — the value $6.667$ that G7 §2 obtains, and the source of the bias $-\theta/(n+1)$ it reports. The order-statistic machinery reproduces it in one line, and says at once what the whole distribution of the estimator is, not just its mean.

Practice 2.4. $U_1,\dots,U_n$ are i.i.d. $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$. Find the density and mean of the minimum, two ways.
Solution

Way 1 — the general density with $k=1$. With $F(x)=x$, $f(x)=1$, $$f_{U_{(1)}}(x)=\frac{n!}{0!\,(n-1)!}\,x^{0}(1-x)^{n-1}=n(1-x)^{n-1},\qquad 0<x<1,$$ which is Beta$(1,n)$. Its mean is $\frac{1}{1+n}$ by the Beta formula, or directly: $\int_0^1 x\,n(1-x)^{n-1}dx=\frac{1}{n+1}$ (substitute $w=1-x$ and integrate $n\int_0^1(1-w)w^{n-1}dw=n\big(\frac1n-\frac{1}{n+1}\big)=\frac{1}{n+1}$).

Way 2 — the tail rule of G3 §1. $\mathbf{P}(U_{(1)}>x)=\prod_{i}\mathbf{P}(U_i>x) =(1-x)^{n}$, so $F_{U_{(1)}}(x)=1-(1-x)^{n}$; differentiating gives $n(1-x)^{n-1}$, the same density. And by the tail formula for a nonnegative random variable, $\mathbb{E}[U_{(1)}]=\int_0^1(1-x)^{n}dx=\frac{1}{n+1}$, agreeing. At $n=5$: $1/6=0.166667$ against the simulated $0.166671$ in the table above. It matches the gap picture — the smallest point sits at the end of the first of $n+1$ equal average gaps.

Practice 2.5. Five customers arrive; their service times are i.i.d. $ ext{Exp}(1)$. (a) What is the expected time until the third service finishes? (b) Which gap between consecutive completions is longest on average, and why?
Solution

(a) The third completion is $X_{(3)}$, and by the gap decomposition $$\mathbb{E}\big[X_{(3)}\big]=\frac15+\frac14+\frac13=0.200000+0.250000+0.333333=0.783333,$$ matching the simulated $0.783701$ (eo_true, eo_sim). Reading the terms: five clocks race to the first completion (mean wait $1/5$), then four race to the second (mean $1/4$), then three to the third (mean $1/3$).

(b) The last one. The gaps have means $\frac{1}{5},\frac14,\frac13,\frac12,1$ — increasing, because each completion removes one competitor and slows the aggregate rate. The final gap, waiting for the single straggler, averages $1$, five times the first gap; the whole batch takes on average $H_5=2.283333$, more than twice the mean of any single service. This is the standard reason a batch job finishes so much later than its average task: you wait for the maximum, not the mean.

§2 cheatsheet — transformations, uniformity, order statistics (all beyond the 6.041 syllabus)
  • Universality of the uniform. For $F$ continuous and strictly increasing: $F(X)\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$, and $F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$. Both are one line of the CDF method (G3 §4). Fails forward for discrete $X$; the generalized inverse $F^{-1}(u)=\min\{x:F(x)\ge u\}$ keeps the backward direction alive for any CDF at all.
  • Inverse-CDF sampling. $X=F^{-1}(U)$. Exponential: $X=-\ln(1-U)/\lambda$ (verified at $\lambda=1.5$: simulated mean $0.667013$ vs $0.666667$, KS distance $0.000614$). Cauchy: $\tan\big(\pi(U-\tfrac12)\big)$. Logistic: $\ln\frac{U}{1-U}$. Normal: no elementary $\Phi^{-1}$, so use Box–Muller instead.
  • 2-D change of variables. $f_{U,V}(u,v)=f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,|\det J|$ with $J$ the Jacobian of the inverse map, $\det J=x_uy_v-x_vy_u$. Requires one-to-one; the support must be transformed too. One dimension gives back $f_Y(y)=f_X(h(y))|dh/dy|$ of G3 §4.
  • Polar. $\det J=r$, so $f_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)=f_{X,Y}(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\,r$ — the general rule behind "the extra factor $r$" of G3 §1 and G4 §1. For a standard normal pair this gives $R$ Rayleigh, $\Theta$ uniform, independent (Box–Muller). For two i.i.d. exponentials, $(X+Y,\ X/(X+Y))$ is Erlang-$2$ times $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$, independent.
  • Order statistics (i.i.d. continuous, CDF $F$): $\{X_{(k)}\le x\}=\{N(x)\ge k\}$ with $N(x)\sim\text{Bin}(n,F(x))$, hence $F_{X_{(k)}}(x)=\sum_{j\ge k}\binom nj F^j(1-F)^{n-j}$ and $f_{X_{(k)}}(x)=\frac{n!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!}F^{k-1}(1-F)^{n-k}f$. $k=n$ gives $F^n$ and $k=1$ gives $1-(1-F)^n$: the max/min rules of G3 §1.
  • Uniform sample. $U_{(j)}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(j,n-j+1)$, mean $j/(n+1)$, variance $\frac{j(n-j+1)}{(n+1)^2(n+2)}$; the $n$ points cut $(0,1)$ into $n+1$ gaps of equal average length. Range mean $(n-1)/(n+1)$. At $n=5$, $\mathbf{P}(U_{(3)}>0.7)=0.163080$ by Beta tail, binomial tail, or simulation ($0.162477$).
  • Exponential sample. $X_{(1)}\sim ext{Exp}(n\lambda)$ and the gaps are independent exponentials of rates $n\lambda,(n-1)\lambda,\dots$, so $\mathbb{E}[X_{(k)}]=\frac1\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{n-i+1}$; the max has mean $H_n/\lambda$ ($H_5=2.283333$), the harmonic pattern of G3 §1. Cross-check into G7 §2: $\mathbb{E}[\max]=\theta\frac{n}{n+1}=6.666667$ at $n=5$, $\theta=8$.
