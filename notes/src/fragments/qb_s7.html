§7   Classical statistical inference

Sixteen problems on the classical half of the course: treating the unknown as a fixed constant and letting the data be the only random thing. They drill the four skills G7 builds — judging a proposed estimator by bias, consistency and mean squared error (including the case where a deliberately biased estimator wins); building a confidence interval, reading it correctly, and using the conservative $\tfrac14$ bound when the variance is unknown; maximizing a likelihood, including the corner solution where setting a derivative to zero is exactly the wrong move, and pinning down what "ML $=$ MAP under a flat prior" does and does not buy you; running least squares from the closed forms and refusing the three claims a fitted line never makes; and calibrating a likelihood ratio test to a target $\alpha$, paying the resulting $\beta$, and saying honestly what "not rejected" means. Every number below is recomputed in computes/qb_s7.py; the subtler ones are cross-checked by simulation.

Q135. ★ · Bias, consistency, MSE · G7 §1.1–1.2

A thermometer returns i.i.d. readings $X_1,\dots,X_n$ with unknown mean $\theta$ and known standard deviation $\sigma=2$ degrees. Let $M_n=(X_1+\cdots+X_n)/n$ be the sample mean. Two engineers propose calibrated estimators: $$\hat A_n = M_n + \frac{3}{n},\qquad\qquad \hat B_n = M_n + 3 .$$ Take $n=25$. (a) Find the bias of each. (b) Find the MSE of each. (c) Which, if either, is unbiased? asymptotically unbiased? consistent? (d) Recompute the MSE of $\hat A_n$ at $n=100$ and say what drives the improvement.

Solution

Setup. Both estimators are the sample mean plus a deterministic constant, so adding the constant shifts the mean but leaves the variance alone. From G7 §1.3, $\mathbb{E}[M_n]=\theta$ and $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n$. With $\sigma=2$, $\sigma^2=4$, and at $n=25$ $$\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\frac{4}{25}=0.16 .$$ Both $\hat A_n$ and $\hat B_n$ have this same variance, since $\operatorname{var}(M_n+c)=\operatorname{var}(M_n)$ for a constant $c$.

Step 1 — biases. By linearity of expectation, $$\mathbb{E}[\hat A_n]=\theta+\frac3n=\theta+\frac{3}{25}=\theta+0.12, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\hat B_n]=\theta+3 .$$ So $\mathrm{bias}(\hat A_{25})=0.12$ and $\mathrm{bias}(\hat B_{25})=3$.

Step 2 — MSEs. Use the decomposition of G7 §1.2, $\mathrm{MSE}=\mathrm{bias}^2+\operatorname{var}$: $$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat A_{25}) = (0.12)^2+0.16 = 0.0144+0.16 = 0.1744,$$ $$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat B_{25}) = 3^2+0.16 = 9+0.16 = 9.16 .$$ $\hat B_{25}$ is worse by a factor $9.16/0.1744 = 52.5229$.

Step 3 — the three properties. Neither is unbiased at finite $n$: both biases are nonzero. $\hat A_n$ is asymptotically unbiased, since $3/n\to0$; $\hat B_n$ is not, since its bias stays at $3$ forever. For consistency we need $\hat\Theta_n\to\theta$ in probability. Write $\hat A_n-\theta=(M_n-\theta)+3/n$: the first term $\to0$ in probability by the weak law (G6 §1), the second is a deterministic sequence $\to0$, so $\hat A_n$ is consistent. For $\hat B_n$, $\hat B_n-\theta=(M_n-\theta)+3\to3\neq0$, so $\hat B_n$ converges in probability to $\theta+3$ — the wrong number. It is not consistent, and no amount of data fixes it.

Step 4 — $n=100$. Bias $=3/100=0.03$, variance $=4/100=0.04$, so $$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat A_{100})=0.03^2+0.04=0.0009+0.04=0.0409 .$$ Both parts shrank, but at different rates: the variance falls like $1/n$ while the squared bias falls like $1/n^2$. At $n=25$ the bias contributed $0.0144/0.1744=8.3\%$ of the MSE; at $n=100$ it contributes $0.0009/0.0409=2.2\%$. Asymptotically the bias term is negligible — which is exactly why a $O(1/n)$ bias is a venial sin and a constant bias is a mortal one.

Answer. $\;$(a) $0.12$ and $3$. (b) $\mathrm{MSE}(\hat A_{25})=0.1744$, $\mathrm{MSE}(\hat B_{25})=9.16$. (c) Neither unbiased; only $\hat A_n$ is asymptotically unbiased and consistent. (d) $\mathrm{MSE}(\hat A_{100})=0.0409$; the variance drops like $1/n$, the squared bias like $1/n^2$.

Trap. "$\hat B_n$ has small variance, so it is a good estimator." Variance alone is not quality: the constant estimator $\hat\Theta_n\equiv0$ has variance $0$ and is useless. MSE is the honest scorecard because it charges you for bias too.

Q136. ★★ · Weighted estimators and efficiency · G7 §1.2–1.3

A sensor logs i.i.d. readings $X_1,\dots,X_n$ with unknown mean $\theta$ and known variance $v$. A colleague argues that later readings are "fresher" and proposes the linearly weighted estimator $$\hat\Theta_n=\frac{2}{n(n+1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n} i\,X_i .$$ (a) Show it is unbiased for every $n$. (b) Derive $\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_n)$ in closed form. (c) With $n=10$ and $v=9$, compare its variance and MSE with those of the sample mean $M_n$. (d) Is $\hat\Theta_n$ consistent? (You may use $\sum_{i=1}^{n} i = n(n+1)/2$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{n} i^2 = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6$.)

Solution

Setup. Write $\hat\Theta_n=\sum_i w_i X_i$ with weights $w_i = \dfrac{2i}{n(n+1)}$. Every estimator of this "linear in the data" form is handled by two facts: expectation is linear, and variances of independent terms add with the coefficients squared.

Step 1 — unbiasedness. The weights sum to one: $$\sum_{i=1}^n w_i=\frac{2}{n(n+1)}\sum_{i=1}^n i =\frac{2}{n(n+1)}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2}=1 .$$ Hence, by linearity of expectation and $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=\theta$ for every $i$, $$\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]=\sum_i w_i\,\mathbb{E}[X_i]=\theta\sum_i w_i=\theta .$$ Unbiased for every $n$ and every $\theta$. (Numerically at $n=10$ the weights are $2/110,4/110,\dots,20/110$, summing to $110/110=1$.)

Step 2 — variance. The $X_i$ are independent, so $$\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_n)=\sum_i w_i^2\operatorname{var}(X_i) = v\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{4i^2}{n^2(n+1)^2} = \frac{4v}{n^2(n+1)^2}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6} = \frac{2(2n+1)}{3n(n+1)}\,v .$$

Step 3 — the comparison at $n=10$, $v=9$. The closed form gives $$\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_{10})=\frac{2(21)}{3(10)(11)}v=\frac{42}{330}v =0.1272727\,v = 0.1272727\times 9 = 1.1454545 .$$ (Direct check: $\sum_{i=1}^{10} i^2=385$, and $4(385)/(10\cdot11)^2=1540/12100=0.1272727$. A $300{,}000$-run simulation with $\theta=5$, $v=9$ gives sample mean $4.99980$ and sample variance $1.14867$ — matching both claims.) For the sample mean, $$\operatorname{var}(M_{10})=\frac{v}{n}=\frac{9}{10}=0.9 .$$ Both estimators are unbiased, so MSE $=$ variance for both, and the ratio is $$\frac{0.1272727}{0.1}=1.2727 .$$ The weighted estimator has $27.3\%$ more MSE. Getting the same precision from it would need about $27\%$ more data.

Step 4 — consistency. $\hat\Theta_n$ is unbiased and $\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_n)=\frac{2(2n+1)}{3n(n+1)}v\to0$ as $n\to\infty$ (the numerator grows like $n$, the denominator like $n^2$; at $n=100$ the coefficient is already $0.0132673$). By the mean-squared-error criterion for consistency (G7 §1.1: $\mathrm{MSE}\to0$ implies convergence in probability, via Chebyshev), $\hat\Theta_n$ is consistent.

Answer. $\;$Unbiased for all $n$; $\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_n)=\dfrac{2(2n+1)}{3n(n+1)}v$; at $n=10,v=9$ its variance is $1.1454545$ against $0.9$ for $M_{10}$, i.e. $1.2727$ times larger; it is consistent.

Trap. "Unbiased and consistent, so it is as good as the sample mean." Unbiasedness is a statement about the center of the sampling distribution, not its width. Among all unbiased linear estimators $\sum w_iX_i$ with $\sum w_i=1$, the variance $v\sum w_i^2$ is minimized by the equal weights $w_i=1/n$ — so any nonconstant weighting strictly loses. "Fresher data" is only a reason to reweight if the data are not identically distributed.

Q137. ★★★ · When a biased estimator wins · G7 §1.2

Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be i.i.d. uniform on $[0,\theta]$ with $\theta>0$ unknown, and let $M=\max\{X_1,\dots,X_n\}$. You may use, without rederiving them, $$\mathbb{E}[M]=\frac{n}{n+1}\theta,\qquad \mathbb{E}[M^2]=\frac{n}{n+2}\theta^2 .$$ Consider the whole family of rescalings $\hat\Theta(c)=cM$, $c>0$. (a) Write $\mathrm{MSE}(c)$ and minimize it over $c$; show the minimizer is $c^\star=\frac{n+2}{n+1}$, which is not the unbiasing constant. (b) At $n=8$, evaluate the MSE (in units of $\theta^2$) of the raw ML estimator $M$, of the unbiased estimator, and of $\hat\Theta(c^\star)$. (c) State precisely what this does and does not say about unbiasedness as a design goal.

Solution

Setup. Everything is a rescaling of one random variable $M$, so the MSE is a quadratic in $c$ and can be minimized by one derivative. At $n=8$ the two moments are $$\mathbb{E}[M]=\tfrac{8}{9}\theta=0.8888889\,\theta,\qquad \mathbb{E}[M^2]=\tfrac{8}{10}\theta^2=0.8\,\theta^2,$$ so $\operatorname{var}(M)=0.8\theta^2-(0.8888889\theta)^2=0.8\theta^2-0.7901235\theta^2 =0.0098765\,\theta^2 .$

Step 1 — the MSE as a quadratic in $c$. By definition $\mathrm{MSE}(c)=\mathbb{E}[(cM-\theta)^2]$. Expand the square and use linearity: $$\mathrm{MSE}(c)=c^2\,\mathbb{E}[M^2]-2c\theta\,\mathbb{E}[M]+\theta^2 .$$ This is an upward parabola in $c$ (the coefficient $\mathbb{E}[M^2]>0$), so it has a unique minimum.

Step 2 — minimize. Differentiate and set to zero: $$\frac{d}{dc}\mathrm{MSE}(c)=2c\,\mathbb{E}[M^2]-2\theta\,\mathbb{E}[M]=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; c^\star=\frac{\theta\,\mathbb{E}[M]}{\mathbb{E}[M^2]} =\frac{\theta\cdot\frac{n}{n+1}\theta}{\frac{n}{n+2}\theta^2} =\frac{n+2}{n+1}.$$ The $\theta$'s cancel completely — which is the only reason this constant is usable in practice. The unbiasing constant is different: $\mathbb{E}[cM]=\theta$ requires $c_{\text{unb}}=\frac{n+1}{n}$. At $n=8$, $$c^\star=\tfrac{10}{9}=1.1111111,\qquad c_{\text{unb}}=\tfrac98=1.125 .$$ So the MSE-optimal rescaling deliberately undershoots the unbiasing one.

Step 3 — the three MSEs at $n=8$ (units of $\theta^2$).

Raw ML estimator $M$ ($c=1$). Substituting $c=1$: $\mathrm{MSE}(1)=0.8-2(0.8888889)+1=0.0222222$, which matches the closed form $\frac{2}{(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac{2}{9\cdot 10}=0.0222222 .$

Unbiased ($c=1.125$). Bias is zero, so MSE is pure variance: $$\mathrm{MSE}=c_{\text{unb}}^2\operatorname{var}(M)=(1.125)^2(0.0098765) =1.265625\times0.0098765=0.0125 .$$

Optimal ($c=c^\star$). Substituting $c^\star=\mathbb{E}[M]/\mathbb{E}[M^2]$ into Step 1 collapses the expression: $$\mathrm{MSE}(c^\star)=\theta^2\left(1-\frac{\mathbb{E}[M]^2}{\mathbb{E}[M^2]}\right) =\theta^2\left(1-\frac{0.7901235}{0.8}\right)=\theta^2(1-0.9876543)=0.0123457\,\theta^2 .$$

Ranking: $0.0123457 < 0.0125 < 0.0222222$. The biased $c^\star$ beats the unbiased estimator by a factor $0.9876543$ — a $1.2346\%$ MSE saving — and both beat the raw ML estimator, whose MSE is $1/0.5625=1.78$ times the unbiased one. (A $400{,}000$-run simulation at $\theta=3$ returns $0.022148$, $0.012418$ and $0.012264$ for the three, and $\mathbb{E}[M]/\theta=0.888932$, confirming all of it.)

Step 4 — the moral, stated carefully. Unbiasedness is a constraint, not an objective. Imposing it removes one degree of freedom from the search, and the optimum of a constrained problem cannot beat the optimum of the unconstrained one. Here the constrained answer ($c=1.125$) sits just to the right of the unconstrained answer ($c=1.1111$), and pays $1.2\%$ for it. What the example does not say is that bias is free: the gap between $c^\star$ and $c_{\text{unb}}$ shrinks like $1/n^2$, so the two agree for large samples, and a bias that does not vanish (as in Q135's $\hat B_n$) destroys consistency outright.

Answer. $\;c^\star=\frac{n+2}{n+1}$; at $n=8$, $\mathrm{MSE}(M)=0.0222222\theta^2$, $\mathrm{MSE}(\text{unbiased})=0.0125\theta^2$, $\mathrm{MSE}(c^\star M)=0.0123457\theta^2$ — the biased estimator wins.

Trap. A careless reader sets $c^\star=\mathbb{E}[M]/\mathbb{E}[M^2]$ and stops, worried that the answer depends on $\theta$. It does not: the numerator carries $\theta^2$ and so does the denominator. Had they not cancelled, $c^\star$ would be an oracle quantity, not an estimator — exactly the failure that sinks the "optimal $c$" of G7 Practice 1.3, where the minimizer $c=\frac{n\theta^2}{n\theta^2+v}$ genuinely needs the unknown $\theta$.

Q138. ★ · The conservative $\tfrac14$ bound · G7 §1.6

An auditor samples $n=900$ shipped units at random and finds $108$ with a defect. Let $\theta$ be the unknown population defect rate, estimated by $\hat\theta_n=$ (defects)$/n$. (a) Show that $\theta(1-\theta)\le\tfrac14$ for every $\theta\in[0,1]$, with equality only at $\theta=\tfrac12$. (b) Give the $95\%$ confidence interval for $\theta$ using the conservative bound, and the one using the plug-in variance $\hat\theta_n(1-\hat\theta_n)$. (c) Management wants a $95\%$ margin of error of at most $2$ percentage points before any data are collected. How many units must be sampled?

Solution

Setup. The indicator of "defective" is Bernoulli($\theta$) with variance $\theta(1-\theta)$, so by G7 §1.4 the CLT-based $95\%$ interval is $\hat\theta_n\pm z\sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)/n}$ with $z=1.9599640$ (the $97.5$th standard normal percentile). The variance depends on the very unknown we are estimating, so §1.6 offers two ways out: bound it, or plug in. Here $\hat\theta_n=108/900=0.12$.

Step 1 — the bound. Write $g(\theta)=\theta(1-\theta)=\theta-\theta^2$. Then $g'(\theta)=1-2\theta$, zero only at $\theta=\tfrac12$, and $g''=-2<0$, so this is the unique maximum. Its value is $g(\tfrac12)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac14=0.25$. (Equivalently, $\tfrac14-\theta(1-\theta)=(\theta-\tfrac12)^2\ge0$, which is zero only at $\theta=\tfrac12$.) So $\sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)/n}\le\sqrt{1/(4n)}$ whatever $\theta$ is — a bound that needs no data at all.

Step 2 — the two intervals.

Conservative. $\sqrt{1/(4\cdot900)}=\sqrt{1/3600}=0.0166667$, so the margin is $$1.9599640\times0.0166667=0.0326661,$$ giving $[0.12-0.0326661,\;0.12+0.0326661]=[0.0873339,\;0.1526661]$.

Plug-in. $\hat\theta_n(1-\hat\theta_n)=0.12\times0.88=0.1056$, so the standard error is $\sqrt{0.1056/900}=\sqrt{0.00011733}=0.0108321$ and the margin is $$1.9599640\times0.0108321=0.0212304,$$ giving $[0.0987696,\;0.1412304]$.

The conservative interval is $0.0326661/0.0212304=1.5386$ times wider, because the true variance $0.1056$ is far below the worst case $0.25$: $\theta=0.12$ is nowhere near $\tfrac12$. Simulation at $\theta=0.12$ confirms the asymmetry: the conservative interval covers $99.74\%$ of the time, the plug-in one $94.62\%$ — the first over-delivers on its $95\%$ promise, the second lands on it.

Step 3 — sample size for a $2$-point margin. Nothing is known yet, so the conservative bound is the only honest choice. Require $$z\sqrt{\frac{1}{4n}}\le0.02 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; n\ \ge\ \frac{z^2}{4(0.02)^2} =\frac{(1.9599640)^2\times0.25}{0.0004}=\frac{3.8414588\times 0.25}{0.0004}=2400.9118 .$$ Sample sizes are integers and the inequality must hold, so round up: $n=2401$. Check: $1.9599640\sqrt{0.25/2401}=0.0199996\le0.02$. $\checkmark$

Answer. $\;$(a) $\max_\theta\theta(1-\theta)=\tfrac14$ at $\theta=\tfrac12$. (b) conservative $[0.0873,\,0.1527]$, plug-in $[0.0988,\,0.1412]$. (c) $n=2401$.

Trap. Rounding $2400.91$ down to $2400$, or — worse — using the plug-in variance for the design calculation. You do not have $\hat\theta_n$ before you sample; using a guessed $\theta=0.12$ would give $n=\lceil z^2(0.1056)/0.0004\rceil=1015$, and if the true rate turned out to be near $\tfrac12$ the delivered margin would be $1.96\sqrt{0.25/1015}=0.0308$, half again the promise.

Q139. ★★ · What a CI means · G7 §1.5

A lab reports: "the $95\%$ confidence interval for the catalyst yield $\theta$ is $[12.1,\ 14.3]$ mg." Four statements are made about it. Mark each true or false and justify in one or two sentences.

(i) $\mathbf{P}(12.1\le\theta\le14.3)=0.95$.
(ii) If the experiment were repeated many times and an interval computed the same way each time, about $95\%$ of those intervals would contain $\theta$.
(iii) $95\%$ of future individual yield measurements will fall in $[12.1,14.3]$.
(iv) A $99\%$ interval from the same data would be narrower, because it is more confident.

Then: the lab runs $20$ such experiments, independently, each producing its own $95\%$ interval. (v) What is the expected number of the $20$ intervals that contain $\theta$? (vi) What is the probability that all $20$ contain $\theta$?

Solution

Setup. The definition in G7 §1.4 is $\mathbf{P}_\theta(\hat\Theta_n^-\le\theta\le\hat\Theta_n^+)\ge1-\alpha$, where the probability is over the data and $\theta$ is a fixed constant. Once the data are in hand, $\hat\Theta_n^\pm$ are realized numbers: $12.1$ and $14.3$.

(i) False. In the classical framework $\theta$ is a constant, not a random variable, and $12.1$ and $14.3$ are constants too. The event $\{12.1\le\theta\le14.3\}$ therefore has probability $1$ (if $\theta$ happens to lie there) or $0$ (if it does not); there is nothing left random to carry a $0.95$. The $0.95$ attaches to the procedure, before the data were seen. Only a Bayesian — who makes $\Theta$ random by putting a prior on it — may write a statement like (i), and then it is a credible interval, not a confidence interval (G7 §5.2).

(ii) True. This is the definition, read as a long-run frequency. Note the subtlety: each repetition produces a different interval, and the claim is about the ensemble of intervals, not about the one printed above.

(iii) False. The interval is built from the standard error $\sigma/\sqrt n$ of the sample mean, which shrinks with $n$; individual measurements have spread $\sigma$, which does not. As $n\to\infty$ the CI collapses to a point, while the spread of individual yields is unchanged. (The interval that would contain $95\%$ of future observations is a prediction interval, roughly $\hat\theta_n\pm1.96\sigma$ — wider by a factor $\sqrt n$.)

(iv) False, and backwards. At fixed data the half-width is $z\sigma/\sqrt n$, and $z$ solves $\Phi(z)=1-\alpha/2$; raising the confidence from $95\%$ to $99\%$ raises $z$ from $1.9599640$ to $2.5758293$, so the interval gets wider by a factor $1.3142$. Higher confidence is bought with vagueness; the only way to have both is more data.

(v) Let $N$ be the number of the $20$ intervals that cover $\theta$. The experiments are independent and each covers with probability $0.95$, so $N\sim\text{Binomial}(20,0.95)$ and $$\mathbb{E}[N]=20\times0.95=19 .$$

(vi) By independence, $$\mathbf{P}(N=20)=0.95^{20}=0.3584859 .$$ So it is more likely than not ($1-0.3584859=0.6415141$) that at least one of the twenty intervals misses — and there is no way to tell which. For reference, $\mathbf{P}(N=19)=0.3773536$ and $\mathbf{P}(N\le18)=0.2641605$.

Answer. $\;$(i) F, (ii) T, (iii) F, (iv) F; (v) $\mathbb{E}[N]=19$; (vi) $0.95^{20}=0.3584859$.

Trap. Reading (vi) as "$95\%$ of $20$ intervals contain $\theta$, so the answer is certain to be $19$." $19$ is the expected count; the standard deviation is $\sqrt{20(0.95)(0.05)}=0.9747$, and the single most likely outcome ($0.3774$) is indeed $19$, but $20$ is nearly as likely and values of $18$ or below carry $26.4\%$ of the mass.

Tool: G7 §1.4 (definition), §1.5 (what a CI does and does not mean), §5.2 (CI versus credible interval) · B&T §9.1

Q140. ★★★ · Which variance bound? · G7 §1.6

You must design a study estimating the mean $\theta$ of a score that is guaranteed by construction to lie in $[0,10]$; nothing else is known about its distribution. The requirement is a $99\%$ confidence interval of half-width at most $0.5$. (a) Prove that any random variable supported on $[0,10]$ has $\operatorname{var}(X)\le25$, and identify the distribution attaining it. (b) Find the smallest $n$ meeting the requirement. (c) Two colleagues propose shortcuts: one takes $\sigma=10$ ("the range"), the other assumes the scores are uniform on $[0,10]$ and uses $\sigma^2=100/12$. Compute each of their $n$'s and say precisely what is wrong with each.

Solution

Setup. The interval is $M_n\pm z\sigma/\sqrt n$ with $\Phi(z)=1-\alpha/2$. For $99\%$, $\Phi(z)=0.995$ and $z=2.5758293$. The variance is unknown, and no data exist yet, so — exactly as with the Bernoulli $\tfrac14$ bound of G7 §1.6 — we must use a worst-case bound valid for every distribution allowed by the model.

Step 1 — the variance bound. Variance is the minimizer of mean squared deviation, so comparing against the midpoint $5$ can only increase it: $$\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2\big]\ \le\ \mathbb{E}\big[(X-5)^2\big].$$ Since $0\le X\le10$ we have $|X-5|\le5$ with probability one, hence $(X-5)^2\le25$ pointwise and therefore $\mathbb{E}[(X-5)^2]\le25$. Chaining, $\operatorname{var}(X)\le25$.

Equality needs $\mathbb{E}[X]=5$ and $|X-5|=5$ almost surely: the two-point law putting mass $\tfrac12$ at $0$ and $\tfrac12$ at $10$. Checking the family $\mathbf{P}(X=10)=p$, $\mathbf{P}(X=0)=1-p$: $\operatorname{var}=100p(1-p)$, maximized at $p=\tfrac12$ with value $25$. (Scanning $p$ on a fine grid returns a maximum of exactly $25.0$.) So $\sigma_{\max}=5$, not $10$.

Step 2 — the sample size. Require $$\frac{z\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le0.5 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; n\ \ge\ \left(\frac{z\sigma_{\max}}{0.5}\right)^2 =\left(\frac{2.5758293\times5}{0.5}\right)^2=(25.758293)^2=663.4897 .$$ Round up: $n=664$. Check: $2.5758293\sqrt{25/664}=2.5758293\times0.1940376=0.4998078\le0.5$. $\checkmark$ (For contrast, the same job at $95\%$ confidence needs only $n=385$: the $99\%$ guarantee costs $(2.5758293/1.9599640)^2=1.727$ times the data.)

Step 3 — the two shortcuts.

"$\sigma=10$." This confuses the range with the standard deviation. It gives $n\ge(2.5758293\times10/0.5)^2=2654$ — valid, since it over-bounds the variance by a factor $4$, but wasteful: it demands $2654/664=3.9970$ times the data for the same guarantee (the factor is $4$ up to the two roundings). Conservative bounds are only useful if they are the tightest distribution-free ones.

"uniform, so $\sigma^2=100/12=8.3333$." This gives $n\ge(2.5758293\sqrt{8.3333}/0.5)^2=222$, and it is not valid: it is an assumption about the shape of the distribution, and the model said only that the scores lie in $[0,10]$. If the scores are in fact bimodal — many $0$'s and many $10$'s, which is common for pass/fail-like scoring — the true variance is up to $25$, three times the assumed value, and the delivered half-width would be $2.5758293\sqrt{25/222}=0.8643917$, not $0.5$. The interval would then be a $99\%$ interval in name only. This is the difference between a bound and a guess.

Answer. $\;$(a) $\operatorname{var}(X)\le25$, attained by the two-point law $\tfrac12\delta_0+\tfrac12\delta_{10}$; (b) $n=664$; (c) $\sigma=10$ gives $n=2654$ (valid but $4\times$ wasteful), the uniform assumption gives $n=222$ (invalid — an unjustified distributional assumption, not a bound).

Trap. Reaching for the Bernoulli $\tfrac14$ bound directly. That bound is $\operatorname{var}\le\tfrac14$ for a variable on $[0,1]$; the general statement is $\operatorname{var}\le(b-a)^2/4$ for support $[a,b]$, and forgetting to rescale by $(b-a)^2=100$ would give $n=\lceil(2.5758293\times0.5/0.5)^2\rceil=7$ — off by two orders of magnitude.

Tool: G7 §1.4 (CI for a mean), §1.6 (bounding an unknown variance; the $\theta(1-\theta)\le1/4$ device) · B&T §9.1

Q141. ★ · Bernoulli ML and invariance · G7 §2.2

A machine either passes or fails a self-test each morning, independently, with unknown failure probability $\theta$. Over $n=40$ mornings it failed $13$ times. (a) Write the likelihood and derive the ML estimate $\hat\theta$ from scratch. (b) Report the ML estimate of the odds $\theta/(1-\theta)$ and of the probability $\theta^2$ that it fails on two specified consecutive mornings, naming the principle you used. (c) How many times more likely are the observed data under $\hat\theta$ than under $\theta=1/2$?

Solution

Setup. Let $X_1,\dots,X_{40}$ be i.i.d. Bernoulli($\theta$), $X_i=1$ for a failure, and let $k=\sum_i x_i=13$ be the observed number of failures.

Step 1 — the likelihood. By independence the joint PMF factors: $$L(\theta)=p_X(x;\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{40}\theta^{x_i}(1-\theta)^{1-x_i} =\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}=\theta^{13}(1-\theta)^{27}.$$ (This is a function of $\theta$ with the data frozen — not a probability distribution over $\theta$; see the §2.1 gotcha.)

Step 2 — log and differentiate. The logarithm is increasing, so it has the same maximizer, and it turns the product into a sum: $$\ell(\theta)=13\log\theta+27\log(1-\theta),\qquad \frac{d\ell}{d\theta}=\frac{13}{\theta}-\frac{27}{1-\theta}.$$ Setting this to zero and multiplying through by $\theta(1-\theta)>0$: $$13(1-\theta)-27\theta=0\;\Longrightarrow\;13=40\theta\;\Longrightarrow\; \hat\theta=\frac{13}{40}=0.325 .$$ It is a maximum: $\ell''(\theta)=-13/\theta^2-27/(1-\theta)^2<0$ everywhere, so $\ell$ is strictly concave and the stationary point is the global maximum. In general $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=k/n$ — the sample frequency.

Step 3 — invariance. ML estimates transform: if $\hat\theta$ maximizes the likelihood in $\theta$ and $g$ is any function, the ML estimate of $g(\theta)$ is $g(\hat\theta)$ (G7 §2.7, the invariance principle). Hence $$\widehat{\text{odds}}=\frac{0.325}{1-0.325}=\frac{0.325}{0.675}=0.4814815, \qquad \widehat{\theta^2}=(0.325)^2=0.105625 .$$ No new maximization is needed.

Step 4 — likelihood ratio against $\theta=1/2$. $$\ell(\hat\theta)=13\log(0.325)+27\log(0.675)=-25.2232411,\qquad \ell(0.5)=40\log(0.5)=-27.7258872 .$$ The difference is $2.5026461$, so $$\frac{L(\hat\theta)}{L(0.5)}=e^{2.5026461}=12.2148 .$$ The data are about $12.2$ times more likely under $\hat\theta=0.325$ than under a fair-coin machine.

Answer. $\;\hat\theta=0.325$; $\widehat{\text{odds}}=0.4814815$; $\widehat{\theta^2}=0.105625$; likelihood ratio $=12.2148$.

Trap. Reading the $12.2$ as "the machine is $12.2$ times more likely to have $\theta=0.325$ than $\theta=0.5$." Likelihoods are probabilities of the data, not of the parameter; converting them into statements about $\theta$ requires a prior (G6 §3). A second trap: estimating the odds by rerunning the maximization in a new parametrization — unnecessary, and invariance guarantees the same answer.

Q142. ★★ · Normal ML, both parameters · G7 §2.3

Five independent readings of a reference voltage, modeled as $N(\mu,v)$ with both $\mu$ and $v$ unknown, are $$9.8,\quad 10.4,\quad 9.1,\quad 10.9,\quad 10.3 .$$ (a) Report $\hat\mu_{\text{ML}}$ and $\hat v_{\text{ML}}$, showing the arithmetic. (b) Compare $\hat v_{\text{ML}}$ with the unbiased sample variance $\hat s_n^2$ and state the exact relation between the two. (c) Give the ML estimate of $\mathbf{P}(X>11)$, i.e. the chance a future reading exceeds $11$ volts.

Solution

Setup. From G7 §2.3, maximizing $\ell(\mu,v)=-\frac n2\log(2\pi v)-\frac{1}{2v}\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2$ gives $$\hat\mu_{\text{ML}}=\frac1n\sum_i x_i=m_n,\qquad \hat v_{\text{ML}}=\frac1n\sum_i(x_i-m_n)^2 .$$ The $\mu$-equation does not involve $v$, so the two maximizations decouple: solve for $\mu$ first, then substitute. Here $n=5$.

Step 1 — the mean. $$\sum_i x_i=9.8+10.4+9.1+10.9+10.3=50.5,\qquad \hat\mu_{\text{ML}}=\frac{50.5}{5}=10.1 .$$

Step 2 — the variance. Deviations from $10.1$: $$-0.3,\quad 0.3,\quad -1.0,\quad 0.8,\quad 0.2 ,$$ whose squares are $0.09,\ 0.09,\ 1.00,\ 0.64,\ 0.04$, summing to $$\sum_i(x_i-10.1)^2=1.86 .$$ Hence $$\hat v_{\text{ML}}=\frac{1.86}{5}=0.372 .$$

Step 3 — the unbiased comparison. $$\hat s_n^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i(x_i-m_n)^2=\frac{1.86}{4}=0.465,\qquad \frac{\hat s_n^2}{\hat v_{\text{ML}}}=\frac{0.465}{0.372}=1.25=\frac{n}{n-1}.$$ Exactly, $\hat v_{\text{ML}}=\frac{n-1}{n}\hat s_n^2$, so $\mathbb{E}[\hat v_{\text{ML}}]=\frac{n-1}{n}v=0.8v$: the ML variance is biased downward by $20\%$ at $n=5$. (Simulating $300{,}000$ samples of size $5$ from $N(0,4)$ gives $\mathbb{E}[\hat v_{\text{ML}}]/v=0.79997$. $\checkmark$) The reason is structural: the deviations are taken from $m_n$, the point that minimizes the sum of squares, so the sum is systematically too small; dividing by $n-1$ instead of $n$ repairs it exactly.

Step 4 — the tail probability, by invariance. $\mathbf{P}(X>11)$ is a function of $(\mu,v)$, so its ML estimate is that function evaluated at the ML estimates (G7 §2.7). With $\hat\sigma=\sqrt{0.372}=0.6099180$, $$z=\frac{11-10.1}{0.6099180}=\frac{0.9}{0.6099180}=1.4756081,\qquad \widehat{\mathbf{P}}(X>11)=1-\Phi(1.4756081)=0.0700246 .$$

Answer. $\;\hat\mu_{\text{ML}}=10.1$, $\hat v_{\text{ML}}=0.372$; $\hat s_n^2=0.465=\frac{n}{n-1}\hat v_{\text{ML}}$; $\widehat{\mathbf{P}}(X>11)=0.0700246$.

Trap. Using $\hat s_n^2=0.465$ in Step 4 "because it is the unbiased one." The invariance principle applies to the ML estimates; substituting $0.465$ gives $1-\Phi(0.9/0.6819091)=1-\Phi(1.3198240)=0.0934469$, a $33.4\%$ larger answer, and it is the ML estimate of nothing. (Note also that unbiasedness is not preserved by nonlinear maps: $\hat s_n$ is not an unbiased estimate of $\sigma$ even though $\hat s_n^2$ is unbiased for $v$.)

Q143. ★★ · ML when calculus fails · G7 §2.4

Detector response times are modeled as i.i.d. shifted exponentials: for an unknown threshold $\theta$, $$f_X(x;\theta)=e^{-(x-\theta)}\ \ \text{for } x\ge\theta,\qquad f_X(x;\theta)=0\ \ \text{for } x<\theta .$$ Five times are recorded: $3.2,\ 4.7,\ 3.9,\ 5.1,\ 3.5$ (seconds). (a) Write the likelihood and explain why setting $d\ell/d\theta=0$ finds nothing. (b) Find $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}$. (c) Given that $\min_i X_i-\theta$ is exponential with rate $n$, find the bias of $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}$, build an unbiased estimator, and compare the two MSEs.

Solution

Setup. $n=5$, $\sum_i x_i=3.2+4.7+3.9+5.1+3.5=20.4$, and $\min_i x_i=3.2$. The parameter here is a boundary of the support, which is the signature of a corner solution (G7 §2.4).

Step 1 — the likelihood, with its constraint written down. By independence, $$L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{5}f_X(x_i;\theta) =\begin{cases}\displaystyle e^{\,n\theta-\sum_i x_i}=e^{5\theta-20.4}, & \theta\le\min_i x_i=3.2,\\[4pt] 0, & \theta>3.2,\end{cases}$$ because a single observation below $\theta$ makes its density — and hence the whole product — zero. The constraint $\theta\le3.2$ is not decoration; it is the information the data carry about $\theta$.

Step 2 — why differentiation fails. On the region where $L>0$, $$\ell(\theta)=5\theta-20.4,\qquad \frac{d\ell}{d\theta}=5 .$$ The derivative is the constant $+5$: never zero, so there is no stationary point at all. The log-likelihood is strictly increasing in $\theta$ and then falls off a cliff to $-\infty$ at $\theta=3.2^+$. Calculus finds nothing because the maximum is not interior.

Step 3 — the ML estimate. Since $\ell$ increases on $(-\infty,3.2]$, push $\theta$ as far right as allowed: $$\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=\min_i x_i=3.2 .$$ Sanity check on the values: $\ell(3.2)=5(3.2)-20.4=-4.4$, while $\ell(3.0)=-5.4$ and $\ell(2.0)=-10.4$ — the likelihood really is climbing right up to the boundary.

Step 4 — bias and correction. Let $Y=\min_i X_i-\theta$. We are given $Y\sim\text{Exp}(n)$, so $\mathbb{E}[Y]=1/n=1/5=0.2$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y)=1/n^2=0.04$. Therefore $$\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}]=\theta+0.2,\qquad \mathrm{bias}=+0.2 ,$$ a systematic overestimate — unsurprising, since the smallest of five observations is still above the true floor. The obvious repair subtracts the bias: $$\tilde\Theta=\min_i X_i-\frac1n = 3.2-0.2=3.0,\qquad \mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta]=\theta .$$ (A $400{,}000$-run simulation with $\theta=3$ gives $\mathbb{E}[\min_iX_i]-\theta=0.19971$ and $\operatorname{var}(\min_iX_i)=0.04001$. $\checkmark$)

Step 5 — the MSEs. Subtracting a constant does not change the variance, so both estimators have variance $1/n^2=0.04$, and $$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat\theta_{\text{ML}})=0.04+(0.2)^2=0.04+0.04=0.08,\qquad \mathrm{MSE}(\tilde\Theta)=0.04+0=0.04 .$$ Here the unbiased version halves the MSE — the opposite verdict from Q137, and for a clear reason: the correction there was a rescaling (which changes the variance too), whereas here it is a shift (which does not), so removing the bias is pure profit.

Answer. $\;\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=3.2$; bias $=+1/n=0.2$; unbiased estimator $\min_iX_i-1/n=3.0$; MSEs $0.08$ and $0.04$ respectively.

Trap. Writing $\ell(\theta)=n\theta-\sum_ix_i$ without the constraint and concluding "no maximum, $\hat\theta=+\infty$." The constraint region is part of the likelihood; dropping it loses the entire answer. The mirror-image trap is answering $\bar x=20.4/5=4.08$ because "the mean of the model is $\theta+1$, so $\hat\theta=\bar x-1=3.08$" — that is the method-of-moments estimate, a legitimate but different estimator, and it is not the maximizer of $L$ (indeed $\theta=3.08$ is feasible here, but with a sample containing a value below $3.08$ it could even be impossible).

Q144. ★★ · ML, MAP and the flat prior · G7 §5.2

A new coin is tossed $n=6$ times and lands heads $k=5$ times. Let $\theta$ be the probability of heads. (a) Give the ML estimate. (b) Give the MAP estimate under a uniform prior on $[0,1]$, and prove the general statement "MAP $=$ ML when the prior is flat". (c) Give the MAP estimate under the prior $f_\Theta(\theta)\propto\theta(1-\theta)$ on $[0,1]$. (d) Give the posterior mean under the uniform prior, using $\int_0^1\theta^a(1-\theta)^b\,d\theta=\frac{a!\,b!}{(a+b+1)!}$. (e) Which of these four numbers would you report, and what question does each answer?

Solution

Setup. The data enter only through the likelihood $L(\theta)=\binom{6}{5}\theta^5(1-\theta)^1$; the binomial coefficient is a constant in $\theta$ and never affects any maximizer.

(a) ML. By Q141's derivation, $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=k/n=5/6=0.8333333$.

(b) MAP under a flat prior. Bayes' rule gives the posterior $$f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=\frac{f_\Theta(\theta)\,p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)} {p_X(x)} .$$ The denominator $p_X(x)$ does not depend on $\theta$. If the prior is flat, $f_\Theta(\theta)=1$ on $[0,1]$, it does not depend on $\theta$ either, so $$f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)\ \propto\ p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)=L(\theta) \quad\text{on }[0,1].$$ Two functions that are positive multiples of one another have the same maximizer, hence $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}$ — provided the ML estimate lies inside the prior's support, which it does here. Numerically, maximizing $\theta^5(1-\theta)$ on a $2{,}000{,}001$-point grid returns $0.8333335$. $\checkmark$ So $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=5/6=0.8333333$.

(c) MAP under $f_\Theta(\theta)\propto\theta(1-\theta)$. Now the posterior is proportional to $$\theta(1-\theta)\cdot\theta^5(1-\theta)=\theta^6(1-\theta)^2 .$$ Take logs and differentiate: $\frac{6}{\theta}-\frac{2}{1-\theta}=0$ gives $6(1-\theta)=2\theta$, i.e. $6=8\theta$ and $$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\frac{6}{8}=0.75 .$$ (Grid check: $0.7500000$. $\checkmark$) The prior, which favors mid-range coins, has pulled the estimate from $0.8333$ toward $0.5$ — it acts like two extra pseudo-observations, one head and one tail.

(d) Posterior mean under the flat prior. $$\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x] =\frac{\int_0^1\theta\cdot\theta^5(1-\theta)\,d\theta}{\int_0^1\theta^5(1-\theta)\,d\theta} =\frac{\frac{6!\,1!}{8!}}{\frac{5!\,1!}{7!}} =\frac{720/40320}{120/5040}=\frac{0.0178571}{0.0238095}=0.75 .$$ In general this is $(k+1)/(n+2)=6/8=0.75$, Laplace's rule of succession. (Numerical integration returns $0.7500000$. $\checkmark$)

(e) What each number answers. $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=0.8333$: "which $\theta$ makes the observed data most likely?" — no prior, no probability statement about $\theta$. $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}$ (flat) $=0.8333$: "which $\theta$ has the highest posterior density?" — numerically identical here, but a genuinely different question, and it comes with a full posterior you can integrate. $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}$ (Beta-shaped prior) $=0.75$: the same question with real prior information supplied. Posterior mean $=0.75$: "what is the LMS estimate, minimizing $\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-\hat\theta)^2\mid X]$?" (G6 §4). With six tosses I would report $0.75$ with the posterior attached, since ML's $0.8333$ has an awkward feature — it would have returned $1.0$ had all six landed heads, asserting that tails is impossible on the evidence of six tosses.

Answer. $\;$ML $=5/6=0.8333333$; MAP (flat) $=5/6=0.8333333$; MAP (prior $\propto\theta(1-\theta)$) $=0.75$; posterior mean (flat) $=(k+1)/(n+2)=0.75$.

Trap. "A flat prior makes everything Bayesian collapse to the classical answer." Only the mode collapses. The posterior mean under the same flat prior is $0.75$, differing from ML by $0.0833$; the posterior variance, credible intervals and predictive distribution have no classical counterpart at all. A flat prior kills the difference between MAP and ML, not the difference between Bayesian and classical inference.

(b) Yes — it is the least-squares slope of the no-intercept model, whose $\mathrm{SSE}=0.0983636>0.0910$. (c) The reported line minimizes no criterion at all and violates both residual identities.

Trap. Concluding "the two slopes are close, so it does not matter." The gap here is small only because $\hat\theta_0=0.09$ is nearly zero. Shift every $y_i$ up by $50$: the correct slope stays $1.97$ (a vertical shift changes only the intercept), while $\sum x_iy_i/\sum x_i^2$ jumps to $(109.7+50\cdot15)/55=15.63$ — a factor of eight wrong.

Solution

Setup. A least-squares line is a summary of the data in the observed range of $x$, under a model $Y_i=\theta_0+\theta_1x_i+W_i$ in which the $x_i$ are fixed, chosen constants and only the noise is random (G7 §3.1). Every one of the four claims oversteps that in a different direction.

(i) False. $R^2$ measures how much of the observed spread $S_{yy}=38.90$ the line removes: $1-0.091/38.90=0.9976607$. It says nothing about (a) linearity outside the five settings tried, (b) whether a curve would do even better, or (c) the absolute size of the residual noise, which here is $\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\mathrm{SSE}/(n-2)}=\sqrt{0.091/3}=\sqrt{0.0303333}=0.1741647$ L/min. $R^2$ is also inflated by a wide spread of $x$'s: the same noise with the settings packed into $[3,3.2]$ would give a much smaller $R^2$ with an identical physical relationship. What the data support: over $x\in[1,5]$, a line fits with residual scale about $0.17$ L/min.

(ii) False — extrapolation. The arithmetic $0.09+1.97(12)=23.73$ is right; the inference is not. No datum was collected above $x=5$, so nothing in the fit constrains the shape of the relation there, and pumps saturate. Confidence intervals do not rescue you: they quantify noise around an assumed model, not the possibility that the model is the wrong shape outside its data (G7 §3.8). What the data support: predictions for $x$ within roughly $[1,5]$.

(iii) False. $R^2$ is not a significance statement; with $n=5$ points, three residual degrees of freedom, one needs the interval itself. With $\sigma$ unknown, G7 §3.6 gives $\hat\theta_1\pm t_{n-2,0.975}\,\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}$. Here $t_{3,0.975}=3.1824463$ and the standard error is $\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}=0.1741647/\sqrt{10}=0.0550757$, so the $95\%$ interval is $$1.97\pm3.1824463\times0.0550757=1.97\pm0.1752755=[1.7947245,\ 2.1452755].$$ It happens to exclude $0$ comfortably, so the conclusion survives — but it survives because the interval was computed, not because $R^2$ was large. (A dataset with $n=3$ and $R^2=0.99$ can easily have an interval straddling zero.)

(iv) False. Least squares is asymmetric: it minimizes squared errors in $y$ only. Regressing $x$ on $y$ minimizes a different criterion and gives slope $$\hat\beta_1=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{yy}}=\frac{19.7}{38.90}=0.5064267,$$ not $1/1.97=0.5076142$. In fact the two slopes multiply to $\hat\theta_1\hat\beta_1=1.97\times0.5064267=0.9976607=R^2\le1$, with equality only for a perfect fit. The two lines coincide only when the points are exactly collinear. What the data support: to predict $y$ from $x$, use $\hat y=0.09+1.97x$; to predict $x$ from $y$, refit the other way; and neither is a statement that changing $x$ causes $y$ to move (G7 §3.8: only an intervention establishes that).

Answer. $\;$All four are false. $\hat\sigma=0.1741647$; $\hat y(12)=23.73$ is arithmetic, not evidence; the $95\%$ slope interval is $[1.7947245,\ 2.1452755]$; regressing $x$ on $y$ gives slope $0.5064267$, and $\hat\theta_1\hat\beta_1=R^2=0.9976607$.

Trap. The tempting answer is that (ii) is true "because the model is linear." The model is linear by assumption, and the assumption was only ever tested where data exist. A fitted line is an interpolation device with an extrapolation-shaped formula.

Q148. ★★ · A discrete LRT · G7 §4.2, §4.5

A supplier's process is either in control ($H_0$: each unit is defective independently with probability $p_0=0.1$) or drifted ($H_1$: $p_1=0.3$). You inspect $n=10$ units and count defectives $X$. (a) Derive the likelihood ratio $L(k)$ and show the LRT rejects $H_0$ for large $k$. (b) Evaluate $L(4)$ and interpret it. (c) Find the rejection region of the form $\{X\ge k\}$ with the largest attainable level not exceeding $\alpha=0.05$, and report the resulting $\alpha$ and $\beta$. (d) Why can you not achieve $\alpha=0.05$ exactly?

Solution

Setup. $X\sim\text{Binomial}(10,p)$ under both hypotheses, differing only in $p$. Its PMF is $p_X(k;p)=\binom{10}{k}p^k(1-p)^{10-k}$.

Step 1 — the ratio. The binomial coefficients are identical under the two hypotheses and cancel: $$L(k)=\frac{p_X(k;H_1)}{p_X(k;H_0)} =\frac{\binom{10}{k}(0.3)^k(0.7)^{10-k}}{\binom{10}{k}(0.1)^k(0.9)^{10-k}} =\left(\frac{0.3}{0.1}\right)^{k}\left(\frac{0.7}{0.9}\right)^{10-k} =3^k(0.7777778)^{10-k}.$$ Monotonicity: consecutive values satisfy $$\frac{L(k+1)}{L(k)}=\frac{0.3}{0.1}\cdot\frac{0.9}{0.7}=3\times1.2857143=3.8571429>1,$$ so $L$ is strictly increasing in $k$. Therefore $\{L(x)>\xi\}$ is always an upper tail $\{X\ge k\}$: rejecting for large $\xi$ is the same as rejecting for many defectives. This is what makes the abstract LRT usable — you never have to sort likelihood ratios by hand.

Step 2 — $L(4)$. $L(4)=3^4(0.7777778)^6=81\times0.2213773=17.9315653$. (Direct ratio of the two binomial PMFs: $17.9315653$. $\checkmark$) The observed count $4$ is about $17.9$ times more likely under drift than under control. For scale, $L(0)=(0.7777778)^{10}=0.0810131$, $L(2)=1.2052767$ and $L(3)=4.6489243$: the ratio crosses $1$ between $k=1$ and $k=2$.

Step 3 — calibrating to $\alpha\le0.05$. Compute upper tails under $H_0$ ($p=0.1$): $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge2)=0.2639011,\quad \mathbf{P}(X\ge3)=0.0701908,\quad \mathbf{P}(X\ge4)=0.0127952,\quad \mathbf{P}(X\ge5)=0.0016349 .$$ The smallest threshold whose tail does not exceed $0.05$ is $k=4$, since $\mathbf{P}(X\ge3)=0.0701908>0.05$ while $\mathbf{P}(X\ge4)=0.0127952\le0.05$. So $R=\{X\ge4\}$ and $\alpha=0.0127952$.

The type II error is the probability of not rejecting when $H_1$ holds: $$\beta=\mathbf{P}(X\le3;\,p=0.3)=0.6496107,$$ so the power is $1-0.6496107=0.3503893$. (Simulation with $400{,}000$ draws each returns $\hat\alpha=0.012705$ and $\hat\beta=0.650373$. $\checkmark$) Any critical value $\xi\in(L(3),L(4)]=(4.6489243,\ 17.9315653]$ produces this same test.

Step 4 — why not exactly $0.05$. $X$ takes eleven values, so $\mathbf{P}(X\ge k;H_0)$ takes only eleven values as $k$ ranges over the integers; $0.05$ is not one of them. It sits inside the jump from $0.0701908$ (at $k=3$) to $0.0127952$ (at $k=4$), a gap equal to $\mathbf{P}(X=3;H_0)=0.0573956$. Following the §4.5 rule — never exceed the promised level — you take $k=4$ and accept a test that is markedly more conservative than advertised. The price is visible in $\beta$: the $k=3$ test would have had $\beta=0.3827828$, roughly $0.27$ better power, but at $\alpha=0.0702$.

Answer. $\;L(k)=3^k(7/9)^{10-k}$, increasing in $k$; $L(4)=17.9315653$; $R=\{X\ge4\}$ with $\alpha=0.0127952$ and $\beta=0.6496107$ (power $0.3503893$); exact $\alpha=0.05$ is unattainable because $X$ is discrete.

Trap. Choosing $k=3$ "because $0.0702$ is closer to $0.05$ than $0.0128$ is." Closeness is not the criterion — the level is a promise, an upper bound on the false-rejection rate, and $0.0702>0.05$ breaks it. A second trap: reporting $\beta=1-\alpha=0.987$. $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are computed under different distributions and do not sum to anything in particular.

Tool: G7 §4.2 (likelihood ratio test, calibrating $\xi$ to $\alpha$), §4.5 (the discrete case: take the largest attainable level $\le\alpha$) · B&T §9.3, Example 9.14

Q149. ★ · What "not rejected" means · G7 §4.1

A researcher tests $H_0:\mu=0$ against $H_1:\mu=0.5$ using $n=9$ i.i.d. normal observations with known $\sigma=1$, rejecting when the sample mean $M_9$ exceeds a threshold calibrated to $\alpha=0.05$. She obtains $M_9=0.41$, does not reject, and writes in her report: "we conclude that $\mu=0$." (a) Find the threshold. (b) Find $\beta$, the probability of failing to reject when $\mu=0.5$ is in fact true. (c) Criticize her sentence and rewrite it. (d) What $n$ would push $\beta$ down to $0.10$?

Solution

Setup. Under either hypothesis $M_9\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)=N(\mu,1/9)$, so the standard error is $\sigma/\sqrt n=1/3=0.3333333$. The rejection region is one-sided (larger $\mu$ means larger $M_n$), $R=\{M_9>c\}$.

Step 1 — the threshold. Calibrate under $H_0$: $$\alpha=\mathbf{P}(M_9>c;\,\mu=0)=1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{c}{1/3}\right)=0.05 \;\Longrightarrow\;\frac{c}{1/3}=z_{0.95}=1.6448536,$$ $$c=\frac{1.6448536}{3}=0.5482845 .$$ Her observed $M_9=0.41<0.5482845$, so indeed she does not reject.

Step 2 — $\beta$. Under $H_1$, $M_9\sim N(0.5,1/9)$, so $$\beta=\mathbf{P}(M_9\le c;\,\mu=0.5) =\Phi\!\left(\frac{0.5482845-0.5}{1/3}\right) =\Phi(0.1448536)=0.5575868 .$$ Equivalently $\beta=\Phi\big(z_{0.95}-\mu_1\sqrt n/\sigma\big)=\Phi(1.6448536-1.5)$. (Simulation of $500{,}000$ sample means gives $\hat\beta=0.55766$ and $\hat\alpha=0.049778$. $\checkmark$) The power is only $0.4424132$.

Step 3 — the critique. "We conclude that $\mu=0$" is unsupportable. Even if $\mu$ really were $0.5$ — the specific alternative she cares about — this test would have failed to reject $55.8\%$ of the time. Not rejecting is therefore only weak evidence, and none of it is evidence for $H_0$: the test was designed to control the false-rejection rate at $0.05$ and was never designed to control the false-acceptance rate at all. The asymmetry is deliberate (G7 §4.1): $H_0$ is the default, and the data either dislodge it or fail to.

A correct sentence: "at the $5\%$ level, the data do not provide sufficient evidence to reject $\mu=0$; with $n=9$ this test has power only $0.44$ against $\mu=0.5$, so the result is also consistent with a real effect of that size."

Step 4 — the $n$ for $\beta=0.10$. Require $\Phi\big(z_{0.95}-\mu_1\sqrt n/\sigma\big)\le0.10$. Since $\Phi$ is increasing and $\Phi(-z_{0.90})=0.10$, this is $z_{0.95}-\mu_1\sqrt n/\sigma\le-z_{0.90}$, i.e. $$\sqrt n\ \ge\ \frac{(z_{0.95}+z_{0.90})\sigma}{\mu_1} =\frac{(1.6448536+1.2815516)\times1}{0.5}=5.8528104,$$ $$n\ \ge\ (5.8528104)^2=34.2554\;\Longrightarrow\; n=35 .$$ Check: $\Phi(1.6448536-0.5\sqrt{35})=\Phi(1.6448536-2.9580399)=\Phi(-1.3131863)=0.0945601\le0.10$. $\checkmark$ Note the cost: detecting a half-standard-deviation effect reliably needs roughly four times her sample.

Answer. $\;c=0.5482845$; $\beta=0.5575868$ (power $0.4424132$); "we conclude $\mu=0$" is wrong — the correct statement is "insufficient evidence to reject at the $5\%$ level"; $n=35$ gives $\beta\le0.10$.

Trap. "Not rejecting at $\alpha=0.05$ means $\mathbf{P}(H_0)=0.95$." There is no probability attached to $H_0$ in classical inference — $\mu$ is a constant, not a random variable. Attaching one requires a prior and turns the problem into the Bayesian MAP comparison of G6 §3. A second trap: reading $\alpha=0.05$ as if it bounded the error rate of both decisions; here the other error rate is $0.5576$, eleven times larger.

Q150. ★★★ · LRT, threshold, and the $\alpha$–$\beta$ tradeoff · G7 §4.2, §4.5

Component lifetimes $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are i.i.d. exponential. The manufacturer claims a failure rate $\lambda_0=1$ per year ($H_0$); a rival claims the parts are twice as good, $\lambda_1=1/2$ ($H_1$). You test $n=10$ components and observe the total lifetime $S=\sum_iX_i$. (a) Derive $L(x)$ and show the LRT rejects $H_0$ for large $S$. (b) With $\alpha=0.05$, find the threshold $\xi$ on $S$ (use: the $0.95$ quantile of a Gamma$(10,\text{rate }1)$ is $15.7052164$) and the corresponding threshold on the likelihood ratio itself. (c) Compute $\beta$. (d) Tabulate $(\alpha,\beta)$ for $\alpha=0.10,\ 0.05,\ 0.01$ using thresholds $14.2059903$, $15.7052164$, $18.7831174$, and state the tradeoff. (e) What $n$ makes $\beta\le0.05$ while keeping $\alpha=0.05$? (Gamma tail values are supplied below.)

Solution

Setup. The exponential density with rate $\lambda$ is $f(x;\lambda)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x\ge0$. Under $H_j$ the sum $S=\sum_{i=1}^{10}X_i$ is Erlang/Gamma with $n=10$ stages and rate $\lambda_j$ (G4 §3), with mean $n/\lambda_j$: $\mathbb{E}[S;H_0]=10$ and $\mathbb{E}[S;H_1]=20$. Longer total life is evidence for the rival's claim.

Step 1 — the likelihood ratio. By independence, $$L(x)=\frac{f_X(x;H_1)}{f_X(x;H_0)} =\frac{\prod_i\lambda_1e^{-\lambda_1x_i}}{\prod_i\lambda_0e^{-\lambda_0x_i}} =\left(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_0}\right)^{n} e^{-(\lambda_1-\lambda_0)\sum_ix_i} =\left(\tfrac12\right)^{10}e^{\,S/2},$$ using $\lambda_1-\lambda_0=0.5-1=-0.5$. The data enter only through $S$ — $S$ is a sufficient statistic — and $e^{S/2}$ is strictly increasing in $S$. Hence $$\{L(x)>\xi_L\}=\{S>\xi\}\quad\text{for the matching }\xi,$$ so the LRT is "reject when total lifetime is large," as intuition demands.

Step 2 — calibrate $\xi$. Choose $\xi$ so the false-rejection probability is $\alpha$: $$\mathbf{P}(S>\xi;\,H_0)=0.05\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\xi=\text{Gamma}(10,1)\text{'s }0.95 \text{ quantile}=15.7052164 .$$ Check: $\mathbf{P}(S>15.7052164;H_0)=0.0500000$. $\checkmark$ On the sample mean this is $\xi/n=1.5705216$ years — reject if the average part lasts more than $1.57$ years, against a claimed mean of $1$. Translating to the ratio scale, $$\xi_L=\left(\tfrac12\right)^{10}e^{\,15.7052164/2} =0.0009765625\times e^{7.8526082}=0.0009765625\times2572.4350=2.5121436 .$$ So the test rejects when the data are more than about $2.51$ times as likely under $H_1$. That the threshold on $L$ exceeds $1$ is the classical asymmetry at work: a bare majority of evidence is not enough to dislodge $H_0$.

Step 3 — $\beta$. Under $H_1$, $S\sim$ Gamma$(10,\text{rate }0.5)$, mean $20$: $$\beta=\mathbf{P}(S\le15.7052164;\,H_1)=0.2652645 ,$$ so the power is $0.7347355$. (Simulation with $300{,}000$ replicates of each hypothesis returns $\hat\alpha=0.050167$ and $\hat\beta=0.265607$. $\checkmark$) Intuitively, $H_1$'s distribution is centered at $20$ and the threshold sits at $15.7052164$, only $(20-15.7052164)/6.3245553=0.6790649$ standard deviations below it (the Gamma standard deviation under $H_1$ is $\sqrt{10}/0.5=6.3245553$), so a substantial left tail falls below the threshold.

Step 4 — the tradeoff. Evaluating $\mathbf{P}(S\le\xi;H_1)$ at the three thresholds:

$$\begin{array}{c|c|c|c} \alpha & \xi & \beta & \text{power }1-\beta\\\hline 0.10 & 14.2059903 & 0.1801000 & 0.8199000\\ 0.05 & 15.7052164 & 0.2652645 & 0.7347355\\ 0.01 & 18.7831174 & 0.4640388 & 0.5359612 \end{array}$$

At fixed $n$, moving $\xi$ right shrinks the $H_0$ tail and grows the $H_1$ one: every factor-of-five reduction in $\alpha$ here roughly doubles $\beta$. There is no threshold that makes both small — the two error probabilities are linked by a single dial, and the only way off the curve is more data (G7 §4.5, §4.6).

Step 5 — the $n$ that buys $\beta\le0.05$. Increase $n$, recalibrating $\xi_n$ each time as the $0.95$ quantile of Gamma$(n,1)$ and evaluating $\beta_n=\mathbf{P}(S\le\xi_n;\text{rate }0.5)$. Scanning upward from $n=10$, the requirement first holds at $$n=23:\qquad \xi_{23}=31.4148102,\qquad \beta_{23}=0.0496647\le0.05 .$$ So $23$ components buy symmetric $5\%$ errors, against $10$ components with $\beta=0.265$. Note the shape of the answer: the separation between the hypotheses is fixed (a factor of two in rate), and both error probabilities shrink only as the sampling distributions pull apart at rate $\sqrt n$.

Answer. $\;L(x)=(1/2)^{10}e^{S/2}$, increasing in $S$; $\xi=15.7052164$ (equivalently $\xi_L=2.5121436$, or $1.5705216$ on the sample mean); $\beta=0.2652645$; the tradeoff is $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.10,0.1801),\ (0.05,0.2653),\ (0.01,0.4640)$; $n=23$ gives $\beta=0.0496647$ at $\alpha=0.05$.

Trap. Rejecting for small $S$ because "$H_1$ has the smaller parameter $\lambda_1=1/2$." The parameter is a rate, not a mean: a smaller rate means longer lifetimes. Getting the direction wrong flips the test into one with $\alpha=0.05$ and $\beta=\mathbf{P}(S>\text{lower quantile};H_1)$ close to $1$ — worse than tossing a coin. Always check the direction on the means ($10$ versus $20$) before calibrating anything.
