Section 3 gave you the posterior $f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)$ — the complete answer to "what do the data say about $\Theta$?" — and one way of collapsing it to a single number, the MAP estimate. This section asks a different question, and gets a different answer. Suppose you must commit to one number $\hat\theta$ and you will be charged the squared distance $(\Theta-\hat\theta)^2$ between your guess and the truth. Which number minimizes your expected bill?

The answer is spectacularly clean, and it is a quantity you have known since G4 §1: the conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$. That single fact is the whole of L22 slide 1. The rest of the lecture is (i) what the resulting error looks like, (ii) what to do when $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ is too hard to compute, which in practice is most of the time, and (iii) the remarkable fact that the "cheap" replacement — the best straight line through the joint distribution — needs only five numbers: two means, two variances, and one covariance.

Interpretation. MAP and LMS answer two different questions and there is no contradiction when they disagree. MAP asks "which single value of $\theta$ is most likely?" and reports the peak of the posterior. LMS asks "which single value minimizes my expected squared miss?" and reports the center of mass of the posterior. A skewed posterior has its peak and its center of mass in different places, so the two estimates differ — you will see exactly that in §4.7, where MAP reports $\hat\theta=x$ and LMS reports the larger $(1-x)/|\ln x|$.

4.1 The problem, and the baseline with no data at all

Definition (estimator, estimate, mean squared error; B&T §8.3). An estimator is a function $g(\cdot)$ applied to the observation: the random variable $\hat\Theta=g(X)$. An estimate is the number $\hat\theta=g(x)$ it produces after $X=x$ is observed. The mean squared error of the estimator is $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-g(X))^2\big],$$ where $\Theta$ is the unknown parameter (a random variable in the Bayesian model of §3) and $X$ is the observation. The conditional mean squared error of a number $\hat\theta$, after $X=x$ has been seen, is $\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\hat\theta)^2\mid X=x\big]$.

Start with the degenerate case: no observation at all. You must pick one constant $\hat\theta$ and you are charged $(\Theta-\hat\theta)^2$ on average.

Property (best constant estimate; L21 slide 6 [DERIVATION GAP], B&T §8.3, first paragraph). $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\hat\theta)^2\big]=\operatorname{var}(\Theta)+\big(\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-\hat\theta\big)^2 ,$$ so the minimizing constant is $\hat\theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$ and the minimum value is $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$.

Derivation, every step. Write $Z=\Theta-\hat\theta$; because $\hat\theta$ is a constant, $Z$ is a random variable with $\mathbb{E}[Z]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-\hat\theta$ and, since shifting by a constant does not change spread, $\operatorname{var}(Z)=\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$. Now use the identity $\mathbb{E}[Z^2]=\operatorname{var}(Z)+(\mathbb{E}[Z])^2$ (G2 §3, the definition of variance rearranged): $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\hat\theta)^2\big]=\mathbb{E}[Z^2]=\operatorname{var}(Z)+(\mathbb{E}[Z])^2 =\operatorname{var}(\Theta)+\big(\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-\hat\theta\big)^2 .$$ The first term does not involve $\hat\theta$; the second is a square, hence $\ge 0$, and equals $0$ exactly when $\hat\theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$. So that choice minimizes, and the minimum value is $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$. $\blacksquare$

Equivalently by calculus: expand $\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-\hat\theta)^2]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2]-2\hat\theta\,\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+\hat\theta^2$, a parabola in $\hat\theta$ opening upward; set the derivative $-2\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+2\hat\theta$ to zero to get $\hat\theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$, and note the second derivative is $2>0$, confirming a minimum.

Interpretation. $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$ is the price of total ignorance. Every number quoted later in this section should be compared against it: an observation is worth having exactly to the extent that it drives the mean squared error below $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$, and §4.4 will show that the drop is precisely $\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X])$.
Practice 4.1. $\Theta$ is exponential with parameter $\lambda=2$ and you get no observation. (a) What is the best constant estimate and its mean squared error? (b) You are forced to report $\hat\theta=1$ instead. What does that cost?
Solution

(a) By the Property above the best constant is $\hat\theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=1/\lambda=1/2=0.5$ (P41_best_const) and the error is $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=1/\lambda^2=1/4=0.25$ (P41_mse).

(b) Use the identity, not a fresh integral: $$\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-1)^2]=\operatorname{var}(\Theta)+(\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-1)^2 =0.25+(0.5-1)^2=0.25+0.25=0.5$$ (P41_mse_at_1). Being off by $0.5$ in the location doubles the bill — the penalty is the square of the displacement, added on top of the irreducible variance.

4.2 The LMS theorem: $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ is optimal

Theorem (least mean squares estimation; L21 slide 7 [DERIVATION GAP], L22 slide 1, B&T §8.3).
  1. Conditional form. For any fixed value $x$ of the observation, $\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\hat\theta)^2\mid X=x\big]$ is minimized over all numbers $\hat\theta$ by $$\hat\theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x],$$ and the minimum value is $\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=x)$, the variance of the posterior.
  2. Unconditional form. Out of all estimators $g(\cdot)$, $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-g(X))^2\big]\ \ge\ \mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X])^2\big],$$ so $\hat\Theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ minimizes the mean squared error. Its value is $\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)\big]$.

The slides state both halves and prove neither: L21 slide 7 writes down the three-line chain (conditional inequality at a fixed $x$, then the same inequality with $x$ replaced by $X$, then the unconditional inequality) and boxes the conclusion in red, but flags the passage between the lines as a gap. Here is the whole argument.

Part 1, every step. Condition on the event $\{X=x\}$ and work inside that new universe. All of §4.1 is available there, because a conditional distribution is a distribution: it has a mean $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$ and a variance $\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=x)$, and the identity $\mathbb{E}[Z^2\mid X=x]=\operatorname{var}(Z\mid X=x)+(\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X=x])^2$ holds verbatim. With $Z=\Theta-\hat\theta$ and $\hat\theta$ a constant, $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\hat\theta)^2\mid X=x\big] =\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=x)+\big(\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]-\hat\theta\big)^2 .$$ The first term is fixed once $x$ is known; the second is a square. It vanishes exactly when $\hat\theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$, which is therefore the minimizer, with minimum value $\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=x)$. $\blacksquare$

Part 2, every step. Let $g(\cdot)$ be any estimator. Fix $x$. Then $g(x)$ is a number, so Part 1 applies to it: $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x])^2\mid X=x\big]\ \le\ \mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-g(x))^2\mid X=x\big]\qquad\text{for every }x. \tag{$\ast$}$$ Both sides of $(\ast)$ are functions of $x$. Replace $x$ by the random variable $X$: now both sides are random variables, and $(\ast)$ says one is $\le$ the other for every outcome, i.e. pointwise on the sample space. Inequalities that hold pointwise survive taking expectations, so $$\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X])^2\mid X\big]\Big]\ \le\ \mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-g(X))^2\mid X\big]\Big].$$ Finally apply the law of iterated expectations (G4 §1) to each side — $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]\big]=\mathbb{E}[Y]$ with $Y$ the respective squared error — to strip the outer conditioning: $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X])^2\big]\ \le\ \mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-g(X))^2\big].$$ That is the claim. Taking $g$ to be the optimal estimator and using the value from Part 1, the optimal mean squared error is $\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)\big]$. $\blacksquare$

Interpretation. Part 1 is the entire content of the theorem; Part 2 is bookkeeping. If you have found the best estimate separately for every possible $x$, you have found the best rule, because nothing links the different values of $x$ — the estimator is free to do whatever it likes at each one. This is why LMS estimation, unlike almost everything else in statistics, has a closed-form answer.
Gotcha. "$\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ minimizes the mean squared error" is a statement about squared error only. Change the penalty and the answer changes: the absolute error $\mathbb{E}[\,|\Theta-\hat\theta|\,]$ is minimized by the posterior median, and the 0–1 penalty (in the discrete case) is minimized by the posterior mode, which is the MAP estimate. The three coincide when the posterior is symmetric and unimodal, and only then.
Gotcha. $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ is a random variable (a function of $X$); $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$ is a number. The theorem's Part 1 is about the number, Part 2 about the random variable. Writing $\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)$ likewise means the random variable whose value at $X=x$ is the posterior variance — which is why the optimal error $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)]$ has an outer expectation.
Practice 4.2. $\Theta$ and $X$ are both $\{0,1\}$-valued with joint PMF $p_{\Theta,X}(0,0)=0.4$, $p_{\Theta,X}(0,1)=0.1$, $p_{\Theta,X}(1,0)=0.2$, $p_{\Theta,X}(1,1)=0.3$. (a) Find the LMS estimator. (b) Find its mean squared error and compare with the no-data baseline. (c) A colleague insists the estimate "must be $0$ or $1$, since $\Theta$ is", and rounds your answer. What does the rounding cost?
Solution

(a) Marginals: $\mathbf{P}(X=0)=0.4+0.2=0.6$ (P42_pX0) and $\mathbf{P}(X=1)=0.1+0.3=0.4$ (P42_pX1). For a $\{0,1\}$-valued $\Theta$ the conditional mean equals the conditional probability of $1$: $$\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=0]=\mathbf{P}(\Theta=1\mid X=0)=\frac{0.2}{0.6}=\frac13=0.333333,\qquad \mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=1]=\frac{0.3}{0.4}=0.75$$ (P42_E_given0, P42_E_given1).

(b) For a Bernoulli conditional distribution with parameter $q$ the variance is $q(1-q)$, so $$\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)]=0.6\cdot\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac23+0.4\cdot0.75\cdot0.25 =0.133333+0.075=0.208333$$ (P42_mse_opt). The baseline is $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=\mathbf{P}(\Theta=1) \mathbf{P}(\Theta=0)=0.5\times0.5=0.25$ (P42_var_prior). The observation buys a reduction of $0.0417$ — this $X$ is only mildly informative.

(c) Rounding gives $g(0)=0$, $g(1)=1$ (this is the MAP rule here). Its squared error is $1$ whenever the guess is wrong and $0$ otherwise, so its mean squared error equals the probability of being wrong: $$\mathbf{P}(X=0)\mathbf{P}(\Theta=1\mid X=0)+\mathbf{P}(X=1)\mathbf{P}(\Theta=0\mid X=1) =0.6\cdot\tfrac13+0.4\cdot0.25=0.2+0.1=0.3$$ (P42_mse_round). That is worse than the no-data baseline $0.25$. Under squared error a fractional estimate is not a failure of nerve; it is the optimum, and rounding it to a "legal" value of $\Theta$ can cost you more than ignoring the data.

4.3 The lecture's uniform example, worked end to end

Example 4.1 (L22 slides 2–3 and 5, [UNSOLVED EXAMPLE]; = B&T Example 8.11). $\Theta$ is uniform on $[4,10]$. Given $\Theta=\theta$, the observation is $X=\Theta+W$ where $W$ is uniform on $[-1,1]$ and independent of $\Theta$; equivalently $X\mid\Theta=\theta$ is uniform on $[\theta-1,\theta+1]$. The slides draw the prior, the conditional density and the joint support, and stop. Find the posterior, the LMS estimate $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$ for every $x$, the conditional mean squared error, and the overall mean squared error.
Solution

Step 1 — the two given densities. The prior is flat on an interval of length $10-4=6$, so its height is the reciprocal: $$f_\Theta(\theta)=\frac{1}{6}=0.166667\quad\text{for }4\le\theta\le10,\qquad 0\text{ otherwise}$$ (L22_prior_h; this is the $1/6$ printed on the slide's top figure). The conditional density is flat on an interval of length $(\theta+1)-(\theta-1)=2$, so $$f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)=\frac{1}{2}=0.5\quad\text{for }\theta-1\le x\le\theta+1$$ (L22_cond_h; the $1/2$ on the slide's middle figure).

Step 2 — the joint density and its support. By the multiplication rule for densities (G3 §2), $f_{\Theta,X}(\theta,x)=f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$, so $$f_{\Theta,X}(\theta,x)=\frac16\cdot\frac12=\frac{1}{12}=0.083333$$ (L22_joint_h) on the set where both factors are nonzero, namely $$4\le\theta\le10\quad\text{and}\quad \theta-1\le x\le\theta+1 ,$$ and zero elsewhere. That set is the slanted parallelogram of the slide: two horizontal edges at $\theta=4$ (spanning $x\in[3,5]$) and $\theta=10$ (spanning $x\in[9,11]$), joined by the two parallel lines $x=\theta-1$ and $x=\theta+1$ of slope $1$. Its area is base $\times$ height $=2\times 6=12$, and $12\times\frac{1}{12}=1$, confirming the density integrates to one.

Step 3 — the posterior is uniform on the vertical slice. Bayes' rule for densities (§3, B&T §8.1) gives $$f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=\frac{f_{\Theta,X}(\theta,x)}{f_X(x)} .$$ The numerator is the constant $1/12$ for every $\theta$ in the slice $$S(x)=\{\theta:\ 4\le\theta\le10\ \text{and}\ \theta-1\le x\le\theta+1\} =[\max(4,\,x-1),\ \min(10,\,x+1)]$$ — the second description follows by rewriting $\theta-1\le x\le\theta+1$ as $x-1\le\theta\le x+1$ and intersecting with $[4,10]$. The denominator $f_X(x)$ does not depend on $\theta$. So the posterior is constant in $\theta$ on $S(x)$ and zero off it: the posterior is uniform on $S(x)$. This is the payoff of a constant joint density, and it is what the slide's picture is for; Figure 4.1 below draws it.

Step 4 — the marginal $f_X$, for completeness. Integrating the joint density over the slice multiplies $1/12$ by the slice length $\ell(x)=\min(10,x+1)-\max(4,x-1)$: $$f_X(x)=\frac{\ell(x)}{12}=\begin{cases} \dfrac{x-3}{12}, & 3\le x<5\quad(\text{left edge, }S(x)=[4,x+1]),\\[6pt] \dfrac{2}{12}=\dfrac16=0.166667, & 5\le x\le 9\quad(\text{interior, }S(x)=[x-1,x+1]),\\[6pt] \dfrac{11-x}{12}, & 9<x\le 11\quad(\text{right edge, }S(x)=[x-1,10]). \end{cases}$$ Checkpoints: $f_X(4)=1/12=0.083333$ (L22_fX_4), $f_X(7)=1/6=0.166667$ (L22_fX_7), $f_X(10)=1/12=0.083333$ (L22_fX_10), and numerical integration over $[3,11]$ returns $1.000000$ (L22_fX_total).

Step 5 — the LMS estimate. The mean of a uniform distribution on $[\alpha,\beta]$ is the midpoint $(\alpha+\beta)/2$, so $$\boxed{\ \mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]=\frac{\max(4,x-1)+\min(10,x+1)}{2}\ } =\begin{cases} \dfrac{x+5}{2}, & 3\le x<5,\\[6pt] x, & 5\le x\le 9,\\[6pt] \dfrac{x+9}{2}, & 9<x\le 11. \end{cases}$$ Numerically: $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=3.5]=(3.5+5)/2=4.25$ (L22_lms_3p5), $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=4]=4.5$ (L22_lms_4), $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=7]=7$ (L22_lms_7), $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=10]=9.5$ (L22_lms_10).

Step 6 — the conditional mean squared error. By the theorem of §4.2 this is the posterior variance, and a uniform distribution on an interval of length $\ell$ has variance $\ell^2/12$: $$\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=x)=\frac{\ell(x)^2}{12}=\begin{cases} (x-3)^2/12, & 3\le x<5,\\ 4/12=1/3=0.333333, & 5\le x\le 9,\\ (11-x)^2/12, & 9<x\le 11. \end{cases}$$ So $\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=4)=1/12=0.083333$ (L22_cvar_4), $\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=7)=1/3=0.333333$ (L22_cvar_mid), $\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=10)=1/12=0.083333$ (L22_cvar_10). The two extreme observations are the most informative: $x=3$ forces $\Theta=4$ exactly and gives zero error, and $x=11$ forces $\Theta=10$. This filling of the slide's blank lower frame is exactly the content of B&T Example 8.11 and its Figure 8.9, and it is plotted as Figure 4.2 below.

Step 7 — the overall mean squared error. Average the conditional error against $f_X$: $$\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)\big]=\int_3^{11}\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=x)f_X(x)\,dx .$$ Split at $x=5$ and $x=9$. On the interior band the integrand is the constant $\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac16=\tfrac1{18}$ over a stretch of length $4$, contributing $\tfrac{4}{18}=\tfrac29=0.222222$ (L22_mse_mid). On the left edge band, substituting $u=x-3$ so $u$ runs from $0$ to $2$, $$\int_3^5\frac{(x-3)^2}{12}\cdot\frac{x-3}{12}\,dx=\int_0^2\frac{u^3}{144}\,du =\frac{1}{144}\cdot\frac{2^4}{4}=\frac{4}{144}=\frac{1}{36}=0.027778$$ (L22_mse_edge), and the right edge band gives the same by the mirror symmetry $x\mapsto 14-x$. Total: $$\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)\big]=\frac{2}{9}+\frac{2}{36}=\frac{4}{18}+\frac{1}{18} =\frac{5}{18}=0.277778$$ (L22_mse_lms, confirmed by quadrature and by a $4\times10^6$-sample simulation, L22_mse_lms_mc $=0.277184$). Compare the no-data baseline $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=(10-4)^2/12=3$ (L22_prior_var): the observation cuts the error by a factor of $10.8$.

Practice 4.3. In Example 4.1 you observe $x=3.5$. (a) Describe the posterior and give the LMS estimate and its conditional mean squared error. (b) An engineer argues that since $X=\Theta+W$ with $\mathbb{E}[W]=0$, the estimate should just be $3.5$. What does that cost, and why is the penalty so large here when it is zero at $x=7$?
Solution

(a) The slice is $S(3.5)=[\max(4,2.5),\ \min(10,4.5)]=[4,4.5]$, of length $\ell=0.5$; the posterior is uniform on it, with height $1/0.5=2$. Hence $$\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=3.5]=\frac{4+4.5}{2}=4.25,\qquad \operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=3.5)=\frac{0.5^2}{12}=0.020833$$ (L22_lms_3p5, L22_cvar_3p5). This is one of the sharpest observations available: the conditional error is $16$ times smaller than the interior value $1/3$.

(b) By the identity of §4.2, for any number $\hat\theta$, $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\hat\theta)^2\mid X=3.5\big] =\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=3.5)+\big(4.25-\hat\theta\big)^2 =0.020833+(4.25-3.5)^2=0.020833+0.5625=0.583333$$ (L22_naive_mse_3p5) — twenty-eight times the optimal error. The reason is that $x=3.5$ is not a possible value of $\Theta$ at all: $\Theta\ge4$ always, so any estimate below $4$ is known in advance to be wrong. Near the middle of the range the prior's boundaries are out of reach, the slice is symmetric about $x$, and "report $x$" is exactly optimal — which is why the penalty vanishes at $x=7$. For comparison, the best line of Example 4.2 reports $0.9\times3.5+0.7=3.85$ (L22_lin_at_3p5) here: better than $3.5$, still below the feasible floor of $4$.

4.4 Properties of the estimation error

Definition (estimator and estimation error; L22 slide 4). Write $$\hat\Theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X],\qquad \tilde\Theta=\hat\Theta-\Theta .$$ $\hat\Theta$ is the LMS estimator — a random variable, being a function of $X$. $\tilde\Theta$ is the estimation error, the (signed) amount by which the estimator overshoots the truth. Note $\Theta=\hat\Theta-\tilde\Theta$, which is the identity slide 4 uses.
Property (L22 slide 4, B&T §8.3). With $\hat\Theta,\tilde\Theta$ as above:
  1. $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta\mid X=x]=0$ for every $x$, and hence $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta]=0$ (the error is unbiased, conditionally and unconditionally);
  2. orthogonality: $\mathbb{E}\big[\tilde\Theta\,h(X)\big]=0$ for every function $h$;
  3. $\operatorname{cov}(\tilde\Theta,\hat\Theta)=0$;
  4. variance decomposition: $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta)+\operatorname{var}(\tilde\Theta)$, and the mean squared error is $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta^2]=\operatorname{var}(\tilde\Theta) =\operatorname{var}(\Theta)-\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta)$.

The slide lists all four with no proof. Each is two lines.

(1). Condition on $\{X=x\}$. Inside that universe $\hat\Theta$ is not random: it takes the known value $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$. Therefore $$\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta\mid X=x]=\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta-\Theta\mid X=x] =\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]}_{\text{the constant }\hat\Theta}-\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]=0,$$ using linearity of conditional expectation. Since this holds for every $x$, the random variable $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta\mid X]$ is identically $0$; applying the law of iterated expectations (G4 §1), $$\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta\mid X]\big]=\mathbb{E}[0]=0 .\qquad\blacksquare$$

(2). Let $h$ be any function for which the expectation exists. Use iterated expectations again, and then the fact that $h(X)$ is a constant inside the conditional universe $\{X=x\}$ so it pulls out of the inner expectation: $$\mathbb{E}\big[\tilde\Theta\,h(X)\big] =\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\big[\tilde\Theta\,h(X)\mid X\big]\Big] =\mathbb{E}\Big[h(X)\,\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta\mid X]}_{=\,0\text{ by (1)}}\Big] =\mathbb{E}[0]=0 .\qquad\blacksquare$$

(3). By definition $\operatorname{cov}(\tilde\Theta,\hat\Theta) =\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta\hat\Theta]-\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta]\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta]$. The second term vanishes because $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta]=0$ by (1). The first term is the special case $h(X)=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ of (2) — legitimate precisely because $\hat\Theta$ is a function of $X$ — so it too is $0$. Hence $\operatorname{cov}(\tilde\Theta,\hat\Theta)=0$. $\blacksquare$

(4). Take variances in $\Theta=\hat\Theta-\tilde\Theta$. For any two random variables, $\operatorname{var}(U-V)=\operatorname{var}(U)+\operatorname{var}(V)-2\operatorname{cov}(U,V)$ (G3 §4), so $$\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta)+\operatorname{var}(\tilde\Theta) -2\underbrace{\operatorname{cov}(\hat\Theta,\tilde\Theta)}_{=\,0\text{ by (3)}} =\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta)+\operatorname{var}(\tilde\Theta).$$ And because $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta]=0$, the mean squared error $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta^2]=\operatorname{var}(\tilde\Theta)+(\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta])^2 =\operatorname{var}(\tilde\Theta)$. Rearranging the decomposition, $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta^2]=\operatorname{var}(\Theta)-\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta)$. $\blacksquare$

Check on Example 4.1. $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=3$ and $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta^2]=5/18=0.277778$, so the decomposition predicts $\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta)=3-5/18=49/18=2.722222$ (L22_var_hat) — the estimator reproduces $90.7\%$ of the parameter's variability, and the leftover $9.3\%$ is irreducible.

Fig. 4.3 — The orthogonality property as geometry. Think of random variables as vectors with inner product $\langle U,V\rangle=\mathbb{E}[UV]$. If it were not — if some $h(X)$ still correlated with $\tilde\Theta$ — you could shave a multiple of $h(X)$ off your estimate and do better, contradicting optimality. Practice 4.4. Construct a pair $(\Theta,X)$ in which $X$ is uninformative — the LMS estimator has the same mean squared error as the no-data estimate — yet $\Theta$ and $X$ are not independent. (This is the question B&T Example 8.14 poses and leaves as "Can you construct an example?")
Solution

By property (4), $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta^2]=\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$ if and only if $\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta)=0$, i.e. the random variable $\hat\Theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ is almost surely constant; since $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$ (iterated expectations), that constant must be $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$. So we need $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$ for every $x$, without independence.

Take $\Theta$ uniform on $\{-1,0,1\}$ and $X=\Theta^2\in\{0,1\}$. Then $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=0$ and $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2]=\frac13(1+0+1)=\frac23 =0.666667$ (P44_var). Conditioning: given $X=0$ we know $\Theta=0$, so $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=0]=0$; given $X=1$, $\Theta$ is equally likely $-1$ or $+1$, so $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=1]=\frac12(-1)+\frac12(1)=0$. Both equal $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=0$, so $\hat\Theta\equiv 0$ and the mean squared error is the full $2/3$: the observation is useless for estimating $\Theta$ under squared error.

But $\Theta$ and $X$ are wildly dependent: $\mathbf{P}(\Theta=0\mid X=0)=1$ while $\mathbf{P}(\Theta=0)=1/3$. The data pin down $|\Theta|$ perfectly and say nothing about its sign, and squared-error estimation of $\Theta$ only cares about the sign. Uninformative-for-a-purpose is much weaker than independent.

4.5 Linear LMS estimation

Everything above is exact and, in practice, often unusable. To compute $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ you need the entire joint distribution $f_{\Theta,X}$, and even when you have it the answer can be a function no one wants to implement. L22 slide 5 makes the pragmatic retreat: keep the squared-error criterion, but search only within the family of straight lines.

Theorem (linear LMS; L22 slides 5–6, B&T §8.4). Among all estimators of the form $aX+b$, the mean squared error $\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-aX-b)^2\big]$ is minimized by $$a=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)}{\operatorname{var}(X)}=\rho\frac{\sigma_\Theta}{\sigma_X}, \qquad b=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-a\,\mathbb{E}[X],$$ that is, by $$\boxed{\ \hat\Theta_L=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+\frac{\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)}{\operatorname{var}(X)} \big(X-\mathbb{E}[X]\big)\ }$$ and the resulting mean squared error is $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat\Theta_L-\Theta)^2\big]=(1-\rho^2)\,\sigma_\Theta^2, \qquad \rho=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)}{\sigma_\Theta\sigma_X}.$$ Here $\sigma_\Theta^2=\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$ and $\sigma_X^2=\operatorname{var}(X)$ are the two variances and $\rho$ is the correlation coefficient of $\Theta$ and $X$ (G3 §4).

The slides quote both formulas and derive neither; slide 6 does not even define $\rho$. Here is the derivation, in the order the book takes it — $b$ first, then $a$.

Step 1 — minimize over $b$ with $a$ held fixed. Group the terms as $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-aX-b)^2\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\big((\Theta-aX)-b\big)^2\big].$$ For fixed $a$ the quantity $Y=\Theta-aX$ is a perfectly ordinary random variable, and we are choosing a constant $b$ to estimate it under squared error. That is precisely the problem of §4.1, so the answer is $$b=\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta-aX]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-a\,\mathbb{E}[X],$$ by linearity of expectation, and the minimum value is $$\min_b \mathbb{E}\big[(Y-b)^2\big]=\operatorname{var}(Y)=\operatorname{var}(\Theta-aX).$$

Step 2 — expand that variance. Using $\operatorname{var}(U+V)=\operatorname{var}(U)+\operatorname{var}(V)+2\operatorname{cov}(U,V)$ with $U=\Theta$, $V=-aX$, and the bilinearity rules $\operatorname{var}(-aX)=a^2\operatorname{var}(X)$ and $\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,-aX)=-a\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)$ (G3 §4): $$\operatorname{var}(\Theta-aX)=\sigma_\Theta^2+a^2\sigma_X^2-2a\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X).$$ This is a quadratic in $a$ with positive leading coefficient $\sigma_X^2$, so it has a unique minimum.

Step 3 — minimize over $a$. Differentiate with respect to $a$ and set to zero: $$\frac{d}{da}\Big[\sigma_\Theta^2+a^2\sigma_X^2-2a\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)\Big] =2a\sigma_X^2-2\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)}{\sigma_X^2}=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)}{\operatorname{var}(X)} .$$ The second derivative is $2\sigma_X^2>0$, so this is the minimum. Substituting $\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)=\rho\,\sigma_\Theta\sigma_X$ gives the alternative form $a=\rho\,\sigma_\Theta/\sigma_X$.

Step 4 — assemble the estimator. With those $a$ and $b$, $$\hat\Theta_L=aX+b=aX+\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-a\mathbb{E}[X] =\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+a\big(X-\mathbb{E}[X]\big) =\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+\frac{\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)}{\operatorname{var}(X)}\big(X-\mathbb{E}[X]\big),$$ which is the boxed formula of slide 5.

Step 5 — the error. Put $a=\rho\sigma_\Theta/\sigma_X$ back into the Step-2 expression and substitute $\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)=\rho\sigma_\Theta\sigma_X$ everywhere: $$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\big[(\hat\Theta_L-\Theta)^2\big] &=\sigma_\Theta^2+a^2\sigma_X^2-2a\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)\\ &=\sigma_\Theta^2+\Big(\rho\frac{\sigma_\Theta}{\sigma_X}\Big)^2\sigma_X^2 -2\Big(\rho\frac{\sigma_\Theta}{\sigma_X}\Big)\big(\rho\,\sigma_\Theta\sigma_X\big)\\ &=\sigma_\Theta^2+\rho^2\sigma_\Theta^2-2\rho^2\sigma_\Theta^2\\ &=(1-\rho^2)\,\sigma_\Theta^2 . \end{aligned}$$ The middle line cancels $\sigma_X^2$ against $1/\sigma_X^2$ in the second term and $\sigma_X$ against $1/\sigma_X$ in the third — the whole point of writing $a$ through $\rho$. $\blacksquare$

Interpretation. Read the formula as a correction, not a computation. Start from the prior mean $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$ — the best guess with no data. Then move away from it in proportion to how surprising the observation was, $X-\mathbb{E}[X]$, with gain $\operatorname{cov}/\operatorname{var}(X)$. The error formula $(1-\rho^2)\sigma_\Theta^2$ prices the whole exercise: with $\rho=0$ the gain is zero, the estimator collapses to $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$ and you pay the full $\sigma_\Theta^2$; with $|\rho|=1$ the error is $0$, because $\Theta$ is then an exact affine function of $X$. Neither of those two remarks appears anywhere on slide 6.
Gotcha. $\rho$ measures linear association only. A model can have $\rho=0$ and yet $X$ determines $\Theta$ almost completely — the widget below is built on exactly such a case. When $\rho=0$ the linear LMS estimator throws the data away, while the true LMS estimator $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ may have near-zero error. "Uncorrelated" never means "uninformative".
Gotcha. $\hat\Theta_L$ can land outside the range of $\Theta$. A line is unbounded and the parameter usually is not; §4.7 exhibits an estimator of a quantity known to lie in $[0,1]$ that reports $8/7=1.142857$ when $x=1$. The exact LMS estimator can never do this, since a conditional mean of a variable confined to $[0,1]$ lies in $[0,1]$.
Example 4.2 (Example 4.1 continued; L22 slide 5). Find the linear LMS estimator for $\Theta\sim U[4,10]$, $X=\Theta+W$, $W\sim U[-1,1]$ independent, and compare it with the exact LMS estimator found in §4.3.
Solution

The five numbers. Everything the formula needs:

  • $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=(4+10)/2=7$ (L22_prior_mean) and $\sigma_\Theta^2=\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=(10-4)^2/12=36/12=3$ (L22_prior_var), the uniform mean and variance from G3 §1.
  • $\operatorname{var}(W)=(1-(-1))^2/12=4/12=1/3=0.333333$ (L22_W_var) and $\mathbb{E}[W]=0$.
  • $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta+W]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+\mathbb{E}[W]=7+0=7$ (L22_EX), by linearity.
  • $\operatorname{var}(X)=\operatorname{var}(\Theta+W)=\operatorname{var}(\Theta)+\operatorname{var}(W) =3+\tfrac13=\tfrac{10}{3}=3.333333$ (L22_varX) — the cross term vanishes because $\Theta$ and $W$ are independent.
  • $\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)=\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,\Theta+W) =\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,\Theta)+\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,W) =\operatorname{var}(\Theta)+0=3$ (L22_cov), again by independence.

The coefficients. $$a=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)}{\operatorname{var}(X)}=\frac{3}{10/3}=\frac{9}{10}=0.9, \qquad b=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-a\,\mathbb{E}[X]=7-0.9\times 7=7-6.3=0.7$$ (L22_a, L22_b), so $$\hat\Theta_L=0.9\,X+0.7 .$$ The error. $$\rho^2=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)^2}{\sigma_\Theta^2\sigma_X^2} =\frac{3^2}{3\times 10/3}=\frac{9}{10}=0.9,\qquad \rho=0.948683$$ (L22_rho2, L22_rho), hence $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat\Theta_L-\Theta)^2\big]=(1-0.9)\times 3=0.3$$ (L22_mse_lin; simulation gives $0.299972$, L22_mse_lin_mc).

The comparison. The exact LMS estimator achieves $5/18=0.277778$; the best line achieves $0.3$. The excess is $0.3-5/18=1/45=0.022222$ (L22_mse_gap) — the line is $8\%$ worse, a small price for an estimator that needed five numbers instead of a full posterior analysis. Where does the loss come from? Only near the ends: the two agree at $x=7$ ($7$ versus $7$), but at $x=4$ the line says $4.3$ while the truth says $4.5$, and at $x=10$ the line says $9.7$ against $9.5$ (L22_lin_at_4, L22_lin_at_7, L22_lin_at_10). The largest discrepancy is $0.6$, at the endpoint $x=11$ (L22_maxgap, L22_maxgap_at), where the line predicts $10.6$ for a parameter that cannot exceed $10$.

When is the line exact?

Property (jointly normal case; L22 slide 8 "if all normal", B&T §8.4). If $\Theta$ and $X$ are jointly normal, then $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ is an affine function of $X$, and therefore $$\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]=\hat\Theta_L=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+\rho\frac{\sigma_\Theta}{\sigma_X} \big(X-\mathbb{E}[X]\big), \qquad \operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=x)=(1-\rho^2)\sigma_\Theta^2\ \text{ for every }x.$$ The restriction to linear estimators costs nothing: the LMS and linear LMS estimators are the same function. The same holds with several observations, $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X_1,\ldots,X_n]=\hat\Theta_L$, whenever $\Theta,X_1,\ldots,X_n$ are jointly normal.

This is the reason linear estimation is not merely a shortcut but the natural language of an entire class of models. Note the second half of the statement: in the normal case the conditional variance does not depend on $x$ at all, so the conditional-MSE plot of Fig. 4.2 would be flat.

Fig. 4.5 — Jointly normal $\Theta,X$ with $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=2$, $\sigma_\Theta=1.5$, $\mathbb{E}[X]=5$, $\sigma_X=2$, $\rho=0.8$. Then $a=\rho\sigma_\Theta/\sigma_X=0.6$ and $b=2-0.6\times5=-1$; the binned empirical conditional means (green) sit on the line, confirming $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]=0.6X-1$. Practice 4.5. $\Theta$ has mean $5$ and variance $4$. You observe $X=\Theta+W$ where $W$ has mean $0$, variance $1$, and is independent of $\Theta$; no distributional form is assumed. (a) Find the linear LMS estimator and its mean squared error. (b) What is the estimate when $x=7$? (c) Why is the estimate not $7$?
Solution

(a) The five numbers, exactly as in Example 4.2: $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=5$, $\sigma_\Theta^2=4$, $\mathbb{E}[X]=5+0=5$, $\operatorname{var}(X)=4+1=5$, $\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)=\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=4$. Hence $$a=\frac{4}{5}=0.8,\qquad b=5-0.8\times5=1,\qquad \hat\Theta_L=0.8X+1 .$$ (P45_a, P45_b.) Also $\rho^2=\dfrac{4^2}{4\times5}=\dfrac{16}{20}=0.8$ (P45_rho2), so the error is $(1-0.8)\times4=0.8$ (P45_mse) against a baseline of $4$ — the observation removes $80\%$ of the variance.

(b) $\hat\theta=0.8\times7+1=6.6$ (P45_est_at_7).

(c) Because $X$ is a noisy read-out and the prior still carries information. The estimate sits $1.6$ above the prior mean $5$ while the observation sits $2$ above it: the estimator shrinks the observation back toward the prior by the factor $a=\sigma_\Theta^2/(\sigma_\Theta^2+\sigma_W^2)=4/5$, which is the fraction of the observation's variability that is genuine signal rather than noise. Reporting $x$ itself would be optimal only if the noise variance were $0$.

Practice 4.6. Show that the linear LMS error satisfies $0\le(1-\rho^2)\sigma_\Theta^2\le\sigma_\Theta^2$, and say exactly when each bound is attained.
Solution

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (G3 §4) $|\rho|\le1$, so $0\le\rho^2\le1$ and hence $0\le1-\rho^2\le1$; multiplying by $\sigma_\Theta^2\ge0$ preserves the inequalities.

Upper bound attained when $\rho=0$: then $a=0$, the estimator is the constant $\hat\Theta_L=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$, and the error is the no-data baseline $\sigma_\Theta^2$ of §4.1. Lower bound attained when $|\rho|=1$, which by the equality case of Cauchy–Schwarz happens exactly when $\Theta=aX+b$ for some constants — the relationship is deterministic and affine, and the estimator is exact with zero error. A useful sanity check: the linear LMS error can never exceed the no-data error, because the constant estimator is itself a member of the family $aX+b$ (take $a=0$).

4.6 Several observations, and choosing what to feed the estimator

L21 slide 8 already posed this case and stopped: with several observations the best estimator is $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X_1,\ldots,X_n]$, but it "can be hard to compute/implement — involves multi-dimensional integrals, etc." That is the whole motivation for going linear here. L22 slide 7 states the general problem — estimators $\hat\Theta=a_1X_1+\cdots+a_nX_n+b$, minimize $\mathbb{E}\big[(a_1X_1+\cdots+a_nX_n+b-\Theta)^2\big]$, "set derivatives to zero, linear system in $b$ and the $a_i$" — without writing the system down. Here it is.

The normal equations. Let $J(a_1,\ldots,a_n,b)=\mathbb{E}[e^2]$ with the residual $e=\sum_i a_iX_i+b-\Theta$. Differentiating under the expectation: $$\frac{\partial J}{\partial b}=2\,\mathbb{E}[e]=0,\qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial a_j}=2\,\mathbb{E}[e\,X_j]=0\quad (j=1,\ldots,n).$$ The first says the residual has mean zero, giving $b=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-\sum_i a_i\mathbb{E}[X_i]$. Substituting that into the second and using $\mathbb{E}[eX_j]=\operatorname{cov}(e,X_j)$ (valid because $\mathbb{E}[e]=0$) turns them into $$\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\operatorname{cov}(X_i,X_j)=\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X_j),\qquad j=1,\ldots,n,$$ an $n\times n$ linear system in $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ whose coefficients are covariances and nothing else. This is slide 7's "only means, variances, covariances matter", made explicit — and note that the equations $\mathbb{E}[e]=0$, $\mathbb{E}[eX_j]=0$ are exactly the orthogonality property of §4.4 restricted to the functions $h(X)=1$ and $h(X)=X_j$, which is all the linear family can "see".

Example 4.3 (L22 slide 8, [DERIVATION GAP]: the cleanest case). $X_i=\Theta+W_i$ for $i=1,\ldots,n$, with $\Theta,W_1,\ldots,W_n$ all independent, $\Theta$ having mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma_0^2$ and $W_i$ having mean $0$ and variance $\sigma_i^2$ (no distributional form assumed). Derive the slide's answer $$\hat\Theta_L=\frac{\mu/\sigma_0^2+\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i/\sigma_i^2} {\sum_{i=0}^{n}1/\sigma_i^2}$$ and find its mean squared error.
Solution

Step 1 — reduce to a clean minimization. Apply the $b$-first argument of §4.5: for any fixed $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ the best $b$ is $\mathbb{E}[\Theta-\sum a_iX_i]=\mu-\mu\sum_i a_i$, since $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=\mu+0=\mu$ for every $i$. With that $b$, the residual is $$\sum_i a_iX_i+b-\Theta=\sum_i a_i(X_i-\mu)-(\Theta-\mu) =\sum_i a_i\big[(\Theta-\mu)+W_i\big]-(\Theta-\mu) =\Big(\sum_i a_i-1\Big)(\Theta-\mu)+\sum_i a_iW_i .$$ Step 2 — its mean square. The $n+1$ random variables $\Theta-\mu,W_1,\ldots,W_n$ are independent with mean zero, so all cross terms vanish and the variances add: $$J(a)=\Big(\sum_i a_i-1\Big)^2\sigma_0^2+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^2\sigma_i^2 .$$ Step 3 — set the derivatives to zero. For each $j$, $$\frac{\partial J}{\partial a_j}=2\Big(\sum_i a_i-1\Big)\sigma_0^2+2a_j\sigma_j^2=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_j\sigma_j^2=-\Big(\sum_i a_i-1\Big)\sigma_0^2 .$$ The right-hand side does not depend on $j$. Call it $\kappa$; then $$a_j=\frac{\kappa}{\sigma_j^2}\quad\text{for every }j .$$ Step 4 — solve for $\kappa$. Summing, $\sum_i a_i=\kappa\sum_{i=1}^n 1/\sigma_i^2$. Feed that back into the definition $\kappa=\big(1-\sum_i a_i\big)\sigma_0^2$: $$\frac{\kappa}{\sigma_0^2}=1-\kappa\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sigma_i^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \kappa\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sigma_i^2}\Big)=1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \kappa=\frac{1}{S},\qquad S:=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{1}{\sigma_i^2}.$$ Step 5 — read off the estimator. $a_j=(1/\sigma_j^2)/S$, and $$b=\mu\Big(1-\sum_i a_i\Big)=\mu\cdot\frac{\kappa}{\sigma_0^2}=\frac{\mu/\sigma_0^2}{S},$$ so $$\hat\Theta_L=b+\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_jX_j =\frac{\mu/\sigma_0^2+\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_j/\sigma_j^2}{\sum_{i=0}^{n}1/\sigma_i^2},$$ exactly slide 8, including the detail that the numerator sum starts at $i=1$ while the denominator sum starts at $i=0$.

Step 6 — the error. Substitute $a_j=\kappa/\sigma_j^2$ and $\sum_i a_i-1=-\kappa/\sigma_0^2$ into $J$: $$J=\frac{\kappa^2}{\sigma_0^4}\sigma_0^2+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\kappa^2}{\sigma_j^4}\sigma_j^2 =\kappa^2\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sigma_j^2}\Big) =\kappa^2 S=\frac{S}{S^2}=\frac{1}{S}.$$ So $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat\Theta_L-\Theta)^2\big]=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=0}^{n}1/\sigma_i^{2}} .$$

Interpretation: precisions add. Call $1/\sigma^2$ the precision of a source. The estimate is the precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the observations, and the precision of the result is the sum of the precisions that went in. Each extra measurement, however noisy, strictly increases $S$ and therefore strictly decreases the error — but a measurement with $\sigma_i^2$ ten times larger contributes a tenth as much. The prior enters on exactly the same footing as a measurement, as one more "observation" of precision $1/\sigma_0^2$. Slide 8 adds that if everything is normal, this linear estimator is the exact conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X_1,\ldots,X_n]$.

Numerical check. Take $\mu=5$, $\sigma_0=2$, three sensors with $\sigma_1=1$, $\sigma_2=2$, $\sigma_3=3$, and readings $x=(6,4,9)$. Then $S=\tfrac14+1+\tfrac14+\tfrac19=1.611111$ (MO_prec), the numerator is $\tfrac54+6+1+1=9.25$ (MO_num), so $$\hat\theta_L=\frac{9.25}{1.611111}=5.741379,\qquad \text{MSE}=\frac{1}{1.611111}=0.620690$$ (MO_est, MO_mse). The weights are $0.620690$ on $x_1$, $0.155172$ on $x_2$, $0.068966$ on $x_3$ and $0.155172$ on the prior mean $\mu$ (MO_w1–MO_w0): the sharpest sensor gets four times the weight of the middling one and nine times that of the worst, and it alone outweighs everything else combined. Solving the general normal equations of this subsection numerically with $\operatorname{cov}(X_i,X_j)=\sigma_0^2+\delta_{ij}\sigma_i^2$ and $\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X_j)=\sigma_0^2$ reproduces both numbers to machine precision (MO_est_check, MO_mse_check).

Choosing the regressors (L22 slide 9)

Property (L22 slide 9). $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X^3]$, but the linear LMS estimators based on $X$ and on $X^3$ are in general different.

Why the first half holds (the slide asserts it with no justification). The map $x\mapsto x^3$ is a strictly increasing bijection of $\mathbb{R}$, with inverse the cube root. Therefore the events $\{X=x\}$ and $\{X^3=x^3\}$ are the same event: knowing one number tells you the other exactly. Conditional expectation depends only on the conditioning event, so $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X^3=x^3]$ for every $x$, i.e. the two conditional expectations are the same random variable. Nothing was lost or gained by relabelling the data.

Why the second half holds. A linear estimator is not allowed to relabel. The family $\{aX+b\}$ and the family $\{aX^3+b\}$ are different sets of functions, and there is no reason the best member of one should equal the best member of the other. Concretely, take $X$ uniform on $[-1,1]$ and $\Theta=X^3+N$ with $N$ independent, mean $0$, variance $\sigma^2=0.01$. Then $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=\mathbb{E}[X^6]+\sigma^2=\tfrac17+0.01=0.152857$ (CUBE_varTheta), while $$\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)=\mathbb{E}[X^4]=\tfrac15=0.2,\qquad \operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X^3)=\mathbb{E}[X^6]=\tfrac17=0.142857$$ (CUBE_cov_X, CUBE_cov_X3; all odd moments of a symmetric $X$ vanish, so the $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]\mathbb{E}[\cdot]$ terms drop out). Using $\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]=\tfrac13$ and $\operatorname{var}(X^3)=\mathbb{E}[X^6]=\tfrac17$: $$\text{MSE}(aX+b)=0.152857-\frac{(1/5)^2}{1/3}=0.032857,\qquad \text{MSE}(aX^3+b)=0.152857-\frac{(1/7)^2}{1/7}=0.01$$ (CUBE_mse_linX, CUBE_mse_linX3). The cube regressor is exact (its error equals the irreducible noise $\sigma^2=0.01$, with $a=1$, CUBE_a_X3) while the raw regressor is more than three times worse, with $a=(1/5)/(1/3)=0.6$ (CUBE_a_X).

Interpretation. "Linear" in linear LMS means linear in the unknown coefficients, not linear in the data. You are free to invent new observations $X,X^2,X^3,\ldots$, or $\log X$, or anything else, and fit $\hat\Theta=a_1X+a_2X^2+a_3X^3+b$ by the normal equations of this subsection. Enlarging the family can only lower the mean squared error, because the smaller family is contained in the larger one — and the price stays the same: means, variances, covariances.
Recipe. Estimating $\Theta$ from data under squared error.
  1. No data? Report $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$; the error is $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$ (§4.1).
  2. Can you get the posterior? If the full joint law is available and $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$ is computable, report it. This is optimal, and the error is $\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=x)$ conditionally, $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)]$ overall (§4.2). Do not integrate blindly first: identify the posterior family (uniform on a slice, beta, normal) and quote its mean.
  3. Jointly normal? Then step 2 and step 4 give the same answer; do the easy one (§4.5).
  4. Otherwise go linear. Collect the five numbers $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$, $\mathbb{E}[X]$, $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$, $\operatorname{var}(X)$, $\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)$ — the law of total variance and iterated expectations (G4 §1) usually get them without the joint density — then $$a=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)}{\operatorname{var}(X)},\quad b=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-a\mathbb{E}[X],\quad \text{MSE}=(1-\rho^2)\sigma_\Theta^2 .$$
  5. Several observations? Solve $\sum_i a_i\operatorname{cov}(X_i,X_j)=\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X_j)$ for $j=1,\ldots,n$, then $b=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-\sum_ia_i\mathbb{E}[X_i]$. For $X_i=\Theta+W_i$ with independent noises, skip the algebra and use the precision-weighted average of Example 4.3.
  6. Error still too large? Add transformed observations $X^2,X^3,\ldots$ and repeat step 5 (§4.6). Sanity-check the answer against the range of $\Theta$ — a line can leave it.

Practice 4.7. In the setting of Example 4.3 you have one sensor with $\sigma_1^2=1$ and a prior with $\sigma_0^2=4$. A second sensor with $\sigma_2^2=4$ is offered free. By how much does it reduce the mean squared error, and what weight does it get?
Solution

With one sensor, $S_1=\frac14+1=1.25$ and the error is $1/1.25=0.8$. With both, $S_2=\frac14+1+\frac14=1.5$ and the error is $1/1.5=0.666667$ — a reduction of $0.133333$, i.e. $16.7\%$. Practice 4.8. $X$ is uniform on $[-1,1]$ and $\Theta=X^2$ exactly (no noise). (a) What is $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ and its mean squared error? (b) What is the linear LMS estimator based on $X$, and its error? (c) What if you are allowed the single observation $X^2$?
Solution

(a) $\Theta$ is a function of $X$, so $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]=X^2=\Theta$: the estimator is exact and the mean squared error is $0$.

(b) $\mathbb{E}[X]=0$ and $\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)=\mathbb{E}[X^3]-\mathbb{E}[X^2]\mathbb{E}[X] =0-0=0$, because all odd moments of a symmetric distribution vanish. So $a=0$, $b=\mathbb{E}[\Theta] =\mathbb{E}[X^2]=1/3$, and the estimator is the constant $1/3$ — the data are discarded. Its error is the full $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=\mathbb{E}[X^4]-(\mathbb{E}[X^2])^2=\frac15-\frac19 =\frac{4}{45}=0.088889$ (WID_gap). This is the widget's $c=0$ setting with $\sigma=0$.

(c) With the observation $Y=X^2$ we have $\Theta=Y$ exactly, so $a=1$, $b=0$ and the error is $0$: the best linear estimator based on the right transform is the optimal estimator. Nothing about the model changed — only the family we searched.

4.7 Recitation 22: Romeo and Juliet, all seven parts

Example 4.4 (rec22 Problem 1; B&T Examples 8.2, 8.7, 8.12, 8.15). Juliet is late on any date by a random amount $X$, uniformly distributed on $[0,\theta]$. The parameter $\theta$ is unknown and modeled as a random variable $\Theta$, uniform on $[0,1]$ (hours). (a) After observing lateness $x$ on the first date, how should Romeo update the distribution of $\Theta$? (b) How should he update it after $n$ dates with latenesses $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ (conditionally independent given $\Theta=\theta$, each uniform on $[0,\theta]$)? (c) Find the MAP estimate from $X=x$. (d) Find the LMS estimate from $X=x$. (e) Compute the conditional mean squared error of the MAP and the LMS estimates and compare. (f) Derive the linear LMS estimator of $\Theta$ based on $X$. (g) Compute the conditional mean squared error of the linear LMS estimate and compare with (e).
Solution

The official rec22 solution document contains no worked answers at all — it lists only the textbook cross-references (Examples 8.2, 8.7, 8.12, 8.15). Everything below fills that gap. Parts (a)–(c) are the posterior/MAP material of §3 and are included because (d)–(g) cannot be stated without them.

(a) The posterior after one observation. The prior is $f_\Theta(\theta)=1$ for $0\le\theta\le1$ (uniform on an interval of length $1$). The observation model is $$f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)=\frac{1}{\theta}\quad\text{for }0\le x\le\theta .$$ By Bayes' rule for densities, $$f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=\frac{f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)} {\int_0^1 f_\Theta(\theta')f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta')\,d\theta'} .$$ The product $f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)=1/\theta$ is nonzero exactly when $0\le x\le\theta\le1$ — a lateness of $x$ is impossible unless $\theta$ is at least $x$, so the observation truncates the prior from below. The denominator is therefore $$f_X(x)=\int_x^1\frac{1}{\theta'}\,d\theta'=\big[\ln\theta'\big]_x^1=\ln 1-\ln x=-\ln x=\ln(1/x) =|\ln x|,$$ using $0<x<1$ so $\ln x<0$. Hence $$\boxed{\ f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=\frac{1}{\theta\,|\ln x|}\quad\text{for }x\le\theta\le1\ }$$ and $0$ otherwise (numerically verified to integrate to $1$ at $x=0.2,0.5,0.8$). Incidentally $f_X(x)=\ln(1/x)$ is the marginal density of the lateness, which we need in part (g) (RJ_fX_check confirms it integrates to $1$).

(b) The posterior after $n$ observations. Given $\Theta=\theta$ the $X_i$ are conditionally independent, so the likelihood is the product $$f_{X\mid\Theta}(x_1,\ldots,x_n\mid\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\theta}=\frac{1}{\theta^n},$$ valid when every $x_i$ lies in $[0,\theta]$, i.e. when $\theta\ge\bar x:=\max\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$. Only the maximum matters — it is a sufficient statistic. Multiplying by the flat prior and normalizing, $$f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=\frac{c(\bar x)}{\theta^{\,n}}\quad\text{for }\bar x\le\theta\le1, \qquad c(\bar x)=\left(\int_{\bar x}^{1}\frac{d\theta}{\theta^{\,n}}\right)^{-1} =\frac{n-1}{\bar x^{\,1-n}-1}\ \ (n\ge2),$$ with $c(\bar x)=1/|\ln\bar x|$ when $n=1$. (The integral is $\int_{\bar x}^1\theta^{-n}d\theta=\frac{\bar x^{1-n}-1}{n-1}$ for $n\ne1$.) Checks at $\bar x=0.4$: $c=1.091357$ for $n=1$, $c=0.666667$ for $n=2$, $c=0.105090$ for $n=5$ (RJ_c_n1_xbar04, RJ_c_n2_xbar04, RJ_c_n5_xbar04). More data concentrate the posterior near $\bar x$: the corresponding LMS estimates $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$ fall from $0.654814$ ($n=1$) to $0.610860$ ($n=2$) to $0.512315$ ($n=5$) (RJ_lmsn_n1_xbar04, RJ_lmsn_n2_xbar04, RJ_lmsn_n5_xbar04), converging down toward $\bar x=0.4$ itself.

(c) The MAP estimate. On its support $[x,1]$ the posterior $1/(\theta|\ln x|)$ is strictly decreasing in $\theta$ (the derivative $-1/(\theta^2|\ln x|)$ is negative). A decreasing function on a closed interval is maximized at the left endpoint, so $$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=x .$$ For $n$ observations the same argument on $[\bar x,1]$ with $1/\theta^n$ gives $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\bar x=\max_i x_i$. Note this is an optimistic estimate: it assumes Juliet can never be later than she has already been.

(d) The LMS estimate. By §4.2 it is the posterior mean: $$\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]=\int_x^1\theta\cdot\frac{1}{\theta\,|\ln x|}\,d\theta =\frac{1}{|\ln x|}\int_x^1 d\theta=\frac{1-x}{|\ln x|}.$$ The $\theta$ in the numerator cancels the $\theta$ in the posterior, which is why the answer is so simple. Values: $0.390865$ at $x=0.1$, $0.541011$ at $x=0.25$, $0.721348$ at $x=0.5$, $0.869015$ at $x=0.75$, $0.949122$ at $x=0.9$ (RJ_lms_x01 … RJ_lms_x09). The LMS estimate always exceeds the MAP estimate $x$ — the posterior's mass lies to the right of its peak — which is the sense in which MAP is "optimistic" and LMS is not.

(e) Conditional mean squared errors of MAP and LMS. Start with the general expansion, valid for any number $\hat\theta$: $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat\theta-\Theta)^2\mid X=x\big] =\hat\theta^2-2\hat\theta\,\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]+\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2\mid X=x].$$ The last piece is a second posterior moment: $$\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2\mid X=x]=\int_x^1\theta^2\cdot\frac{1}{\theta|\ln x|}\,d\theta =\frac{1}{|\ln x|}\int_x^1\theta\,d\theta =\frac{1}{|\ln x|}\cdot\frac{1-x^2}{2}=\frac{1-x^2}{2|\ln x|}.$$ MAP ($\hat\theta=x$): $$\mathbb{E}\big[(x-\Theta)^2\mid X=x\big]=x^2-\frac{2x(1-x)}{|\ln x|}+\frac{1-x^2}{2|\ln x|}.$$ LMS ($\hat\theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$): the general expansion collapses to the posterior variance, $$\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=x)=\frac{1-x^2}{2|\ln x|}-\left(\frac{1-x}{|\ln x|}\right)^{2},$$ as §4.2 promised. (f) The linear LMS estimator. We need the five numbers, and we get them by conditioning rather than by integrating the joint density.

  • $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=\tfrac12$ and $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=\tfrac1{12}=0.083333$ (uniform on $[0,1]$), so $\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2]=\tfrac1{12}+\tfrac14=\tfrac13$ (RJ_ETheta2).
  • $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\Theta=\theta]=\theta/2$ (mean of a uniform on $[0,\theta]$), so by iterated expectations (G4 §1) $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid\Theta]\big]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta/2] =\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac14=0.25$ (RJ_EX).
  • $\operatorname{var}(X\mid\Theta=\theta)=\theta^2/12$, so by the law of total variance (G4 §1) $$\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid\Theta)\big] +\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid\Theta]\big) =\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{\Theta^2}{12}\Big]+\operatorname{var}\Big(\frac{\Theta}{2}\Big) =\frac{1}{12}\cdot\frac13+\frac14\cdot\frac{1}{12} =\frac{1}{36}+\frac{1}{48}=\frac{4}{144}+\frac{3}{144}=\frac{7}{144}=0.048611$$ (RJ_EvarXgT, RJ_varEXgT, RJ_varX).

Practice 4.9. In Example 4.4, suppose Romeo observes $n=5$ dates with maximum lateness $\bar x=0.4$. (a) Give the MAP and LMS estimates. (b) Explain, without computing anything, why the LMS estimate must lie strictly between $\bar x$ and $1$, and why it must decrease as $n$ grows with $\bar x$ held fixed.
Solution

(a) MAP $=\bar x=0.4$; LMS $=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]=0.512315$ (RJ_lmsn_n5_xbar04), computed from the part-(b) posterior $c(\bar x)/\theta^5$ with $c(0.4)=0.105090$.

(b) The posterior is supported on $[\bar x,1]$ and puts positive density on the whole interval, so its mean — a weighted average of points strictly inside — lies strictly between the endpoints. As $n$ grows, the likelihood $\theta^{-n}$ decays faster in $\theta$, so the posterior tilts ever more sharply toward its left endpoint $\bar x$ and its mean slides down toward $\bar x$: the sequence $0.654814,\ 0.610860,\ 0.512315$ for $n=1,2,5$ shows exactly that. In the limit the LMS and MAP estimates coincide, which is the general phenomenon B&T note in Example 8.13 — with enough data the prior stops mattering and all reasonable estimators agree.

§4 cheatsheet.
  • No data. $\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-\hat\theta)^2]=\operatorname{var}(\Theta) +(\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-\hat\theta)^2$; best constant $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$, cost $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$.
  • LMS theorem (L22 slide 1): $\hat\theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$ minimizes $\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-\hat\theta)^2\mid X=x]$, with minimum $\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=x)$; $\hat\Theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ minimizes $\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-g(X))^2]$ over all $g$, with minimum $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)]$. Pointwise optimality plus the tower rule.
  • Error properties (L22 slide 4), $\tilde\Theta=\hat\Theta-\Theta$: $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta\mid X]=0$, $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta]=0$, $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta h(X)]=0$ for every $h$, $\operatorname{cov}(\tilde\Theta,\hat\Theta)=0$, $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta)+\operatorname{var}(\tilde\Theta)$.
  • Linear LMS (L22 slides 5–6): $\hat\Theta_L=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+\frac{\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)}{\operatorname{var}(X)} (X-\mathbb{E}[X])$, error $(1-\rho^2)\sigma_\Theta^2$ with $\rho=\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)/(\sigma_\Theta\sigma_X)$. Five numbers, no posterior.
  • Many observations (L22 slides 7–8): normal equations $\sum_i a_i\operatorname{cov}(X_i,X_j)=\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X_j)$; for $X_i=\Theta+W_i$ with independent noises, $\hat\Theta_L=\big(\mu/\sigma_0^2+\sum X_i/\sigma_i^2\big)\big/\sum_{i=0}^n 1/\sigma_i^2$ with error $1/\sum_{i=0}^n1/\sigma_i^2$ — precisions add.
  • Normal $\Rightarrow$ linear is exact (L22 slide 8): jointly normal $\Theta,X$ give $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]=\hat\Theta_L$ and a conditional variance $(1-\rho^2)\sigma_\Theta^2$ that does not depend on $x$.
  • Regressors (L22 slide 9): $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X^3]$ but the linear estimators differ; enlarging the family to $X,X^2,X^3,\ldots$ can only help.
  • Worked numbers. Uniform example: $\hat\Theta_L=0.9X+0.7$, linear error $0.3$ versus LMS error $5/18=0.2778$ versus baseline $3$. Romeo & Juliet: MAP $x$ (error $1/9$), LMS $(1-x)/|\ln x|$ (error $1/3-\ln(4/3)=0.045651$), linear $\frac67X+\frac27$ (error $1/21=0.047619$), baseline $1/12=0.083333$.
