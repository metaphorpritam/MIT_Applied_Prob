§0   How to use this question bank

Companion to notes G1–G7 · all 150 questions original, written against those notes' notation · Bertsekas–Tsitsiklis (B&T) cited throughout for the underlying theory.

This is the practice half of the course. It holds 150 questions, organised into seven sections that mirror the seven study notes one-for-one — fundamentals, discrete random variables, continuous random variables, iterated expectations and arrival processes, Markov chains, limit theorems and Bayesian inference, and classical statistical inference. Nothing here restates a worked example from the notes: every question is fresh, self-contained (you never have to go hunting for a missing definition), and tagged with the note section that teaches the tool it wants. Every solution is worked in full — each step justified, each intermediate value substituted, ending in a bold Answer line — and every solution is hidden behind a click, so the page reads as a problem set until you decide otherwise.

The questions are deliberately mixed in form. Some ask for a number; some ask you to derive an identity; some are true-or-false and score only the reason; some ask only which tool applies and why, with no arithmetic at all; some hand you a plausible-looking wrong solution and ask you to find the break. That mixture is on purpose: an exam tests whether you can choose the right machinery under time pressure, and choosing is a separate skill from grinding.

Recipe (how to practise with this bank).
  1. Attempt before opening. The single highest-value habit here. Reading a worked solution feels like learning and mostly is not; the struggle before the solution is where the encoding happens. Give a ★★ question five to ten minutes of genuine effort before you touch the toggle.
  2. If stuck, open the note — not the solution. Each question's grey meta line cites the section that teaches its tool (e.g. G3 §4.2). Go there, re-read that one section, and come back. You will usually finish the problem yourself, and you will have repaired the actual gap instead of patching one problem.
  3. Write the model down first. Sample space or random variables, what is known, what is asked, in symbols — before any formula. Most wrong answers in this material are model errors made before the arithmetic begins, not arithmetic errors.
  4. Read the closing trap line even when you were right. Where a question has a tempting wrong answer, the solution names it and says why it fails. That line is often worth more than the question.
  5. Re-attempt the ★★★ items about a week later, cold, without notes. Spaced re-attempts of the hardest problems are what carry across to an exam; re-reading is not.
  6. Use the filters as a diagnostic. Before a quiz, set Difficulty to ★★ and pin one section at a time; after a quiz, filter to whichever section you lost marks in and work it end to end.

The difficulty scale. ★ warm-up — one definition or one formula, applied directly; if these are slow, the gap is in the note, not in your problem-solving. ★★ exam-level — two or three ideas combined, the standard quiz difficulty; this is the bulk of the bank. ★★★ challenging — a nonobvious setup, a trap that survives a first reading, or a derivation with a real idea in it; expect to need the note, and expect these to be slow the first time.

How to read a question card. Every question carries one grey meta line directly under its number, in three parts: the stars (difficulty, as above), the topic in words (the skill being drilled), and the note section that teaches it — G5 §3 means note G5 (Markov chains), its section 3 (steady state). A bare "§N" inside a citation is always a section of the cited note; references to the textbook always carry the "B&T" prefix. At the foot of each solution, the small Tool: … line repeats the teaching section together with the book section, so you can go one level deeper when a solution's step is the thing you did not follow. And, as everywhere in these notes, every number printed in a question or a solution was recomputed independently in Python (see computes/), with the subtle ones cross-checked by Monte Carlo simulation — so if your value disagrees with the printed one, the disagreement is real and worth chasing rather than shrugging off.
