§0   From outcomes to numbers

Sources: L05 slides 1–2, 4 · L06 slide 1 · L07 slide 1 · Bertsekas–Tsitsiklis (cited throughout as B&T) §2.1 · numbers from computes/g2_s0.py. A bare "§N.k" always means this note's own subsection; every reference to the textbook carries the "B&T" prefix.

Note G1 built one object: a probabilistic model $(\Omega, \mathbf{P})$ — a sample space of outcomes plus a law that assigns probabilities to sets of outcomes. That machinery answers "how likely is this event?" and nothing more. It cannot answer "how large, on average?", because the outcomes $\omega \in \Omega$ need not be numbers at all: HT, "the third door", "student 7 got hat 4" are outcomes, and you cannot add or average them. This note (G2, lectures L05–L07) adds the missing ingredient — a random variable, which is simply a number attached to every outcome (L05 slide 2). Once outcomes carry numbers, arithmetic becomes available: averages, spreads, sums of several quantities, and algebra on all of them. This section fixes the picture and the notation; the mathematics starts in §1.

0.1 What a random variable is, and why numbers change everything

Definition (random variable; L05 slide 2, B&T §2.1). A random variable is an assignment of a value (a number) to every possible outcome — mathematically, a function $$X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}, \qquad \omega \mapsto X(\omega).$$ Here $\Omega$ is the sample space of the underlying model, $\omega$ is one outcome of it, and $X(\omega)$ is the number that $X$ attaches to that outcome. The values may be discrete (finitely or countably many, the subject of this note) or continuous (a later topic). Several different random variables can be defined on the same sample space (L05 slide 2), because "attach a number" can be done in many ways at once.

Nothing about the underlying model changes when you introduce $X$. The pair $(\Omega, \mathbf{P})$ is still there, still supplying every probability; $X$ is a layer of bookkeeping laid on top of it. That is worth stressing because the definition is deceptively plain: a random variable is neither random nor a variable — it is a deterministic function, and the randomness lives entirely in which $\omega$ the experiment produces.

Fig. 0.1 — A random variable as a function $\Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ (L05 slide 2). Two independent tosses of a fair coin; $X(\omega)$ counts the heads. Two distinct outcomes are sent to the same value, and their probabilities add: this merging is exactly what a PMF records.

Read Fig. 0.1 left to right. The experiment is two independent tosses of a fair coin, so $\Omega = \{\texttt{HH}, \texttt{HT}, \texttt{TH}, \texttt{TT}\}$ has $4$ equally likely outcomes, each of probability $1/4$ (independence, G1 §3, and the discrete uniform law, G1 §1.3). Define $X(\omega) = $ number of heads in $\omega$, so $$X(\texttt{TT}) = 0, \qquad X(\texttt{TH}) = 1, \qquad X(\texttt{HT}) = 1, \qquad X(\texttt{HH}) = 2 .$$ Collecting all outcomes with the same value and adding their probabilities — the recipe of L05 slide 4 — gives the numbers under the value $1$ in the figure: $$\mathbf{P}(X = 1) = \mathbf{P}(\{\texttt{TH}, \texttt{HT}\}) = \mathbf{P}(\texttt{TH}) + \mathbf{P}(\texttt{HT}) = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12,$$ the middle step by additivity (the two outcomes are disjoint events), and likewise $\mathbf{P}(X=0) = 1/4$ and $\mathbf{P}(X=2) = 1/4$. Those three numbers are the PMF of $X$, the object §1 is about; they already sum to $1/4 + 1/2 + 1/4 = 1$. Averaging the values against those weights gives $\mathbb{E}[X] = 0\cdot\tfrac14 + 1\cdot\tfrac12 + 2\cdot\tfrac14 = 1$ head on average, and the spread turns out to be $\operatorname{var}(X) = 1/2$ — quantities that simply do not exist for a bare sample space of letter strings.

The same $\Omega$ carries other random variables. Let $Y = 1$ if the first toss is a head and $Y = 0$ otherwise, and let $Z = 1$ if the two tosses agree and $Z = 0$ otherwise. Then $Y(\texttt{HH}) = Y(\texttt{HT}) = 1$ while $Z(\texttt{HH}) = Z(\texttt{TT}) = 1$: three different functions, one model, and each with its own PMF ($\mathbb{E}[Y] = \mathbb{E}[Z] = 1/2$, $\operatorname{var}(Y) = \operatorname{var}(Z) = 1/4$). Studying such variables together — how one constrains another — is §4 of this note (L07 slide 1).

Interpretation. A random variable is a summary device. It throws away everything about the outcome except one number, which is exactly why it is useful: the summary is comparable, addable, and averageable. Fig. 0.1 shows the price of the summary — $\texttt{TH}$ and $\texttt{HT}$ become indistinguishable once you look only at $X$. Choosing which number to attach is therefore the same kind of modeling decision as choosing the granularity of $\Omega$ was in G1: keep what the question needs, discard the rest.
Gotcha — capital $X$ versus lowercase $x$. This is the single most common source of confusion in Chapter 2 (L05 slide 2 flags the notation deliberately). $X$ is the function, the whole assignment of arrows in Fig. 0.1. $x$ is one particular number, a possible value that the function might take. So:
  • $X = x$ is not an equation to solve — it is an event, the set $\{\omega \in \Omega \ \text{s.t.}\ X(\omega) = x\}$ of outcomes that $X$ sends to $x$ (L05 slide 3). Only events have probabilities, which is why $\mathbf{P}(X = x)$ makes sense.
  • $p_X(x)$ has $X$ as a subscript (which random variable) and $x$ as an argument (which value). Writing $p_x(X)$, or $p(x)$ when two random variables are in play, loses exactly the information you need in §4.
  • $X$ never has a numeric value in a formula: expressions like "$X = 1.7$, so $\mathbb{E}[X] = 1.7$" are category errors. By contrast $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is a number — a fixed constant computed from the whole PMF, not a random quantity.
  • $g(X)$ is again a random variable (a function of a function), while $g(x)$ is a number. This distinction is what makes $\mathbb{E}[g(X)] \neq g(\mathbb{E}[X])$ in general (L05 slide 7; proved and used in §2.2 here, with a worked case in §3.3).
Rule of thumb: if the symbol is capitalized, it is still waiting for an outcome; if it is lowercase, the outcome has already been reduced to a number.

0.2 Roadmap of this note

G2 covers L05–L07 and rec05–rec07, i.e. Chapter 2 of the textbook (B&T §2.1–§2.7). The five sections form one chain: attach numbers to outcomes, average them, measure their spread and re-average them under partial information, let several of them interact, then collect the whole toolbox on one page.

Fig. 0.2 — How the five sections of this note chain together (built from the lecture outlines: L05 slide 1, L06 slide 1, L07 slide 1).
  • §1 — Random variables and PMFs (L05 slides 2–5, B&T §2.1–2.2). The definition above made precise; the PMF $p_X(x) = \mathbf{P}(X = x)$ and its two defining properties $p_X(x) \ge 0$, $\sum_x p_X(x) = 1$; the standard families — Bernoulli, discrete uniform, binomial, geometric. Hook: for two independent rolls of a fair tetrahedral die with $X = \min(F,S)$, exactly $5$ of the $16$ equally likely cells have $\min = 2$, so $p_X(2) = 5/16$ (L05 slide 4, where the slide leaves the answer blank).
  • §2 — Expectation (L05 slides 6–7, rec05, B&T §2.3–2.4). $\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\,p_X(x)$ as center of gravity of the PMF and as the long-run average; the expected value rule $\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)\,p_X(x)$, which spares you the PMF of $g(X)$; linearity $\mathbb{E}[\alpha X + \beta] = \alpha\mathbb{E}[X] + \beta$. Hook: $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]$ and $g(\mathbb{E}[X])$ genuinely differ, and rec05's marksman problem shows how much.
  • §3 — Variance, conditioning, and the geometric distribution (L06 slides 1–7, rec06 P3, rec07 P3, B&T §2.3–2.4, B&T §2.6). $\operatorname{var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2$, standard deviation, and the scaling rule $\operatorname{var}(\alpha X + \beta) = \alpha^2 \operatorname{var}(X)$; then the conditional PMF $p_{X\mid A}(x) = \mathbf{P}(X = x \mid A)$ — an ordinary PMF on a smaller world — the total expectation theorem, the geometric random variable and its memorylessness, and state recursions for waiting-time problems. Hook: shifting a random variable never changes its spread, but stretching it by $\alpha$ multiplies the variance by $\alpha^2$, not by $\alpha$; and memorylessness turns $\mathbb{E}[X] = 1/p$ into a three-line argument.
  • §4 — Joint PMFs and multiple random variables (L07 slides 1–6, rec06 P1–P2, rec07 P1–P2, B&T §2.5–2.7). Joint PMFs $p_{X,Y}(x,y)$, marginals, conditional PMFs and the chain rule, independence of random variables, expectation of a function of two variables, linearity for sums, the variance of a sum, and the indicator decomposition that delivers the binomial mean and variance in three lines. Hook: $\mathbb{E}[X + Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y]$ holds with no independence assumption whatsoever — the workhorse identity of the whole chapter — while $\operatorname{var}(X+Y) = \operatorname{var}(X) + \operatorname{var}(Y)$ does need it.
  • §5 — Synthesis: the discrete toolbox (all of L05–L07 and rec05–rec07, B&T §2.1–2.7). The PMF zoo on one page, the expectation and variance identities collected in a single table together with the hypothesis each one needs, the six mistakes that generate most wrong answers, and the bridge to G3. Hook: every identity in that table is either unconditional or requires independence, and knowing which is which is most of the skill.

Recitation material is folded into the section it belongs to: rec05 (derivations of the expected value rule, the marksman, size-biased bus sampling, the St. Petersburg paradox), rec06 (die-then-coin joint PMFs, eight points in the plane, variance of the geometric), rec07 (the two-variable expected value rule, a joint PMF puzzle, expected tosses until two consecutive identical outcomes). Each appears as a full statement with an expandable worked solution.

0.3 Where this sits in the course

Behind you (G1): the model $(\Omega, \mathbf{P})$, conditional probability and Bayes' rule, independence, and counting. All of it stays in force — a random variable is defined on top of a model, never instead of one, and every probability in this note is ultimately $\mathbf{P}$ evaluated on a subset of $\Omega$. Two G1 tools get used constantly: independence, to multiply per-trial probabilities when building the binomial and geometric PMFs (L05 slides 3, 5), and counting, to say how many outcome sequences produce a given value — the $\binom{n}{k}$ in the binomial PMF is G1 §4 doing its job.

Ahead: continuous random variables, where a single value has probability $0$, the PMF $p_X(x)$ is replaced by a density $f_X(x)$, and every $\sum_x$ in this note becomes an $\int dx$. Almost every definition you meet here — expectation, variance, conditioning, joint distributions, independence — is restated verbatim in that setting with that one substitution, which is the strongest practical argument for learning the discrete versions properly now.
