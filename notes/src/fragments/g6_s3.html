Everything up to this point has been probability: a fully specified model was handed to us and we computed consequences. §1 and §2 of this note were the last and most powerful such computations — the weak law and the central limit theorem tell you what a sample average does when the model is known. This section reverses the arrow. The model is not known, data are available, and we want to run the machinery backwards: from the data to the unknown. That is statistical inference (L21 slide 1; B&T Chapter 8 preamble, p. 408).

The Bayesian answer to "how do you run probability backwards?" is disarmingly simple: refuse to leave probability theory at all. Model the unknown as a random variable $\Theta$, give it a prior distribution, write down the observation model that says how the data $X$ are generated once $\Theta$ is fixed, and then apply Bayes' rule — the same Bayes' rule from G1 §2 and G3 §3 — to get the posterior distribution of $\Theta$ given the data. Every question you could ask about the unknown then has a unique, calculable answer, because you never left the realm of a fully specified probabilistic model (B&T §8.1, p. 409).

This section does three things. §3.1 sets up the framework: what the prior, the observation model, and the posterior are; how Bayesian inference differs from classical statistics; the two flavors of inference problem (hypothesis testing and estimation) and the two flavors of inference target (model versus variable); and the four syntactically parallel versions of Bayes' rule you need depending on whether $\Theta$ and $X$ are discrete or continuous. §3.2 computes posteriors in the three canonical cases: uniform prior with binomial data (the coin of L21 slide 4), normal prior with normal noise (B&T Example 8.3, the workhorse of engineering), and the uniform-scale parameter of rec22 (Romeo and Juliet). §3.3 squeezes the posterior down to a single number — the MAP estimate or the conditional expectation — explains what each one optimizes, and shows exactly when squeezing is a bad idea. §4 takes the conditional-expectation estimator seriously and proves its optimality; here we only define it.

Interpretation. The entire Bayesian apparatus is one formula, $$\text{posterior} \;\propto\; \text{prior}\times\text{likelihood},$$ read four different ways depending on which of $\Theta$, $X$ is discrete. The prior is what you believed before the data; the likelihood is how surprising the data would be under each candidate value of $\Theta$; the posterior multiplies them and renormalizes. The renormalizing constant is the only genuinely new work, and for the MAP rule you do not even need it.

3.1 The Bayesian setup: prior, observation model, posterior

Classical versus Bayesian: what kind of object is the unknown?

L21 slide 2 draws the same block diagram twice and changes one letter. In both diagrams an unknown feeds a probabilistic model, noise $N$ is injected, an observation $X$ comes out, and an estimator turns $X$ into a guess $\hat\Theta$. The difference is what the unknown is.

  • Classical (frequentist) statistics. The unknown is a number $\theta$ — not a random variable, just a constant that happens to be unknown. The model is written $p_X(x;\theta)$ with a semicolon: there is one probability model for each candidate $\theta$, and the semicolon is a deliberate reminder that we are not conditioning on anything, because $\theta$ is not an event. L21 slide 2's example: $\theta$ = mass of the electron. There is nothing random about it (B&T §8.1, p. 409).
  • Bayesian statistics. The unknown is a random variable $\Theta$ carrying a prior distribution $p_\Theta$ or $f_\Theta$, and the model is written $p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$ with a conditioning bar. There is one joint model over $(\Theta, X)$, and inference is conditioning — which is a thing we already know how to do.
Fig. 3.1 — The classical and Bayesian pipelines side by side (L21 slide 2; B&T §8.1, its Figure 8.1). Only the status of the unknown changes: constant $\theta$ above, random variable $\Theta$ with a prior below. The Bayesian pipeline has an extra output the classical one cannot produce — a full posterior distribution, not just a point.
Interpretation (the debate in two sentences). The classical statistician objects that the mass of the electron is not random and that any particular prior is arbitrary. The Bayesian replies that a prior is not a claim about randomness but a bookkeeping device for the state of knowledge — if past experiments pin the constant to a rough range, a prior concentrated on that range says exactly that — and that putting all assumptions in one visible place is a feature, not a bug (B&T §8.1, pp. 409–410). In 6.041 we do Bayesian inference in this chapter and classical inference in the next; you are not required to pick a side.

Two dimensions of classification

Inference problems get sorted along two independent axes, both of which L21 slide 2 mentions and B&T §8.1 (pp. 409–411) develops. Getting them straight prevents most of the confusion in this chapter.

Axis 1 — what are you inferring? B&T's running example is a linear model $X = aS + W$, where $S$ is a transmitted signal, $a$ is a channel attenuation, $W$ is noise, and $X$ is what the receiver sees (L21 slide 2, red ink; B&T Example 8.1, p. 410).

  • Model building / model inference. You know $S$ (a pilot sequence, agreed on in advance), you observe $X$, and you infer $a$ — you are building a description of the channel itself. Other examples: do planets move on ellipses? what is the Poisson rate of customer arrivals?
  • Estimation in the presence of noise / variable inference. You know $a$ (perhaps because you already inferred it from a pilot), you observe $X$, and you estimate $S$ — you want the value of a hidden variable, not a law of nature. Other examples: where am I, given a few GPS readings?

B&T stress that the distinction is not sharp — describe a model by a finite list of parameters and model inference becomes variable inference — and that the same methods apply to both, which is why we will not belabor it (B&T §8.1, p. 410).

Axis 2 — what does a good answer look like?

  • Hypothesis testing. $\Theta$ takes one of a small finite number of values (two, in the binary case), each value being a competing hypothesis. Success is measured by the probability of an incorrect decision, and you want that small (L21 slide 2). Example: is this email spam or legitimate (B&T Example 8.5, p. 418)?
  • Estimation. $\Theta$ ranges over a continuum. Success is measured by an estimation error — typically the mean squared error $\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-\hat\Theta)^2]$ — and you want that small (L21 slide 2). Example: what is the bias of this coin?
Gotcha. The two axes are independent. You can do hypothesis testing on a model ("is the channel attenuating or not?") and estimation of a variable ("what bit-value voltage was sent?"). What actually drives the algebra is not axis 1 at all — it is whether $\Theta$ and $X$ are discrete or continuous, because that decides which of the four Bayes formulas you write.

The three ingredients, and the posterior

Definition (Bayesian inference model; L21 slides 2–3; B&T §8.1, p. 412). A Bayesian inference problem consists of:
  1. an unknown random variable $\Theta$ (possibly a vector $\Theta=(\Theta_1,\dots,\Theta_m)$), together with a prior distribution $p_\Theta(\theta)$ if $\Theta$ is discrete or $f_\Theta(\theta)$ if it is continuous — this encodes everything you believe about $\Theta$ before seeing data;
  2. an observation (data) random variable $X$, possibly a vector $X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)$, together with an observation model $p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$ or $f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$ — this says how the data are generated once the unknown is fixed at $\theta$, and it is where the noise lives;
  3. an observed value $x$ of $X$.
The complete answer to the inference problem is the posterior distribution $p_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)$ or $f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)$, obtained from Bayes' rule. Read as a function of $\theta$ with $x$ fixed, $p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$ is called the likelihood: it is not a distribution in $\theta$ and need not integrate to 1 in $\theta$.

The four versions of Bayes' rule below are the four ways of writing one idea. They are syntactically identical: start from the all-discrete version and, for each variable that becomes continuous, replace its PMF by a PDF and, in the denominator, its sum by an integral (B&T §8.1, p. 413). We recap them here because §3.2 uses three of the four; the derivations were done in G1 §2 (discrete) and G3 §3 (the mixed and continuous variants).

The four versions of Bayes' rule (L21 slide 3, slide 4; B&T §8.1, p. 413).
  • $\Theta$ discrete, $X$ discrete: $\displaystyle p_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=\frac{p_\Theta(\theta)\,p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)}{\sum_{\theta'}p_\Theta(\theta')\,p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta')}$
  • $\Theta$ discrete, $X$ continuous: $\displaystyle p_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=\frac{p_\Theta(\theta)\,f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)}{\sum_{\theta'}p_\Theta(\theta')\,f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta')}$
  • $\Theta$ continuous, $X$ discrete: $\displaystyle f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=\frac{f_\Theta(\theta)\,p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)}{\int f_\Theta(\theta')\,p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta')\,d\theta'}$
  • $\Theta$ continuous, $X$ continuous: $\displaystyle f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=\frac{f_\Theta(\theta)\,f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)}{\int f_\Theta(\theta')\,f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta')\,d\theta'}$
In every case the denominator is the marginal $p_X(x)$ or $f_X(x)$ evaluated at the observed $x$; $\theta'$ is a dummy variable of summation/integration. When $\Theta$ is multidimensional the integrals are multidimensional.
Fig. 3.2 — Which of the four versions of Bayes' rule to use (L21 slides 3–4; B&T §8.1, p. 413). Two yes/no questions — is $\Theta$ discrete? is $X$ discrete? — pick the formula. Recipe (computing a posterior).
  1. Name the unknown and the data. Which quantity is $\Theta$, which is $X$? Write down whether each is discrete or continuous — this picks the formula from Fig. 3.2.
  2. Write the prior $f_\Theta(\theta)$ (or $p_\Theta$), including its support. The support is where beginners lose points.
  3. Write the likelihood $f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$ (or $p_{X\mid\Theta}$), again including the range of $\theta$ for which it is nonzero. With $n$ conditionally independent observations, multiply: $f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n f_{X_i\mid\Theta}(x_i\mid\theta)$.
  4. Multiply and intersect the supports. The numerator $f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$ is nonzero only where both factors are; this intersection is the support of the posterior.
  5. Recognize or integrate. If the $\theta$-dependence matches a standard family (beta, normal, gamma, exponential), read the normalizing constant off the standard density and stop. Otherwise integrate the numerator over $\theta$ to get the denominator.
  6. Sanity-check that the posterior integrates (or sums) to 1 and that its support makes physical sense.
Gotcha (the denominator is a constant). $p_X(x)$ and $f_X(x)$ depend on the observed value $x$, which is a fixed number once you have the data — they do not depend on $\theta$. So the posterior is proportional, as a function of $\theta$, to the numerator alone: $$f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)\;=\;c(x)\,f_\Theta(\theta)\,f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta), \qquad c(x)=\frac{1}{\int f_\Theta(\theta')f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta')\,d\theta'}.$$ Two consequences. (i) For MAP estimation — maximizing the posterior over $\theta$ — you may drop $c(x)$ entirely and never compute an integral (B&T §8.2, p. 421). (ii) You may drop every other factor that does not involve $\theta$ as you go, absorbing them into $c(x)$ at the end. This is the single biggest labor saver in the chapter. What you may not drop is anything $\theta$-dependent, including the $1/\theta$ in a $\mathrm{U}[0,\theta]$ density and the $\theta$-dependent limits of the support.
Example 3.1 (L21 slide 3 — the quadratic trajectory, worked in full). An object moves on a trajectory that is quadratic in time, $$Z_t=\Theta_0+t\,\Theta_1+t^2\,\Theta_2,\qquad X_t=Z_t+W_t,\qquad t=1,2,\dots,n,$$ where $\Theta_0,\Theta_1,\Theta_2$ are the unknown coefficients (initial position, velocity, and half the acceleration), $Z_t$ is the true position at time $t$, $W_t$ is measurement noise, and $X_t$ is the reading of the instrument at time $t$. L21 slide 3 writes only the name of the posterior, $f_{\Theta_0,\Theta_1,\Theta_2\mid X_1,\dots,X_n}$, and stops — it specifies neither the prior nor the noise law and evaluates nothing. Fill the gap: assume $\Theta_0,\Theta_1,\Theta_2$ are independent, each normal with mean 0 and variance $\sigma_p^2$, that $W_1,\dots,W_n$ are independent normal with mean 0 and variance $\sigma_w^2$, and that the $\Theta_j$ are independent of the $W_t$. Find the posterior.
Solution

Step 1 — which Bayes variant. $\Theta=(\Theta_0,\Theta_1,\Theta_2)$ is continuous and $X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ is continuous, so we are in the fourth box of Fig. 3.2, with a three-dimensional integral in the denominator: $$f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=\frac{f_\Theta(\theta)\,f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)} {\displaystyle\int\!\!\int\!\!\int f_\Theta(\theta')\,f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta')\,d\theta'_0\,d\theta'_1\,d\theta'_2}.$$

Step 2 — the prior. The three coefficients are independent, so their joint density is the product of three normal densities: $$f_\Theta(\theta_0,\theta_1,\theta_2) =\prod_{j=0}^{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_p^2}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{\theta_j^2}{2\sigma_p^2}\Big) =\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma_p^2)^{3/2}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_p^2}\sum_{j=0}^{2}\theta_j^2\Big).$$

Step 3 — the likelihood. Condition on $\Theta=\theta$. Then $Z_t$ is the known number $\theta_0+t\theta_1+t^2\theta_2$, and $X_t=Z_t+W_t$ is normal with mean $Z_t$ and variance $\sigma_w^2$. The $W_t$ are independent of each other and of $\Theta$, so conditioned on $\Theta=\theta$ the $X_t$ are independent and the joint density factors: $$f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)=\prod_{t=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_w^2}} \exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x_t-\theta_0-t\theta_1-t^2\theta_2)^2}{2\sigma_w^2}\Big) =\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma_w^2)^{n/2}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_w^2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(x_t-\theta_0-t\theta_1-t^2\theta_2)^2\Big).$$

Step 4 — multiply, and drop everything free of $\theta$. Using the Gotcha above, with $c(x)$ absorbing the two constants $(2\pi\sigma_p^2)^{-3/2}$ and $(2\pi\sigma_w^2)^{-n/2}$ as well as the denominator, $$\boxed{\;f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=c(x)\, \exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_w^2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big(x_t-\theta_0-t\theta_1-t^2\theta_2\big)^2 -\frac{1}{2\sigma_p^2}\big(\theta_0^2+\theta_1^2+\theta_2^2\big)\Big).\;}$$ This is the explicit prior$\times$likelihood expression the slide omits.

Step 5 — recognize the family. Collect the exponent in matrix form. Let $A$ be the $n\times 3$ design matrix whose $t$-th row is $(1,\;t,\;t^2)$, so that the vector of true positions is $A\theta$. Then $\sum_t(x_t-\theta_0-t\theta_1-t^2\theta_2)^2=\|x-A\theta\|^2$ and the exponent is $$-\tfrac12\Big[\tfrac{1}{\sigma_w^2}(x-A\theta)^{\!\top}(x-A\theta)+\tfrac{1}{\sigma_p^2}\theta^{\!\top}\theta\Big] =-\tfrac12\Big[\theta^{\!\top}\underbrace{\Big(\tfrac{A^{\!\top}\!A}{\sigma_w^2}+\tfrac{I}{\sigma_p^2}\Big)}_{\textstyle \Sigma^{-1}}\theta -2\,\theta^{\!\top}\tfrac{A^{\!\top}x}{\sigma_w^2}\Big]+\text{const},$$ where we expanded the square and pushed the $\theta$-free piece $x^{\!\top}x/\sigma_w^2$ into the constant. Completing the square, with $\mu=\Sigma A^{\!\top}x/\sigma_w^2$, the exponent equals $-\tfrac12(\theta-\mu)^{\!\top}\Sigma^{-1}(\theta-\mu)+\text{const}$. So the posterior is multivariate normal: $$\Theta\mid X=x\ \sim\ \mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma),\qquad \Sigma=\Big(\frac{A^{\!\top}\!A}{\sigma_w^2}+\frac{I}{\sigma_p^2}\Big)^{\!-1},\qquad \mu=\Sigma\,\frac{A^{\!\top}x}{\sigma_w^2}.$$ The three-dimensional integral is never evaluated by brute force — recognizing the normal shape does the whole job.

Step 6 — numbers. Take $n=5$ readings at $t=1,\dots,5$, prior variance $\sigma_p^2=4$, noise variance $\sigma_w^2=0.25$ (noise standard deviation $0.5$), and the readings $x=(1.1219,\,2.4523,\,2.1952,\,2.6681,\,1.8498)$, generated from the true coefficients $(1,\,0.5,\,-0.05)$. Then (all values from computes/g6_s3.py section C) $$\mu=(0.062177,\;1.459398,\;-0.216699),\qquad \sqrt{\Sigma_{jj}}=(0.894923,\;0.686263,\;0.114485)$$ (C_post_mean, C_post_sd; the full covariance is C_post_cov). The posterior standard deviations say how much uncertainty is left: the quadratic coefficient is pinned to $\pm0.11$, the constant term barely at all. The posterior correlations are enormous — $\rho_{01}=-0.917481$ (C_post_corr_01), $\rho_{02}=0.824771$ (C_post_corr_02), $\rho_{12}=-0.974683$ (C_post_corr_12) — which is the mathematical statement of a familiar fact: over a short arc you cannot separate "started higher" from "decelerating harder", only their combination. This is why reporting three separate error bars is misleading and the full posterior matters.

Step 7 — sanity check against no prior. Ordinary least squares (the $\sigma_p^2\to\infty$ limit, in which the prior term vanishes and $\mu\to(A^{\!\top}\!A)^{-1}A^{\!\top}x$) gives $(-0.227720,\;1.696046,\;-0.254814)$ (C_least_squares; the per-coordinate shrinkage percentages are C_shrinkage_pct). The Bayesian estimate is pulled toward the prior mean 0 in every coordinate — that is shrinkage, and it is what a prior buys you when $n$ is small.

Practice 3.1. A sensor is either faulty ($\Theta=1$) or healthy ($\Theta=2$), with prior $p_\Theta(1)=0.3$, $p_\Theta(2)=0.7$. A calibration reading $X$ is normal with variance 1 and mean 1 if the sensor is faulty, mean 0 if it is healthy. You read $x=1.2$. Which of the four Bayes formulas applies, and what is the posterior?
Solution

$\Theta$ is discrete and $X$ is continuous — the second box of Fig. 3.2, with a sum in the denominator and a density in the likelihood: $$p_{\Theta\mid X}(m\mid x)=\frac{p_\Theta(m)f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid m)}{\sum_{j=1}^{2}p_\Theta(j)f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid j)}.$$ The likelihoods at $x=1.2$ are $f_{X\mid\Theta}(1.2\mid 1)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(1.2-1)^2/2}$ and $f_{X\mid\Theta}(1.2\mid 2)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-1.2^2/2}$. Numerators: $0.3\times 0.391043=0.117313$ and $0.7\times 0.194186=0.135930$ (F_P34_numerators). Their sum is the marginal density $f_X(1.2)=0.253243$ (F_P34_fX). Hence $$p_{\Theta\mid X}(1\mid 1.2)=0.463242,\qquad p_{\Theta\mid X}(2\mid 1.2)=0.536758$$ (F_P34_posteriors). Even though the reading 1.2 is more typical of a faulty sensor than of a healthy one (the faulty likelihood is twice the healthy one), the prior 0.7 versus 0.3 wins and the posterior still favors "healthy" — narrowly. Practice 3.2. After multiplying prior by likelihood you obtain $f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=c\,\theta^{5}e^{-4\theta}$ for $\theta>0$. Identify $c$ without integrating from scratch, and give the posterior mean.

3.2 Computing posteriors: the three canonical calculations

Uniform prior, binomial data: the coin of L21 slide 4

L21 slide 4 states the "$\Theta$ continuous, $X$ discrete" formula in a box, poses the coin problem, says "assume a prior on $\Theta$ (e.g., uniform)", and stops. We carry the calculation out (this fills the [DERIVATION GAP] flagged there; it is B&T Example 8.4, p. 417).

Example 3.2 (L21 slide 4 / B&T Example 8.4 — coin with unknown bias). A coin has an unknown probability of heads. Model it as a random variable $\Theta$ with the uniform prior on $[0,1]$ — the flat prior that says "before tossing, I regard all biases as equally plausible". Toss the coin $n$ times independently (conditionally on $\Theta=\theta$) and let $X$ be the number of heads. Observe $X=k$. Find the posterior, and evaluate it for $n=10$, $k=7$.
Solution

Step 1 — the variant. $\Theta$ is continuous, $X$ is discrete: third box of Fig. 3.2, exactly the boxed pair on L21 slide 4, $$f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid k)=\frac{f_\Theta(\theta)p_{X\mid\Theta}(k\mid\theta)}{p_X(k)}, \qquad p_X(k)=\int_0^1 f_\Theta(\theta)\,p_{X\mid\Theta}(k\mid\theta)\,d\theta .$$

Step 2 — prior. $f_\Theta(\theta)=1$ for $0\le\theta\le1$ and $0$ otherwise.

Step 3 — likelihood. Given $\Theta=\theta$, the tosses are independent Bernoulli$(\theta)$ trials, so the number of heads is binomial (G2 §1): $$p_{X\mid\Theta}(k\mid\theta)=\binom{n}{k}\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n .$$

Step 4 — the normalizing integral. Substituting into the boxed formula, $$p_X(k)=\int_0^1 1\cdot\binom{n}{k}\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}\,d\theta =\binom{n}{k}\int_0^1\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}\,d\theta .$$ The remaining integral is the Beta function $B(k+1,n-k+1)=\dfrac{k!\,(n-k)!}{(n+1)!}$ (B&T Example 8.4, p. 417; it also follows from integration by parts $k$ times). Hence $$p_X(k)=\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}\cdot\frac{k!\,(n-k)!}{(n+1)!}=\frac{n!}{(n+1)!}=\frac{1}{n+1}.$$ Every one of the $n+1$ possible head-counts is equally likely a priori — a small miracle that is special to the flat prior.

Step 5 — the posterior. Dividing, $$\boxed{\;f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid k)=\frac{(n+1)!}{k!\,(n-k)!}\,\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}, \qquad 0\le\theta\le1,\;}$$ which is the beta density with parameters $\alpha=k+1$ and $\beta=n-k+1$.

Step 6 — numbers for $n=10$, $k=7$. (All from computes/g6_s3.py section A.) $\int_0^1\theta^7(1-\theta)^3d\theta=7!\,3!/11!=1/1320=0.000757576$ (A_beta_integral_exact, reproduced by quadrature as A_beta_integral_quad); $\binom{10}{7}=120$ (A_comb_n_k); so $p_X(7)=120/1320=1/11=0.090909$ (A_pX_k), matching $1/(n+1)$ exactly (A_pX_uniform_max_dev $=0$ across all $k$). The posterior is Beta$(8,4)$, with $$\text{mode}=\frac{k}{n}=0.7,\qquad \text{mean}=\frac{k+1}{n+2}=\frac{8}{12}=0.666667,\qquad \text{sd}=0.130744,$$ (A_MAP, A_post_mean, A_post_sd) and the density at the mode is $2.935107$ (A_post_density_at_MAP). The posterior assigns probability $\mathbf{P}(\Theta>0.5\mid X=7)=0.886719$ (A_post_P_gt_half) to the coin being biased toward heads; its median is $0.676196$ (A_post_median). (Where the mean and the mode come from is §3.3's business — the mean of a Beta$(\alpha,\beta)$ is $\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$, here $8/12$.)

Fig. 3.3 — Prior to posterior: the flat prior narrows into a spike as data accumulate (L21 slide 4; B&T Example 8.4). The observed head frequency is held at $0.7$ while $n$ grows. Posterior standard deviations: $0.288675$ (prior), $0.130744$ ($n=10$), $0.097120$ ($n=20$), $0.070500$ ($n=40$), $0.045320$ ($n=100$) (A_narrowing). Multiplying $n$ by 10 shrinks the spread by a factor $0.346632$ (A_sd_ratio_100_over_10) — the $1/\sqrt n$ rate of the CLT in §2 predicts $1/\sqrt{10}=0.316228$, and at these small $n$ the agreement is only fair (9.6% off, A_sd_ratio_100_over_10_rel_err), because the Beta sd carries an $n+2$ and an $n+3$ where the CLT carries a bare $n$. Take the decade further out and the constant washes out: $n=100\to1000$ gives $0.319399$ (A_sd_ratio_1000_over_100), within 1.0% of $1/\sqrt{10}$.
Interpretation (the prior is one fictitious observation of each kind). The flat prior is Beta$(1,1)$, and the posterior is Beta$(k+1,n-k+1)$: the data add $k$ to the first parameter and $n-k$ to the second. So the flat prior behaves exactly like one previously-seen head and one previously-seen tail. That is why the posterior mean is $(k+1)/(n+2)$ rather than $k/n$ — it is the head frequency after those two imaginary tosses are appended. With $n=10$, $k=7$: $8/12$ instead of $7/10$. With $n=100$, $k=70$: $71/102=0.696078$ instead of $0.7$ — the fiction is drowned out. This is what "the data eventually swamp the prior" means concretely, and it is the same convergence the weak law gave us in §1.

Normal prior, normal noise: the conjugate workhorse

Example 3.3 (B&T Example 8.3, p. 415 — inference of a common mean). An unknown quantity $\Theta$ (a voltage, a position, a physical constant) has a normal prior with mean $x_0$ and variance $\sigma_0^2$. We take $n$ measurements $$X_i=\Theta+W_i,\qquad i=1,\dots,n,$$ where the noises $W_i$ are independent normal, mean 0, known variances $\sigma_1^2,\dots,\sigma_n^2$, independent of $\Theta$. Find the posterior of $\Theta$ given $X=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$.
Solution

Step 1 — variant and ingredients. Both continuous: fourth box of Fig. 3.2. Prior and likelihood, with $c_1,c_2$ the normalizing constants that do not involve $\theta$: $$f_\Theta(\theta)=c_1\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(\theta-x_0)^2}{2\sigma_0^2}\Big),\qquad f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)=c_2\prod_{i=1}^{n}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x_i-\theta)^2}{2\sigma_i^2}\Big).$$ Conditional independence of the $X_i$ given $\Theta=\theta$ is what lets us multiply.

Step 2 — a notational trick. Write the prior term as the $i=0$ term of the product by setting $\sigma_0^2$ to be the prior variance and treating $x_0$, the prior mean, as a "measurement number zero". Then the numerator of Bayes' rule is $$f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)=c_1c_2\exp\!\Big(-\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{(x_i-\theta)^2}{2\sigma_i^2}\Big).$$ This is B&T's compression, and it already tells you the answer's shape.

Step 3 — complete the square. Expand the exponent, writing $\tau_i=1/\sigma_i^2$ for the precision of measurement $i$ (precision = 1/variance; it is the natural currency here): $$\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{(x_i-\theta)^2}{2\sigma_i^2} =\frac12\Big[\theta^2\sum_{i=0}^{n}\tau_i-2\theta\sum_{i=0}^{n}\tau_i x_i+\sum_{i=0}^{n}\tau_i x_i^2\Big].$$ Put $T=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\tau_i$ and $S=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\tau_i x_i$. The bracket is $T\theta^2-2S\theta+\text{const}=T\big(\theta-S/T\big)^2+\text{const}'$, where both constants are free of $\theta$. Therefore $$f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)=d\cdot\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(\theta-m)^2}{2v}\Big), \qquad m=\frac{S}{T},\quad v=\frac{1}{T},$$ with $d$ depending on the $x_i$ but not on $\theta$.

Step 4 — divide by the denominator. The denominator of Bayes' rule is also free of $\theta$, so it merely rescales $d$. A density proportional to $\exp(-(\theta-m)^2/2v)$ must be the normal density with mean $m$ and variance $v$ — no integral needed. Hence $$\boxed{\;\Theta\mid X=x\ \sim\ \mathcal N(m,v),\qquad m=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_i/\sigma_i^2}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}1/\sigma_i^2},\qquad v=\frac{1}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}1/\sigma_i^2}.\;}$$ In words: posterior precision = prior precision + sum of data precisions, and the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the data, with the prior mean counted as one more observation.

Step 5 — numbers, one observation. Prior $\mathcal N(1,4)$, noise variance $\sigma^2=1$, observe $x=3$. Prior precision $1/4=0.25$, data precision $1/1=1$, posterior precision $1.25$ (B1_post_precision), so $v=1/1.25=0.8$ (B1_post_var) and posterior sd $=0.894427$ (B1_post_sd). The mean is $$m=\frac{1/4+3/1}{1.25}=\frac{0.25+3}{1.25}=\frac{3.25}{1.25}=2.6 $$ (B1_post_mean). The weight on the observation is $1/(1.25)=0.8$ (B1_weight_on_x) and on the prior mean $0.25/1.25=0.2$ (B1_weight_on_prior): the datum is four times as precise as the prior, so it gets four times the weight. Brute-force numerical integration of prior$\times$likelihood reproduces $m=2.6$ and $v=0.8$ to 13 decimals (B1_post_mean_quad, B1_post_var_quad, errors B1_mean_err and B1_var_err), and the marginal $f_X(3)$ equals the $\mathcal N(1,\,4+1)$ density at 3, namely $0.119593$ (B1_Z_quad versus B1_fX_closed_form, agreeing to B1_fX_err).

Step 6 — equal variances. If $\sigma_0^2=\sigma_1^2=\dots=\sigma_n^2=\sigma^2$ the formulas collapse to $$m=\frac{x_0+x_1+\dots+x_n}{n+1},\qquad v=\frac{\sigma^2}{n+1},$$ so the prior mean is literally averaged in as one extra data point, and the posterior standard deviation decays like $\sigma/\sqrt{n+1}$ — again the $1/\sqrt n$ of §1–§2. Worked instance with five measurements $x=(2.97455,\,2.511645,\,1.90224,\,4.454797,\,2.452679)$ drawn around a true value $2.4$ (B2_xs, B2_truth), prior $\mathcal N(0,1)$, $\sigma^2=1$: $m=14.295911/6=2.382652$ (B2_post_mean), $v=1/6=0.166667$ (B2_post_var), sd $=0.408248$ (B2_post_sd). The plain sample average is $2.859182$ (B2_sample_mean); the posterior mean is pulled down toward the prior mean 0, which is shrinkage again.

Interpretation (why this example matters more than any other). Two features, both remarked on by B&T (§8.1, p. 416). (i) Conjugacy: prior normal in, posterior normal out. The posterior is described by two numbers, so you never carry a function around. (ii) Recursion: because the posterior is in the same family as the prior, when a new measurement $x_{n+1}$ with variance $\sigma_{n+1}^2$ arrives you can treat the old posterior $(m,v)$ as the new prior and update, $$m_{\text{new}}=\frac{m/v+x_{n+1}/\sigma_{n+1}^2}{1/v+1/\sigma_{n+1}^2},\qquad v_{\text{new}}=\frac{1}{1/v+1/\sigma_{n+1}^2},$$ instead of redoing the whole problem. That recursion, run in real time, is the Kalman filter, and it is why this example underlies tracking, navigation, and sensor fusion. The compute script verifies sequential-equals-batch on the five-measurement example: updating on the first four gives $(2.368646,\,0.2)$ (B2_after4_mean, B2_after4_var), then folding in the fifth reproduces $(2.382652,\,0.166667)$ (B2_recursive_mean, B2_recursive_var) to machine precision (B2_recursive_matches_batch).

Because parameter dependence is the story here — how the answer slides between the prior mean and the data as the two variances change — it is worth playing with directly.

Gotcha (the likelihood is not a density in $\theta$). The orange curve in the widget is $\theta\mapsto f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$ with $x$ nailed down at the observed value. It is a perfectly good function of $\theta$, but it is not a probability density in $\theta$ and generally does not integrate to 1 over $\theta$. Only after multiplying by the prior and renormalizing do you get a density. In this normal example the likelihood happens to be a bell-shaped curve in $\theta$ too (because $(x-\theta)^2$ is symmetric in its two arguments), which makes it easy to forget; in the coin example the likelihood $\binom{n}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}$ integrates to $1/(n+1)$ over $\theta$, not 1.

Uniform prior, uniform-scale observations: Romeo and Juliet

Example 3.4 (rec22 Problem 1(a),(b); B&T Example 8.2, p. 414). Romeo and Juliet start dating. Juliet is late on any date by a random amount $X$, uniformly distributed on $[0,\theta]$. The maximum possible lateness $\theta$ is unknown and is modeled as a random variable $\Theta$, uniform between 0 and 1 hour.
(a) Juliet is late by an amount $x$ on the first date. How should Romeo update the distribution of $\Theta$?
(b) How should Romeo update it after observing latenesses $x_1,\dots,x_n$ on the first $n$ dates, assuming that given $\Theta=\theta$ the $X_i$ are uniform on $[0,\theta]$ and conditionally independent?
Note. The official rec22 solution document contains no derivation at all — it only cites "Example 8.2 page 414". Everything below fills that [SOLUTION GAP].
Solution

(a) Step 1 — variant. $\Theta$ continuous, $X$ continuous: fourth box of Fig. 3.2.

Step 2 — prior, with support. $$f_\Theta(\theta)=\begin{cases}1,&0\le\theta\le1,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

Step 3 — likelihood, with support. Given $\Theta=\theta$, $X$ is uniform on $[0,\theta]$, so its density is the constant $1/\theta$ on that interval: $$f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)=\begin{cases}1/\theta,&0\le x\le\theta,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$ Note carefully: the height $1/\theta$ does depend on $\theta$ and must not be dropped, and the condition $x\le\theta$ is a constraint on $\theta$ once $x$ is observed.

Step 4 — intersect the supports. The product $f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$ is nonzero exactly when $0\le x\le\theta\le1$. Read as a statement about $\theta$ with $x$ fixed: $$\theta\in[x,1].$$ This is the entire content of the observation — Juliet was $x$ late, so the maximum possible lateness is at least $x$. Values of $\theta$ below $x$ are now impossible; they had positive prior density and get posterior density zero.

Step 5 — normalize. With $x\in[0,1]$, $$\int_x^1 f_\Theta(\theta')f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta')\,d\theta' =\int_x^1\frac{1}{\theta'}\,d\theta'=\big[\ln\theta'\big]_x^1=\ln 1-\ln x=-\ln x=|\ln x|,$$ where $|\ln x|=-\ln x$ because $0<x<1$ makes $\ln x$ negative. Therefore $$\boxed{\;f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\theta\,|\ln x|},&x\le\theta\le1,\\[4pt] 0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\;}$$

Step 6 — read it. The posterior is decreasing in $\theta$ on $[x,1]$: given that Juliet was $x$ late, small values of $\theta$ just above $x$ are the most plausible, because a small $\theta$ makes a lateness near its own maximum relatively likely (density $1/\theta$ is large), whereas a large $\theta$ spreads its probability thinly and makes the observed small lateness a coincidence. For $x=0.5$: $|\ln 0.5|=0.693147$ (D_abs_log_x), the posterior runs from height $1/(0.5\times0.693147)=2.885390$ at $\theta=0.5$ (D_post_at_x) down to $1/0.693147=1.442695$ at $\theta=1$ (D_post_at_1), and it integrates to 1 (D_post_integrates_to).

(b) $n$ dates. Conditional independence lets us multiply the $n$ likelihoods: $$f_{X\mid\Theta}(x_1,\dots,x_n\mid\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{X_i\mid\Theta}(x_i\mid\theta) =\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\theta^{n}},&0\le x_i\le\theta\ \text{ for every }i,\\[4pt] 0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$ "$x_i\le\theta$ for every $i$" is the single condition $\bar x\le\theta$ where $$\bar x=\max\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$$ is the largest observed lateness — the only feature of the data that matters (a sufficient statistic, though 6.041 does not use the word until the next chapter). So $$f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{c(\bar x)}{\theta^{n}},&\bar x\le\theta\le1,\\[4pt] 0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases} \qquad c(\bar x)=\frac{1}{\displaystyle\int_{\bar x}^{1}\frac{d\theta'}{(\theta')^{n}}}.$$ Evaluate the constant for $n\ge2$ (the case $n=1$ is part (a)): $$\int_{\bar x}^{1}(\theta')^{-n}\,d\theta'=\Big[\frac{(\theta')^{1-n}}{1-n}\Big]_{\bar x}^{1} =\frac{1-\bar x^{\,1-n}}{1-n}=\frac{\bar x^{\,1-n}-1}{n-1} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;c(\bar x)=\frac{n-1}{\bar x^{\,1-n}-1}.\;}$$ Check with numbers ($\bar x=0.5$): $n=2$ gives $c=1/(2-1)=1$ (D_n2_xbar0.5); $n=3$ gives $c=2/(4-1)=2/3=0.666667$ (D_n3_xbar0.5, fraction D_n3_half_c_frac); $n=5$ gives $c=4/(16-1)=0.266667$ (D_n5_xbar0.5); $n=10$ gives $c=0.017613$ (D_n10_xbar0.5). Each entry carries a norm_check field confirming by numerical integration that the posterior integrates to $1.000000$.

What the extra dates buy. The support shrinks only through $\bar x$, which can only grow with $n$; but the shape sharpens fast, because $\theta^{-n}$ falls off ever more steeply. With $\bar x$ held at $0.5$, the posterior mean drops from $0.721348$ ($n=1$, D_n1_xbar0.5) to $0.693147$ ($n=2$, D_n2_xbar0.5), $0.666667$ ($n=3$, D_n3_xbar0.5), $0.622222$ ($n=5$, D_n5_xbar0.5), $0.561399$ ($n=10$, D_n10_xbar0.5) — converging down onto $\bar x=0.5$ itself. More data pushes the belief toward "$\theta$ is just barely above the largest lateness you have seen".

Fig. 3.4 — The Romeo–Juliet posterior for three observed latenesses (rec22 P1(a); B&T Example 8.2). The density is zero below $x$, jumps to $1/(x|\ln x|)$, and decays like $1/\theta$ up to 1. Because the density is decreasing, the MAP sits at the left edge $\theta=x$ while the mean $(1-x)/|\ln x|$ sits well to its right — the skew that §3.3 exploits.
Gotcha (conjugacy is the exception, not the rule). Two of our three canonical calculations were conjugate — beta prior stays beta under binomial data, normal prior stays normal under normal noise — and it is tempting to expect the pattern always. It does not hold. Romeo–Juliet is not the counterexample, despite appearances: the posterior $c(\bar x)/\theta^{n}$ on $[\bar x,1]$ is not uniform, but the larger family $\{c/\theta^{m}\ \text{on}\ [b,1]\}$ that it belongs to is closed under further $\mathrm{U}[0,\Theta]$ observations — one more lateness $x$ simply sends $(m,b)\mapsto(m+1,\max(b,x))$ — and the uniform prior is its $m=0$, $b=0$ member. So uniform prior $+$ $\mathrm{U}[0,\Theta]$ data is a conjugate pair too (the Pareto family, in the usual naming). A genuine failure is Practice 3.4: uniform prior on $[1,2]$ for an exponential rate gives the posterior $\lambda e^{-x\lambda}/Z$ on $[1,2]$, which is not uniform — the uniform family is not closed, and you must enlarge it to truncated gammas before conjugacy returns. That is the usual situation: conjugacy holds for particular prior/likelihood pairs, and the pair you are handed is usually not one of them. B&T note (§8.1, p. 416) that "this situation where the posterior is in the same family as the prior is not very common"; outside the normal and beta/Bernoulli pairs you should expect to integrate.

3.3 Point estimates: MAP versus conditional expectation

The posterior is the complete answer, and L21 slide 5 shows it in both flavors — a stem plot when $\Theta$ is discrete, a curve when it is continuous. But you often have to report one number: a decision, a dial setting, a headline. Two candidates dominate.

Definition (estimator, estimate; B&T §8.2, pp. 422–423). An estimator is a random variable $\hat\Theta=g(X)$ for some function $g$; its realized value $\hat\theta=g(x)$, once $X=x$ is observed, is an estimate. The estimator is random because the data are; the estimate is a number.
Definition (the two point estimates; L21 slide 5; B&T §8.2, p. 423). Given the observed value $x$:
  • The maximum a posteriori probability (MAP) estimate $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}$ is a value maximizing the posterior over $\theta$, ties broken arbitrarily: $$p_{\Theta\mid X}(\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}\mid x)=\max_\theta p_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x) \quad\text{or}\quad f_{\Theta\mid X}(\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}\mid x)=\max_\theta f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x).$$ Equivalently — and this is how you always compute it — it maximizes the numerator $p_\Theta(\theta)p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$, $p_\Theta(\theta)f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$, $f_\Theta(\theta)p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$, or $f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$, according to the case, since the denominator does not depend on $\theta$.
  • The conditional expectation (LMS) estimate is $$\hat\theta_{\text{LMS}}=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]=\int\theta\,f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)\,d\theta \quad\text{or}\quad \sum_\theta\theta\,p_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x).$$
(L21 slide 5 prints the conditional-expectation line with a typo, conditioning on $X=y$ while integrating $f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)$; read it as $X=x$ throughout.)
Property (MAP minimizes the probability of error; L21 slide 5; B&T §8.2, p. 421 and its footnote). Suppose $\Theta$ takes finitely many values. Let $g$ be any decision rule and let $g_{\text{MAP}}$ be the MAP rule. Then for every observation value $x$, $$\mathbf{P}\big(g(X)\ne\Theta\mid X=x\big)\;\ge\;\mathbf{P}\big(g_{\text{MAP}}(X)\ne\Theta\mid X=x\big),$$ and consequently $\mathbf{P}(g(X)\ne\Theta)\ge\mathbf{P}(g_{\text{MAP}}(X)\ne\Theta)$.

Proof (the slide's footnote, spelled out). Fix $x$. Whatever value $\theta_1=g(x)$ the rule reports, it is correct exactly when $\Theta=\theta_1$, so $$\mathbf{P}\big(g(X)=\Theta\mid X=x\big)=p_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta_1\mid x).$$ The MAP rule reports a maximizer, so its conditional probability of being correct is $\max_\theta p_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)$, which is $\ge p_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta_1\mid x)$ by definition of maximum. Taking complements gives the conditional statement. For the unconditional one, let $I=1$ if $g$ is correct and $I=0$ otherwise, similarly $I_{\text{MAP}}$; then $\mathbb{E}[I\mid X]\le\mathbb{E}[I_{\text{MAP}}\mid X]$ pointwise, and the law of iterated expectations (G4 §1) gives $\mathbb{E}[I]\le\mathbb{E}[I_{\text{MAP}}]$, i.e. $\mathbf{P}(g(X)=\Theta)\le\mathbf{P}(g_{\text{MAP}}(X)=\Theta)$. $\blacksquare$

Gotcha. This optimality is a statement about discrete $\Theta$. If $\Theta$ is continuous then $\mathbf{P}(\hat\Theta=\Theta)=0$ for every rule, so "minimizes the probability of error" is vacuous — every rule is tied at error probability 1 (B&T §8.2, p. 421 footnote). For continuous $\Theta$, MAP is still a reasonable and very widely used estimator, but its justification is "report the most likely value", not an optimality theorem. The estimator with a genuine optimality theorem in the continuous case is $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$, which minimizes mean squared error — proved in §4.
Example 3.5 (rec22 Problem 1(c); B&T Example 8.7, p. 424 — MAP for Romeo and Juliet). MAP. Example 3.6 (B&T Example 8.8, p. 424 — MAP and conditional expectation for the coin). For the coin of Example 3.2 (uniform prior, $k$ heads in $n$ tosses), derive both point estimates.
Solution

MAP. Maximize the numerator $\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}$ — no need for the Beta constant. Take logarithms (legitimate because $\ln$ is increasing, so it does not move the maximizer): $$h(\theta)=k\ln\theta+(n-k)\ln(1-\theta),\qquad h'(\theta)=\frac{k}{\theta}-\frac{n-k}{1-\theta}.$$ Set $h'(\theta)=0$: $$k(1-\theta)=(n-k)\theta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k-k\theta=n\theta-k\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\;\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\frac{k}{n}.\;}$$ This is the observed head frequency — the flat prior contributes nothing to the mode. (The stationary point is a maximum since $h''<0$ throughout $(0,1)$; for $k=0$ or $k=n$ the maximum moves to the boundary, $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=0$ or 1.)

Conditional expectation. The posterior is Beta$(k+1,n-k+1)$, whose mean is $\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$: $$\boxed{\;\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=k]=\frac{k+1}{n+2}.\;}$$ (Directly: $\int_0^1\theta\cdot\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}d\theta\big/\int_0^1\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}d\theta =\frac{(k+1)!(n-k)!/(n+2)!}{k!(n-k)!/(n+1)!}=\frac{k+1}{n+2}$.)

Contrast. The two answers differ most when $n$ is tiny: $n=1$, $k=1$ gives MAP $=1$ but mean $=2/3=0.666667$ (A_contrast_n1_k1); $n=2$, $k=2$ gives MAP $=1$, mean $=0.75$ (A_contrast_n2_k2); $n=4$, $k=3$ gives $0.75$ versus $0.666667$ (A_contrast_n4_k3); $n=10$, $k=7$ gives $0.7$ versus $0.666667$ (A_contrast_n10_k7); and for large $n$ they nearly coincide, since $\frac{k+1}{n+2}\to\frac kn$. The MAP's willingness to answer "$\hat\theta=1$" after two heads — declaring a two-headed coin on the strength of two tosses — is exactly the behavior the extra $+1,+2$ in the posterior mean tempers.

When a single number lies

L21 slide 5 ends with the warning "Single answers can be misleading!" and draws a bimodal posterior next to a discrete one. Here is a concrete instance with numbers.

Example 3.7 (L21 slide 5 — a bimodal posterior). An inference problem yields the posterior $$f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=0.45\cdot\mathcal N(\theta;\,0,\,0.30^2)+0.55\cdot\mathcal N(\theta;\,4,\,1.20^2),$$ a mixture of a tight peak near 0 and a broad hump near 4 (this shape arises whenever the prior itself is a mixture — "the target is in lane A or lane B" — and the data do not resolve which). Compute the MAP and the conditional expectation and comment.
Solution

The two local maxima are at $\theta\approx 0.000296$ (E_MAP; density $0.599121$, E_density_at_MAP) and $\theta\approx 4.000000$ (E_local_peak2; density $0.182849$, E_density_at_peak2), so the global maximum — the MAP — is essentially $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=0$. By linearity of expectation for mixtures, the posterior mean is $$\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]=0.45\times 0+0.55\times 4=2.2 $$ (E_post_mean_formula, matched by quadrature as E_post_mean). Now evaluate the posterior density at the posterior mean: it is $0.059362$ (E_density_at_mean), which is $10.1$ times smaller than the density at the MAP (E_density_ratio_MAP_over_mean). Between the two humps the density has a valley at $\theta=1.035570$ (E_valley_location). The posterior probability of landing within $\pm0.5$ of the reported mean is only $0.061430$ (E_P_within_0p5_of_mean), while within $\pm0.5$ of the MAP it is $0.407914$ (E_P_within_0p5_of_MAP).

Comment. The conditional expectation is a value the parameter is very unlikely to be near — it sits in the trough. The MAP is a genuinely likely value but discards the 55% of posterior probability sitting near 4, so as a summary it is equally dishonest. Both single numbers are defensible answers to precisely stated questions ("minimize squared error"; "name the single most likely value") and both are terrible descriptions of the posterior. Report the posterior, or at least report that it is bimodal.

Recipe (which point estimate?).
  1. Is $\Theta$ discrete with few values? Use MAP. It provably minimizes the probability of an incorrect decision, both conditionally on each $x$ and overall. Compute it by comparing the numerators $p_\Theta(\theta)p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$ (or with $f_{X\mid\Theta}$) — never bother with the denominator.
  2. Is $\Theta$ continuous and is your loss squared error? Use $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$; §4 proves it is optimal for that loss.
  3. Is $\Theta$ continuous and do you want "the most likely value"? Use MAP. Maximize $\ln\big(f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)\big)$ — logs turn products into sums — by setting the derivative to zero, and then check the boundary of the support, which is where the maximum lives whenever the posterior is monotone (Example 3.5).
  4. Is the posterior normal, or symmetric and unimodal? Then the two estimates coincide and the choice is moot (B&T §8.2, p. 423). Example 3.3 and Example 3.1 are of this type.
  5. Is the posterior bimodal or badly skewed? Refuse to summarize. Report the posterior, a credible interval, or both modes (Example 3.7).

Gotcha (MAP is not invariant under reparametrization). Suppose you decide to work with $\varphi=g(\Theta)$ instead of $\Theta$ for some smooth invertible $g$. The posterior density transforms with a Jacobian factor, $f_{\varphi\mid X}(\varphi\mid x)=f_{\Theta\mid X}\big(g^{-1}(\varphi)\mid x\big)\cdot\big|\frac{d}{d\varphi}g^{-1}(\varphi)\big|$ (G3 §4), and a Jacobian that varies with $\varphi$ moves the maximum. Concretely, take the Beta$(8,4)$ posterior of Example 3.2 ($n=10$, $k=7$), whose MAP in $\theta$ is $0.7$ (H_theta_MAP):
  • in the logit coordinate $\varphi=\ln\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}$ the Jacobian is $\theta(1-\theta)$, so you maximize $\theta^{8}(1-\theta)^{4}$ and land at $8/12=0.666667$ (H_logit_MAP_back_in_theta);
  • in the coordinate $\varphi=\theta^{2}$ the Jacobian is $1/(2\sqrt\varphi)$, so you maximize $\theta^{6}(1-\theta)^{3}$ and land at $6/9=0.666667$ (H_square_MAP_back_in_theta);
  • in the coordinate $\varphi=1/\theta$ the Jacobian is $1/\varphi^{2}$, so you maximize $\theta^{9}(1-\theta)^{3}$ and land at $9/12=0.75$ (H_recip_MAP_back_in_theta).
Three different answers — $0.7$, $0.666667$, $0.75$ (H_MAPs_differ) — for the same posterior, depending only on which coordinate you happened to write it in. The conditional expectation has no such pathology in the sense that $\mathbb{E}[g(\Theta)\mid X=x]$ is a single unambiguous number for each $g$; but note it does not commute with $g$ either — for this posterior $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid x]=0.666667$ (H_post_mean_theta) while $\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2\mid x]=0.461538\ne0.666667^2=0.444444$ (H_post_mean_theta_sq). Moral: say which parametrization you are maximizing in, and do not read too much metaphysics into a mode.
Practice 3.6. In Practice 3.4 (uniform$[1,2]$ prior on the failure rate, one exponential observation at $x=0.4$), find the MAP estimate of $\Lambda$. Compare it with the posterior mean $1.523118$.
Solution

Maximize the numerator $\lambda e^{-0.4\lambda}$ over $\lambda\in[1,2]$. Setting the derivative $e^{-0.4\lambda}(1-0.4\lambda)$ to zero gives the interior candidate $\lambda=1/0.4=2.5$ (F_P32_unconstrained_stationary_point) — which lies outside $[1,2]$. On $[1,2]$ we have $1-0.4\lambda\ge1-0.8=0.2>0$, so the numerator is increasing throughout and the maximum is at the right endpoint: $$\hat\lambda_{\text{MAP}}=2 $$ (F_P32_MAP). Here the MAP ($2$) exceeds the posterior mean ($1.523118$, F_P32_post_mean), the reverse of the Romeo–Juliet ordering, because this posterior is skewed the other way. The lesson is the one from Example 3.5: always check the endpoints of the support; the derivative-equals-zero recipe alone would have produced the nonsense answer $2.5$, a rate the prior declares impossible.

Practice 3.7. You toss a coin with uniform prior on its bias $n=3$ times and get 3 heads. Give the posterior, the MAP estimate, and the conditional expectation. Which would you report to someone about to bet on the next toss, and why?
Solution

By Example 3.2 the posterior is Beta$(k+1,n-k+1)=$ Beta$(4,1)$, i.e. $f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid 3)=4\theta^{3}$ on $[0,1]$. This is increasing, so the maximum is at the boundary: $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=k/n=1$. The conditional expectation is $(k+1)/(n+2)=4/5=0.8$.

Report $0.8$. The MAP says "this coin never lands tails", a claim three tosses cannot support, and it is exactly the kind of over-commitment the Gotcha above warns about; moreover, if what you care about is the chance the next toss is heads, the correct quantity is $\mathbf{P}(X_{4}=1\mid X=3)=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=3]=0.8$ by the total expectation theorem (G4 §1) — the conditional expectation, not the mode, is what predicts the next toss.

Practice 3.8. True or false, with reasons. (i) The posterior density $f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)$ always has smaller variance than the prior $f_\Theta(\theta)$. (ii) To compute a MAP estimate you never need the denominator of Bayes' rule. (iii) If the posterior is normal, MAP and conditional expectation agree.
Solution

(i) False. Data can widen the posterior. Take a prior concentrated near 0 and an observation strongly favoring a distant region: the posterior can straddle both, as in Example 3.7, whose posterior variance far exceeds that of the tight prior component. The general guarantee is about averages: $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)]\le\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$ by the law of total variance (G4 §1) — on average the posterior is narrower, not always.

(ii) True, and it is the main computational saving of the MAP rule: the denominator is a function of $x$ only, so it cannot affect which $\theta$ maximizes (B&T §8.2, p. 421). You do need it if you want to report a posterior probability, a variance, or a conditional expectation.

(iii) True. A normal density is symmetric and unimodal about its mean, so its maximum is at the mean; hence $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=m=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$. This is the case in Example 3.3 (posterior mean $2.6$ = MAP; the Beta case's numerical mode check is A_MAP_numeric) and Example 3.1 (posterior mean vector = MAP vector, which the compute script confirms by direct numerical maximization, C_MAP_numeric, to within $2.6\times10^{-9}$, C_MAP_matches_mean_maxerr).

§3 cheatsheet.
  • Setup. Prior $p_\Theta$ or $f_\Theta$; observation model $p_{X\mid\Theta}$ or $f_{X\mid\Theta}$; observed value $x$; answer = posterior $p_{\Theta\mid X}(\cdot\mid x)$ or $f_{\Theta\mid X}(\cdot\mid x)$ (L21 slides 2–3; B&T §8.1, p. 413).
  • Four Bayes variants, chosen by (is $\Theta$ discrete?) $\times$ (is $X$ discrete?): posterior $=$ prior $\times$ likelihood $/$ (sum or integral of the same). Denominator is free of $\theta$ — drop it whenever you only need a shape or a maximizer (Fig. 3.2).
  • Uniform prior + Binomial$(n,\theta)$ data $\Rightarrow$ posterior Beta$(k+1,n-k+1)$; marginal $p_X(k)=1/(n+1)$; MAP $=k/n$; mean $=(k+1)/(n+2)$. For $n=10$, $k=7$: Beta$(8,4)$, MAP $0.7$ (A_MAP), mean $0.666667$ (A_post_mean), sd $0.130744$ (A_post_sd) (Example 3.2, Example 3.6; Fig. 3.3).
  • Normal prior + normal noise $\Rightarrow$ normal posterior with precision $1/v=\sum_{i=0}^{n}1/\sigma_i^2$ and mean $m=v\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_i/\sigma_i^2$ (prior mean $=$ observation number 0). Conjugate and recursive; MAP $=$ mean. Example: prior $\mathcal N(1,4)$, $\sigma^2=1$, $x=3$ $\Rightarrow$ $m=2.6$ (B1_post_mean), $v=0.8$ (B1_post_var) (Example 3.3; widget).
  • Uniform prior + $\mathrm{U}[0,\Theta]$ data $\Rightarrow$ $f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=1/(\theta|\ln x|)$ on $[x,1]$; with $n$ dates, $c(\bar x)/\theta^{n}$ on $[\bar x,1]$ with $c(\bar x)=(n-1)/(\bar x^{1-n}-1)$ and $\bar x=\max_i x_i$ (Example 3.4; Fig. 3.4).
  • MAP. Maximize the numerator. Minimizes $\mathbf{P}(\text{error})$ when $\Theta$ is discrete — conditionally on each $x$ and overall. Check boundaries: for Romeo–Juliet the maximum is at the endpoint, $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=x$ (Example 3.5). Not invariant under reparametrization.
  • Conditional expectation. $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$; for Romeo–Juliet $(1-x)/|\ln x|$, always above the MAP, gap peaking at $0.298426$ (D_max_gap_value) when $x=0.166414$ (D_max_gap_x) (Fig. 3.5). Optimal for squared error — proved in §4.
  • Do not over-summarize. On a bimodal posterior the mean can sit in a valley where the density is $10\times$ lower than at the mode (E_density_ratio_MAP_over_mean; Example 3.7; Fig. 3.6). When the posterior is normal or symmetric-unimodal the two estimates coincide and the worry evaporates (Fig. 3.7).
