Sections 1–3 asked you to produce a number: an estimate $\hat\theta$ of an unknown constant $\theta$, with a bias, a variance, and a confidence interval attached. This section asks you to produce a decision. There are exactly two candidate probabilistic models for the data, and you must pick one. Nothing else changes: $\theta$ is still not random, there is still no prior, and every probability we write still carries a semicolon rather than a conditioning bar.

What is new is that the whole problem collapses onto a single scalar — the ratio of the two likelihoods — and that, once you have fixed how often you are willing to be wrong in one direction, there is a provably best rule. That is the Neyman–Pearson Lemma, and it is one of the few optimality theorems in the course whose statement is as sharp as its proof is short.

Interpretation. Everything in this section is one idea seen four times. Compute how much more likely the data are under $H_1$ than under $H_0$, and reject $H_0$ when that number is large enough. The likelihood ratio test says it directly (§4.2). The normal-mean and normal-variance tests say it after taking a logarithm (§4.3–§4.4). The significance test says it with the ratio hidden inside a hand-picked statistic (§4.7). The chi-square goodness-of-fit statistic is a likelihood ratio, to second order in a Taylor expansion (§4.8).

4.1 The setup: two hypotheses, a rejection region, two errors

Definition (simple binary hypothesis testing; L25 slide 2, B&T §9.3). We observe a random vector $X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ whose distribution is one of exactly two fully specified candidates: $$H_0:\ X\sim p_X(x;H_0)\quad[\text{or } f_X(x;H_0)],\qquad H_1:\ X\sim p_X(x;H_1)\quad[\text{or } f_X(x;H_1)].$$ $H_0$ is the null hypothesis (the default, the thing you would believe without evidence); $H_1$ is the alternative hypothesis. Both are simple: each names one complete distribution, with no free parameters left over. A test is a set $R$ of possible data values, called the rejection region: you reject $H_0$ if and only if the observed $x$ lands in $R$. Its complement $R^c$ is the acceptance region.

The three symbols in that definition are the whole apparatus. $X$ is the data — the random variable before you look, $x$ the number or vector you actually see. $R$ is a subset of the data space, chosen in advance, before any data arrive. And the semicolon in $p_X(x;H_0)$ reads "under hypothesis $H_0$": it is a label on which of the two probabilistic models is being used to compute, not a conditioning event, because in the classical framework $H_0$ is not a random variable and has no probability of its own.

Gotcha (the semicolon is not a bar). Writing $\mathbf{P}(X\in R\mid H_0)$ is a category error here, and the lecture is scrupulous about it (L25 slide 2, and B&T's footnote on p. 487). A conditional probability $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B)$ requires $B$ to be an event in one single probabilistic model. Here there are two models and no umbrella model containing both, because there is no prior. The notation $\mathbf{P}(\cdot\,;H_0)$ means "evaluate this probability in model $H_0$." Contrast G6 §3, where $\Theta$ was random and bars were correct.
Definition (the two error probabilities; L24 slide 7, B&T §9.3 and its Figure 9.8). For a rejection region $R$:
  • False rejection (type I error, "false alarm"): reject $H_0$ although $H_0$ is true. $$\alpha(R)=\mathbf{P}(X\in R;\,H_0).$$ $\alpha$ is also called the significance level of the test.
  • False acceptance (type II error, "missed detection"): fail to reject $H_0$ although $H_1$ is true. $$\beta(R)=\mathbf{P}(X\notin R;\,H_1).$$ The complementary quantity $1-\beta(R)=\mathbf{P}(X\in R;H_1)$ is the power of the test.
There is no third error probability and no "overall" error probability: averaging $\alpha$ and $\beta$ would require weights, and weights are a prior, which we do not have.
Fig. 4.1 — The four outcomes of a binary test. Only the two off-diagonal cells matter, and they are governed by different hypotheses: $\alpha$ is computed entirely inside model $H_0$, $\beta$ entirely inside model $H_1$. Nothing on this table is a probability that a hypothesis is true (L24 slide 7, where the type I / type II vocabulary and both formulas are introduced; B&T §9.3 and its Figure 9.8).
Gotcha (what $\alpha=0.05$ does not mean). Three standard misreadings, all of which the semicolon notation is designed to block.
  1. $\alpha$ is not $\mathbf{P}(H_0\text{ true}\mid\text{rejected})$. That is a posterior probability and needs a prior; you get it from G6 §3, not from here.
  2. $\alpha$ is not the probability that this particular rejection is a mistake. It is a long-run frequency property of the rule $R$, fixed before the data arrive.
  3. Not rejecting is not the same as accepting. Failing to reject $H_0$ at level $0.05$ means only that the data are not startling under $H_0$; $H_1$ may still be far more likely. The wording is deliberate in B&T Example 9.15, and so is L25 slide 5's ("$H_0$ not rejected").
Practice 4.1. A test of $H_0$ against $H_1$ uses rejection region $R$ and has $\alpha(R)=0.04$, $\beta(R)=0.30$. Someone proposes the "opposite" test $R'=R^c$. What are $\alpha(R')$ and $\beta(R')$?
Solution

By definition $\alpha(R')=\mathbf{P}(X\in R^c;H_0)=1-\mathbf{P}(X\in R;H_0)=1-0.04=0.96$, using the complement rule inside model $H_0$. Similarly $\beta(R')=\mathbf{P}(X\notin R^c;H_1)=\mathbf{P}(X\in R;H_1)=1-\beta(R)=1-0.30=0.70$.

So $(\alpha,\beta)$ goes from $(0.04,0.30)$ to $(0.96,0.70)$ — both errors get worse, and the sum went from $0.34$ to $1.66$. Complementing a good region gives a bad region; the two errors do not trade off along that direction. (The genuine tradeoff, §4.5, comes from shrinking $R$, not from flipping it.)

Practice 4.2. Under $H_0$, $X$ is uniform on $[0,1]$; under $H_1$, $X$ is uniform on $[0,2]$. Take $R=\{x: x>0.9\}$. Compute $\alpha$ and $\beta$.
Solution

Under $H_0$ the density is $f_X(x;H_0)=1$ on $[0,1]$, so $\alpha=\mathbf{P}(X>0.9;H_0)=\int_{0.9}^{1}1\,dx=0.1$.

Under $H_1$ the density is $f_X(x;H_1)=1/2$ on $[0,2]$, so the power is $\mathbf{P}(X>0.9;H_1)=\int_{0.9}^{2}\tfrac12\,dx=\tfrac{1.1}{2}=0.55$, hence $\beta=1-0.55=0.45$.

Note the asymmetry: $H_1$ is "true" whenever $X>1$, an event of probability $0.5$ that cannot happen under $H_0$ — yet $\beta$ is still $0.45$, because more than half of the $H_1$ probability sits inside $[0,1]$ where the two models overlap. No test can do better than $\beta=0.5$ at $\alpha=0$ here.

4.2 The likelihood ratio test, and where it comes from

Nothing so far tells you which $R$ to use, and there are $2^{|\text{data space}|}$ of them. The lecture writes down the answer in one line (L25 slide 2); B&T §9.3 motivates it by running the Bayesian machinery of G6 §3 and then deleting the prior. That motivation is worth doing in full, because it explains the otherwise mysterious threshold.

The Bayesian rehearsal. Pretend, temporarily, that $\Theta$ is random and takes the two values $\theta_0$ ("$H_0$") and $\theta_1$ ("$H_1$") with prior probabilities $p_\Theta(\theta_0)$ and $p_\Theta(\theta_1)$. G6 §3 says the rule minimizing the probability of error is MAP: declare $\theta_1$ when the posterior at $\theta_1$ exceeds the posterior at $\theta_0$. By Bayes' rule (G3 §3) the two posteriors share the denominator $p_X(x)$, so declaring $\theta_1$ means $$p_\Theta(\theta_1)\,p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta_1)\ >\ p_\Theta(\theta_0)\,p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta_0).$$ Divide both sides by the positive quantity $p_\Theta(\theta_1)\,p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta_0)$ — division by a positive number preserves the inequality — to isolate the data on the left: $$\frac{p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta_1)}{p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta_0)}\ >\ \frac{p_\Theta(\theta_0)}{p_\Theta(\theta_1)}.$$ The left side depends only on the data; the right side only on the prior. So the optimal Bayesian rule already has the shape "likelihood ratio versus a constant", and that constant is the prior odds in favor of $H_0$.

Definition (likelihood ratio and the LRT; L25 slide 2, B&T §9.3). The likelihood ratio at the observed data $x$ is $$L(x)=\frac{p_X(x;H_1)}{p_X(x;H_0)}\qquad\text{(discrete)},\qquad L(x)=\frac{f_X(x;H_1)}{f_X(x;H_0)}\qquad\text{(continuous)}.$$ The likelihood ratio test (LRT) with critical value $\xi>0$ uses the rejection region $$R=\{x: L(x)>\xi\},\qquad\text{i.e. reject } H_0 \text{ iff } L(x)>\xi .$$ Before the data arrive, $L(X)$ is a random variable — a function of $X$ — and its distribution differs under the two hypotheses. That is exactly what makes $\alpha$ and $\beta$ computable.

The classical version now drops the prior and keeps the shape: $\xi$ is a free dial, set by whatever external requirement you care to impose. The lecture's requirement (L25 slide 2) is a target false rejection probability:

Recipe as a formula (L25 slide 2). Fix $\alpha$ (typically $0.10$, $0.05$ or $0.01$). Choose $\xi$ so that $$\mathbf{P}\big(\text{reject } H_0;\,H_0\big)=\mathbf{P}\big(L(X)>\xi;\,H_0\big)=\alpha,$$ then reject $H_0$ exactly when the realized $L(x)$ exceeds that $\xi$.
Interpretation (why a ratio, and why a threshold). $L(x)$ answers "how many times more likely were these exact data under $H_1$ than under $H_0$?" A value $L(x)=8$ says the data are eight times better explained by $H_1$. The threshold rule is the only sensible way to convert that into a decision, because $L$ is a sufficient summary: two data sets with the same likelihood ratio carry literally the same evidence about which of the two models generated them, so they must be decided the same way. The Neyman–Pearson Lemma of §4.6 turns this plausibility argument into a theorem.
Interpretation (the special case $\xi=1$). $\xi=1$ makes the test "reject $H_0$ iff $p_X(x;H_1)>p_X(x;H_0)$": pick whichever hypothesis makes the data more likely. That is the maximum likelihood rule of §2, applied to a two-point parameter set. In the Bayesian rehearsal it corresponds to $p_\Theta(\theta_0)=p_\Theta(\theta_1)=1/2$, i.e. a flat prior — the same "ML = MAP with a flat prior" bridge B&T draw in §9.1.
Gotcha ($\xi$ is not a probability, and it need not exceed 1). $\xi$ is a threshold on a ratio of densities, so it can be any positive number, and densities are not probabilities: $L(x)$ can be $37$ or $0.004$. In §4.4 you will meet a perfectly ordinary $5\%$-level test whose critical value is $\xi=0.9358$ — smaller than one. That is not a mistake; it merely says that at the $5\%$ level, with $n=10$ observations, you should reject $H_0$ even on data that slightly favor $H_0$ pointwise, because the accumulated evidence structure makes those data sufficiently rare under $H_0$.
Practice 4.3. $X$ is a single observation. Under $H_0$ it is exponential with rate $1$ ($f_X(x;H_0)=e^{-x}$, $x\ge 0$); under $H_1$ it is exponential with rate $2$ ($f_X(x;H_1)=2e^{-2x}$). Find $L(x)$, show the LRT rejects for small $x$, and find the rejection region with $\alpha=0.05$ together with the resulting $\beta$.
Solution

Step 1 — the ratio. $L(x)=\dfrac{2e^{-2x}}{e^{-x}}=2e^{-2x+x}=2e^{-x}$ for $x\ge 0$.

Step 2 — direction. $2e^{-x}$ is strictly decreasing in $x$, so $L(x)>\xi$ is equivalent to $e^{-x}>\xi/2$, i.e. $-x>\ln(\xi/2)$, i.e. $x<\ln(2/\xi)=:c$. The rejection region is a left tail $\{x<c\}$. This is the whole point of doing the algebra rather than guessing: $H_1$ says "the rate is higher", which means "the observations are typically smaller", so small data are the evidence against $H_0$.

Step 3 — calibrate. Under $H_0$, $\mathbf{P}(X<c;H_0)=\int_0^c e^{-x}dx=1-e^{-c}$. Set $1-e^{-c}=0.05$: $e^{-c}=0.95$, so $c=-\ln 0.95=0.05129329$ (P41_C). The check $1-e^{-c}=0.05000000$ is P41_ALPHA_CHECK. The equivalent critical value on the ratio scale is $\xi=2e^{-c}=2(0.95)=1.9$ (P41_XI_EQUIV).

Step 4 — the other error. Under $H_1$, $\beta=\mathbf{P}(X\ge c;H_1)=\int_c^\infty 2e^{-2x}dx=e^{-2c}=(e^{-c})^2=0.95^2=0.9025$ (P41_BETA).

A $\beta$ of $0.9025$ is dismal: one exponential observation simply cannot distinguish rate $1$ from rate $2$ at the $5\%$ level. Nothing is wrong with the test — the Neyman–Pearson Lemma guarantees no other $5\%$ test does better. The fix is more data, not a cleverer region.

4.3 Worked example: the test on a normal mean (L25 slide 3)

Example 4.1 (test on a normal mean; L25 slide 3, filling the slide's "algebra" gap). We have $n$ i.i.d. observations $X_1,\dots,X_n$ and $$H_0:\ X_i\sim N(0,1),\qquad H_1:\ X_i\sim N(1,1).$$ Derive the LRT, reduce it to a threshold on $\sum_i X_i$, choose that threshold for $\alpha=0.05$, and compute $\beta$.

Step 1 — write the two likelihoods. Independence lets us multiply the marginal densities (G3 §2), and each is the $N(\mu,1)$ density $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2}$: $$f_X(x;H_1)=\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Big)^{n}\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-1)^2\Big\}, \qquad f_X(x;H_0)=\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Big)^{n}\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\Big\}.$$ Here $x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is the observed vector and $n$ the sample size.

Step 2 — form the ratio. The constants $\big(1/\sqrt{2\pi}\big)^n$ are identical and cancel; the exponentials combine because $e^a/e^b=e^{a-b}$: $$L(x)=\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^n\big[(x_i-1)^2-x_i^2\big]\Big\}.$$

Step 3 — expand the bracket. $(x_i-1)^2-x_i^2=x_i^2-2x_i+1-x_i^2=-2x_i+1$, so $$-\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^n(-2x_i+1)=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i-\frac{n}{2}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad L(x)=\exp\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i-\frac n2\Big\}.$$ Every $x_i^2$ has vanished: the data enter only through their sum. This is the "algebra" the slide compresses into one word.

Step 4 — take logarithms. $\log$ is strictly increasing, so $L(x)>\xi$ if and only if $\log L(x)>\log\xi$ — the rejection region is unchanged. Thus $$\sum_{i=1}^n x_i-\frac n2>\log\xi \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \sum_{i=1}^n x_i>\log\xi+\frac n2\ =:\ \xi'.$$ The new symbol $\xi'$ is the threshold expressed on the data scale rather than the likelihood-ratio scale. The two are in one-to-one correspondence, $\xi=e^{\xi'-n/2}$, so calibrating either one calibrates the other.

Step 5 — calibrate $\xi'$ (the "use normal tables" gap). Under $H_0$ each $X_i$ is $N(0,1)$ and they are independent, so by the sum-of-independent-normals fact (G3 §4) the statistic $S=\sum_i X_i$ is normal with mean $\sum_i 0=0$ and variance $\sum_i 1=n$; that is, $S\sim N(0,n)$ and $S/\sqrt n\sim N(0,1)$. Therefore $$\alpha=\mathbf{P}(S>\xi';H_0)=\mathbf{P}\Big(\frac{S}{\sqrt n}>\frac{\xi'}{\sqrt n}\Big) =1-\Phi\!\Big(\frac{\xi'}{\sqrt n}\Big),$$ where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF. Setting this to $\alpha$ and solving, $$\boxed{\ \xi'=\sqrt{n}\;z_{1-\alpha}\ },\qquad \Phi(z_{1-\alpha})=1-\alpha .$$ For $\alpha=0.05$, $z_{0.95}=1.6448536$ (Z_0950; the check $1-\Phi(z_{0.95})=0.0500000$ is Z_0950_CHECK_TAIL).

Step 6 — compute $\beta$. Under $H_1$ each $X_i$ is $N(1,1)$, so $S\sim N(n,n)$ — mean $\sum_i 1=n$, variance still $n$. Then $$\beta=\mathbf{P}(S\le\xi';H_1)=\Phi\!\Big(\frac{\xi'-n}{\sqrt n}\Big) =\Phi\!\Big(\frac{\sqrt n\,z_{1-\alpha}-n}{\sqrt n}\Big)=\Phi\big(z_{1-\alpha}-\sqrt n\big).$$ The $n$-dependence has collapsed to a single quantity $\sqrt n$, which is the separation between the two hypotheses measured in standard deviations of the statistic: under $H_0$ the statistic $S/\sqrt n$ is $N(0,1)$, under $H_1$ it is $N(\sqrt n,1)$.

Step 7 — the numbers ($\alpha=0.05$ throughout; keys MEAN_n*_*).

  • $n=1$: $\xi'=1.6448536$, $\xi=e^{1.6449-0.5}=3.1419814$, $\beta=\Phi(1.6449-1)=0.7404890$.
  • $n=4$: $\xi'=3.2897073$, $\xi=e^{3.2897-2}=3.6317232$, $\beta=\Phi(1.6449-2)=0.3612400$.
  • $n=9$: $\xi'=4.9345609$, $\xi=1.5442848$, $\beta=\Phi(1.6449-3)=0.0876855$.
  • $n=10$: $\xi'=5.2014839$, $\xi=1.2232165$, $\beta=\Phi(1.6449-3.1623)=0.0645798$.
  • $n=25$: $\xi'=8.2242681$, $\xi=0.0139019$, $\beta=\Phi(1.6449-5)=0.0003966$.

An independent Monte-Carlo run of $400\,000$ replications at $n=10$ returns $\hat\alpha=0.0493725$ and $\hat\beta=0.0649050$ (MEAN_n10_ALPHA_MC, MEAN_n10_BETA_MC), against the exact $0.05$ and $0.0645798$.

Fig. 4.2 — The picture behind every threshold test, drawn for Example 4.1 with $n=4$. One vertical line at $\xi'=3.290$ cuts both densities. The blue right tail of $f_S(s;H_0)$ has area $\alpha=0.050$; the orange left tail of $f_S(s;H_1)$ has area $\beta=0.361$. Sliding the line right shrinks blue and grows orange — that is the tradeoff of §4.5. Widening the gap between the two peaks (more data) shrinks both (L25 slide 3; keys MEAN_n4_XIP, MEAN_n4_BETA).
Interpretation (what buying more data buys). At $\alpha=0.05$ fixed, $\beta$ falls from $0.740$ at $n=1$ to $0.361$ at $n=4$ to $0.088$ at $n=9$ to $0.0004$ at $n=25$. The mechanism is visible in Fig. 4.2: the two densities have means $0$ and $n$ but standard deviation only $\sqrt n$, so their separation in standard deviations is $n/\sqrt n=\sqrt n$, which grows without bound. The general formula, worth memorizing, is $$\beta=\Phi\big(z_{1-\alpha}-\sqrt n\big).$$ Demanding $\beta\le\alpha=0.05$ needs $z_{0.95}-\sqrt n\le-z_{0.95}$, i.e. $n\ge(2z_{0.95})^2=10.822174$ (MEAN_NMIN_EXACT), so $n=11$, which delivers $\beta=0.0472847$ (MEAN_NMIN_BETA05, MEAN_NMIN_BETA05_BETA). This is the same $(z_{1-\alpha}+z_{1-\beta})^2$ sample-size formula that governs confidence-interval width in §1 — the two are the same CLT calculation wearing different hats.
Gotcha (the threshold on $\xi$ moves the "wrong" way with $n$). Read the $\xi$ column above: $3.142,\ 3.632,\ 1.544,\ 1.223,\ 0.0139$. It rises then falls below 1. Nothing is broken — $\xi$ and $\xi'$ live on different scales, and $\xi=e^{\xi'-n/2}$ has the term $-n/2$ growing linearly while $\xi'=\sqrt n\,z$ grows only like $\sqrt n$. Always calibrate on the scale where you know the distribution (here $\xi'$, since $\sum_i X_i$ is normal) and convert afterwards if you want $\xi$ at all. You usually do not.
Practice 4.4. Same setup as Example 4.1 with $n=4$, but at the $1\%$ level. Find $\xi'$, the equivalent threshold on the sample mean $M_4$, and $\beta$.
Solution

The derivation is unchanged up to Step 5; only the quantile changes. $z_{0.99}=2.3263479$ (Z_0990), so $$\xi'=\sqrt4\,z_{0.99}=2(2.3263479)=4.6526957$$ (P42_XIP). Since $M_4=\frac14\sum_i X_i$, the equivalent rule is $M_4>4.6526957/4=1.1631739$ (P42_XBAR).

Then $\beta=\Phi(z_{0.99}-\sqrt4)=\Phi(2.3263479-2)=\Phi(0.3263479)=0.6279194$ (P42_BETA).

Compare with $\alpha=0.05$ at the same $n=4$, where $\beta=0.3612400$. Tightening $\alpha$ from $0.05$ to $0.01$ nearly doubled $\beta$. That is the tradeoff of §4.5 in one line.

Practice 4.5. In Example 4.1, how large must $n$ be, at $\alpha=0.05$, to guarantee $\beta\le 0.01$? Verify with the exact formula.
Solution

Require $\Phi(z_{0.95}-\sqrt n)\le 0.01$. Since $\Phi$ is increasing and $\Phi(-z_{0.99})=0.01$, this is $z_{0.95}-\sqrt n\le -z_{0.99}$, i.e. $\sqrt n\ge z_{0.95}+z_{0.99}=1.6448536+2.3263479=3.9712015$, i.e. $n\ge 3.9712015^2=15.770$. So $n=16$.

Check at $n=16$: $\beta=\Phi(1.6448536-4)=\Phi(-2.3551464)=0.0092577$ (MEAN_n16_BETA), indeed below $0.01$; and $\xi'=\sqrt{16}\,z_{0.95}=6.5794145$ (MEAN_n16_XIP). At $n=15$ one would get $\sqrt{15}=3.873<3.971$, so $\beta$ would exceed $0.01$.

4.4 Worked example: the test on a normal variance (L25 slide 4)

Example 4.2 (test on a normal variance; L25 slide 4, filling both of the slide's gaps). $n$ i.i.d. observations with $$H_0:\ X_i\sim N(0,1),\qquad H_1:\ X_i\sim N(0,4).$$ Same mean, different spread. Derive the LRT, reduce it to a threshold on $\sum_i X_i^2$, justify the chi-square distribution the slide asserts, and produce numbers.

Step 1 — likelihoods. The $N(0,\sigma^2)$ density is $\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$. Under $H_1$, $\sigma^2=4$ so $\sigma=2$ — this is what the slide's red "$2$" in $1/(2\sqrt{2\pi})$ and red "$4$" in $2\cdot 4$ are flagging: $$f_X(x;H_1)=\Big(\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\Big)^{n}\exp\Big\{-\frac{\sum_i x_i^2}{2\cdot 4}\Big\}, \qquad f_X(x;H_0)=\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\Big)^{n}\exp\Big\{-\frac{\sum_i x_i^2}{2}\Big\}.$$

Step 2 — the ratio. The constants no longer cancel; their ratio is $\big(\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\big)^n\big/\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\big)^n=2^{-n}$. The exponentials give $e^{a}/e^{b}=e^{a-b}$ with $a=-\tfrac18\sum_i x_i^2$ and $b=-\tfrac12\sum_i x_i^2$, so $$L(x)=2^{-n}\exp\Big\{\Big(\tfrac12-\tfrac18\Big)\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\Big\} =2^{-n}\exp\Big\{\tfrac38\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\Big\},$$ using $\tfrac12-\tfrac18=\tfrac{4-1}{8}=\tfrac38$ (VAR_n10_38).

Step 3 — logarithms. $\log L(x)=-n\log 2+\tfrac38\sum_i x_i^2$, with $\log 2=0.6931472$ (VAR_n10_LOG2). So $L(x)>\xi$ becomes $$\tfrac38\sum_i x_i^2>\log\xi+n\log 2 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2>\tfrac83\big(\log\xi+n\log 2\big)=:\xi' .$$ The coefficient $3/8$ is positive, so multiplying by $8/3$ does not flip the inequality. Again the data enter through a single scalar — this time the sum of squares, because the hypotheses differ in spread, not location.

Step 4 — the distribution of $\sum_i X_i^2$ (the "derived distribution problem"). The slide asserts "chi-square"; here is why. Let $Z\sim N(0,1)$ and $Y=Z^2$. By the CDF method of G3 §4, for $y>0$, $$F_Y(y)=\mathbf{P}(Z^2\le y)=\mathbf{P}(-\sqrt y\le Z\le\sqrt y)=2\Phi(\sqrt y)-1,$$ using symmetry of the standard normal. Differentiating and applying the chain rule ($\frac{d}{dy}\sqrt y=\frac{1}{2\sqrt y}$), $$f_Y(y)=2\varphi(\sqrt y)\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt y}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi y}}e^{-y/2},\qquad y>0,$$ which is the Gamma density with shape $1/2$ and rate $1/2$. Sums of independent Gammas with a common rate add their shapes (convolution, G3 §4), so $\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i^2$ is Gamma with shape $n/2$ and rate $1/2$ — which is precisely the definition of the chi-square distribution with $n$ degrees of freedom, written $\chi^2_n$. Its mean is $n$ and its variance $2n$.

Under $H_0$ the $X_i$ are standard normal, so $\sum_i X_i^2\sim\chi^2_n$ exactly and $$\alpha=\mathbf{P}\big(\chi^2_n>\xi';H_0\big)\ \Longrightarrow\ \xi'=\text{the }(1-\alpha)\text{ quantile of }\chi^2_n .$$ Under $H_1$ we can write $X_i=2Z_i$ with $Z_i\sim N(0,1)$, so $\sum_i X_i^2=4\sum_i Z_i^2\sim 4\chi^2_n$, and $$\beta=\mathbf{P}\Big(4\chi^2_n\le\xi'\Big)=\mathbf{P}\Big(\chi^2_n\le\frac{\xi'}{4}\Big).$$

Step 5 — numbers at $\alpha=0.05$ (keys VAR_n*_*):

  • $n=1$: $\xi'=3.8414588$, $\xi=2.1115027$, $\beta=\mathbf{P}(\chi^2_1\le 0.9603647)=0.6729050$.
  • $n=5$: $\xi'=11.0704977$, $\xi=1.9851624$, $\beta=0.2642423$.
  • $n=10$: $\xi'=18.3070381$, $\xi=0.9358196$, $\beta=\mathbf{P}(\chi^2_{10}\le 4.5767595)=0.0823981$.
  • $n=25$: $\xi'=37.6524841$, $\xi=0.0403971$, $\beta=0.0020439$.

Monte Carlo at $n=10$ with $400\,000$ replications: $\hat\alpha=0.0502200$, $\hat\beta=0.0822025$ (VAR_n10_ALPHA_MC, VAR_n10_BETA_MC) against the exact $0.05$ and $0.0823981$. The $n=10$ entry is the promised example of a legitimate test with $\xi=0.9358196<1$.

Gotcha (which tail?). The reduction gave $\sum_i X_i^2>\xi'$ — an upper tail — only because the coefficient $\tfrac12-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma_1^2}$ came out positive, which happens exactly when $\sigma_1^2>1$. If instead $H_1$ said $X_i\sim N(0,1/4)$, the coefficient would be $\tfrac12-2=-\tfrac32<0$, dividing by it would flip the inequality, and the rejection region would be a lower tail $\{\sum_i x_i^2<\xi'\}$. Never assume the rejection region is on the right; let the algebra tell you.
Practice 4.6. With $n=5$ and $\alpha=0.05$ in Example 4.2, someone suggests the "natural-looking" region $R=\{x:\sum_i x_i^2>12\}$ instead of the calibrated $\xi'=11.0704977$. Is the resulting test valid at the $5\%$ level, and what has it cost?
Solution

Validity first: $12>11.0704977$, and $\mathbf{P}(\chi^2_5>t)$ is decreasing in $t$, so the new region has $\alpha<0.05$. Exactly, $\alpha_{\text{new}}=\mathbf{P}(\chi^2_5>12)=0.0347878$ (P44_ALPHA_12). It is a valid (conservative) $5\%$-level test — it simply spends only $3.48\%$ of the $5\%$ it was allowed.

Cost: raising the threshold shrinks $R$, so $\beta$ rises. Exactly, $\beta_{\text{new}}=\mathbf{P}(\chi^2_5\le 12/4)=\mathbf{P}(\chi^2_5\le 3)=0.3000142$ (P44_BETA_12), against $\beta_{\text{old}}=\mathbf{P}(\chi^2_5\le 2.7676244)=0.2642423$. Since $3>2.7676$, the new $\beta$ is strictly larger — $0.300$ versus $0.264$, a $13.5\%$ relative increase in the missed detection rate, bought with $1.5$ percentage points of unused significance level.

Moral: any threshold above the calibrated one is "safe" but wasteful — it throws away detection power in exchange for a false rejection probability you never asked for. This is the discrete-case compromise of §4.5 arising in continuous clothing.

4.5 The tradeoff, and what changes when $L(X)$ is discrete

Property (the $\alpha$–$\beta$ tradeoff; B&T §9.3 and its Figure 9.9). As $\xi$ increases, the rejection region $R=\{x:L(x)>\xi\}$ shrinks (it is a nested family: $\xi_1<\xi_2\Rightarrow R_{\xi_2}\subseteq R_{\xi_1}$). Consequently $$\alpha(\xi)=\mathbf{P}(L(X)>\xi;H_0)\ \text{is non-increasing in }\xi,\qquad \beta(\xi)=\mathbf{P}(L(X)\le\xi;H_1)\ \text{is non-decreasing in }\xi.$$ Both statements are just monotonicity of probability on nested sets. There is therefore no "best" $\xi$ without an external criterion; $\xi=0$ gives $\alpha=1,\beta=0$ and $\xi=\infty$ gives $\alpha=0,\beta=1$.
Fig. 4.3 — Moving $\xi$ trades one error against the other. Left, Example 4.1 with $n=4$: $L(X)$ is a continuous random variable, $\alpha(\xi)$ sweeps continuously from $1$ to $0$, and every target level is exactly attainable — the marked point is $\xi=3.632$, $\alpha=0.050$, $\beta=0.361$. Right, B&T Example 9.10 with one die roll: $L(X)$ takes only two values, so $\alpha$ can only be $0$, $1/3$ or $1$ and a $5\%$ test does not exist (B&T §9.3 and its Figure 9.9).
Example 4.3 (the loaded die; B&T Example 9.10). One roll of a six-sided die, with $$H_0:\ p_X(x;H_0)=\tfrac16,\ x=1,\dots,6;\qquad H_1:\ p_X(x;H_1)=\begin{cases}\tfrac14,&x=1,2\\[2pt]\tfrac18,&x=3,4,5,6.\end{cases}$$ Enumerate every LRT and its error pair.

First check $H_1$ is a legitimate PMF: $2\cdot\tfrac14+4\cdot\tfrac18=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1$ (DIE10_H1_CHECK). The likelihood ratio takes just two values: $$L(x)=\frac{1/4}{1/6}=\frac{6}{4}=\frac32=1.5\ \ (x=1,2),\qquad L(x)=\frac{1/8}{1/6}=\frac68=\frac34=0.75\ \ (x=3,4,5,6),$$ keys DIE10_L_HI, DIE10_L_LO. Because $L$ has only two values, the condition $L(x)>\xi$ produces only three distinct regions:

  • $\xi<3/4$: every $x$ satisfies $L(x)>\xi$, so $R=\{1,\dots,6\}$ — always reject. $\alpha=1$, $\beta=0$.
  • $3/4<\xi<3/2$: only $x\in\{1,2\}$ qualifies, $R=\{1,2\}$. Then $\alpha=\mathbf{P}(X\in\{1,2\};H_0)=\tfrac16+\tfrac16=\tfrac13=0.3333333$ and $\beta=\mathbf{P}(X\in\{3,4,5,6\};H_1)=4\cdot\tfrac18=\tfrac12=0.5$ (DIE10_ALPHA_MID, DIE10_BETA_MID).
  • $\xi>3/2$: no $x$ qualifies, $R=\varnothing$ — never reject. $\alpha=0$, $\beta=1$.

Only the middle regime is a test at all; the other two ignore the data entirely. And the attainable significance levels are $\{0,\ 1/3,\ 1\}$ — the customary $\alpha=0.05$ is unreachable.

Recipe (what to do when $\alpha$ is unattainable; B&T §9.3, p. 491). When $L(X)$ is discrete, $\mathbf{P}(L(X)>\xi;H_0)$ jumps and may skip your target $\alpha$. Three options, in order of practical usefulness:
  1. Be conservative. Take the smallest $\xi$ with $\mathbf{P}(L(X)>\xi;H_0)\le\alpha$. You get a valid level-$\alpha$ test whose true false rejection probability is below target. This is the standard choice and the one used below.
  2. Settle for approximate equality, accepting an $\alpha$ slightly above target if that is much closer.
  3. Randomize: flip an auxiliary coin to choose between the two neighbouring thresholds, hitting $\alpha$ exactly. Theoretically tidy, essentially never used, since it means two statisticians with the same data can reach different conclusions.
Example 4.4 (the same die, four rolls — the discrete tradeoff with room to move). Roll the die of Example 4.3 four times independently. Find the LRT family and tabulate $(\alpha,\beta)$.

Let $K$ be the number of rolls landing in $\{1,2\}$. Because the rolls are independent, the joint likelihood ratio is the product of the per-roll ratios, and each roll contributes $3/2$ if it lands in $\{1,2\}$ and $3/4$ otherwise: $$L(x_1,\dots,x_4)=\Big(\tfrac32\Big)^{K}\Big(\tfrac34\Big)^{4-K} =\Big(\tfrac34\Big)^{4}\Big(\tfrac{3/2}{3/4}\Big)^{K}=\frac{81}{256}\,2^{K}.$$ This is strictly increasing in $K$, so "$L>\xi$" is equivalent to "$K\ge k$" for a suitable integer $k$: the four-dimensional data have been reduced to one integer. The five values are $L=0.3164063,\,0.6328125,\,1.2656250,\,2.5312500,\,5.0625000$ for $K=0,\dots,4$ (DIE10N4_L_k0…DIE10N4_L_k4).

Under $H_0$ a roll lands in $\{1,2\}$ with probability $2/6=1/3$, so $K\sim\text{Bin}(4,1/3)$; under $H_1$ with probability $2\cdot\tfrac14=1/2$, so $K\sim\text{Bin}(4,1/2)$ (DIE10N4_P0, DIE10N4_P1). Hence, with $\alpha_k=\mathbf{P}(K\ge k;H_0)$ and $\beta_k=\mathbf{P}(K<k;H_1)$:

  • $k=0$ (always reject): $\alpha=1$, $\beta=0$.
  • $k=1$: $\alpha=0.8024691$, $\beta=0.0625000$.
  • $k=2$: $\alpha=0.4074074$, $\beta=0.3125000$.
  • $k=3$: $\alpha=0.1111111$, $\beta=0.6875000$.
  • $k=4$: $\alpha=0.0123457=(1/3)^4$, $\beta=0.9375000=1-(1/2)^4$.

(Keys DIE10N4_ALPHA_K*, DIE10N4_BETA_K*; the $k=4$ entries are confirmed in exact rational arithmetic as DIE10N4_ALPHA_K4_EXACT and DIE10N4_BETA_K4_EXACT.) At a target $\alpha=0.05$, Recipe option 1 selects $k=4$: reject only if all four rolls land in $\{1,2\}$, giving true $\alpha=0.0123457$ and a brutal $\beta=0.9375$. Four rolls are simply not enough evidence.

Example 4.5 (a discrete LRT with a real answer; B&T Example 9.14). $n=25$ independent tosses of a coin; $X$ is the number of heads. Under $H_0$ the probability of heads is $\theta_0=1/2$; under $H_1$ it is $\theta_1=2/3$. Set the false rejection probability to $0.1$. What is the rejection region?

Step 1 — the ratio. Both PMFs are binomial with the same $\binom{25}{k}$, which cancels: $$L(k)=\frac{\binom{n}{k}\theta_1^{k}(1-\theta_1)^{n-k}}{\binom{n}{k}\theta_0^{k}(1-\theta_0)^{n-k}} =\Big(\frac{\theta_1}{\theta_0}\Big)^{k}\Big(\frac{1-\theta_1}{1-\theta_0}\Big)^{n-k} =\Big(\frac{4}{3}\Big)^{k}\Big(\frac{2}{3}\Big)^{25-k}.$$ Pull out $(2/3)^{25}$ and note $\tfrac43\cdot\tfrac32=2$: $$L(k)=\Big(\tfrac23\Big)^{25}\Big(\tfrac43\cdot\tfrac32\Big)^{k}=2^{k}\Big(\tfrac23\Big)^{25},$$ exactly the book's form. Since $2^k$ increases in $k$, $L(k)>\xi$ is equivalent to $k>\gamma$ for a suitable $\gamma$: reject $H_0$ if $X>\gamma$.

Step 2 — calibrate exactly. We need the smallest integer $\gamma$ with $\mathbf{P}(X>\gamma;H_0)\le 0.1$, where $X\sim\text{Bin}(25,1/2)$. The tail probabilities (E914_TAIL_g*) are $$\mathbf{P}(X>14)=0.2121781,\quad \mathbf{P}(X>15)=0.1147615,\quad \mathbf{P}(X>16)=0.0538761,\quad \mathbf{P}(X>17)=0.0216426.$$ The first value at or below $0.1$ is at $\gamma=16$, matching the book. The achieved level is $\alpha=0.0538761$, comfortably under target, and the corresponding critical likelihood ratio is $L(16)=2^{16}(2/3)^{25}=2.5953651$ (E914_LR_AT_K). The resulting $\beta=\mathbf{P}(X\le 16;H_1)=0.4624211$ with $X\sim\text{Bin}(25,2/3)$ (E914_BETA_AT16).

Step 3 — the CLT shortcut, and its trap. Under $H_0$, $\mathbb{E}[X]=25(0.5)=12.5$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)=25(0.5)(0.5)=6.25$, so the standard deviation is $2.5$ (E914_MEAN_H0, E914_SD_H0). The CLT (G6 §2) gives $$0.1=\mathbf{P}(X>\gamma;H_0)\approx\mathbf{P}\Big(Z>\frac{\gamma-12.5}{2.5}\Big) \Longrightarrow \frac{\gamma-12.5}{2.5}=z_{0.90}=1.2815516 \Longrightarrow \gamma=12.5+2.5(1.2815516)=15.7038789,$$ (E914_GAMMA_CLT), the book's $15.7$. Rounding "reject if $X>15.7$" to the integer grid gives "reject if $X>15$" — but that region has exact level $\mathbf{P}(X>15;H_0)=0.1147615>0.1$ (E914_ALPHA_AT15). The approximation overshot the constraint.

Gotcha (never round a CLT critical value toward a bigger region). The exact answer is $\gamma=16$; the CLT answer $15.7$ rounded down to $15$ inflates $\alpha$ from $0.054$ to $0.115$ — more than double the target. When the statistic is integer-valued, round the critical value in the conservative direction (up, for a right-tail region), or better, evaluate the exact tail as in Step 2. The payoff for the sloppier choice is real ($\beta$ falls from $0.4624$ to $0.3044$, E914_BETA_AT15) but it is bought with a level you did not agree to.
Practice 4.7. A single observation $X$ is Poisson: $H_0:\lambda=2$ versus $H_1:\lambda=5$. Show the LRT rejects for large $X$, then find the level-$0.05$ conservative rejection region and its $\beta$.
Solution

Ratio. $L(k)=\dfrac{e^{-5}5^k/k!}{e^{-2}2^k/k!}=e^{-3}\Big(\dfrac52\Big)^{k}$. The factorials cancel. Consecutive ratios satisfy $L(k+1)/L(k)=5/2=2.5>1$ (P43_LR_RATIO), so $L$ is strictly increasing in $k$ and $L(k)>\xi$ is equivalent to $k>\gamma$.

Calibrate. With $X\sim\text{Poisson}(2)$: $\mathbf{P}(X>4)=0.0526530$ (P43_TAIL_k4) and $\mathbf{P}(X>5)=0.0165636$ (P43_TAIL_k5). The first tail at or below $0.05$ is at $\gamma=5$, so reject $H_0$ iff $X\ge 6$, with achieved $\alpha=0.0165636$ (P43_GAMMA, P43_ALPHA).

The other error. $\beta=\mathbf{P}(X\le 5;H_1)$ with $X\sim\text{Poisson}(5)$, which is $0.6159607$ (P43_BETA).

Note how much level is wasted: we wanted $0.05$ and got $0.0166$, because the Poisson tail jumps by a factor of three between $k=4$ and $k=5$. That waste is paid for in $\beta$. One observation again buys very little.

4.6 Neyman–Pearson optimality, and the ROC picture

So far the LRT is only "the shape a Bayesian rule would have". The following lemma says it is the best possible shape, full stop.

Neyman–Pearson Lemma (B&T §9.3, p. 491). Fix a critical value $\xi$ and let the LRT $R_\xi=\{x:L(x)>\xi\}$ have error probabilities $$\alpha=\mathbf{P}(L(X)>\xi;H_0),\qquad \beta=\mathbf{P}(L(X)\le\xi;H_1).$$ Let $R$ be any other rejection region whatsoever with $\mathbf{P}(X\in R;H_0)\le\alpha$. Then $$\mathbf{P}(X\notin R;H_1)\ \ge\ \beta,$$ with strict inequality whenever $\mathbf{P}(X\in R;H_0)<\alpha$. In words: among all tests whose false rejection probability is at most $\alpha$, the LRT minimizes the false acceptance probability.

Justification (B&T's argument, every step). Fabricate a Bayesian problem whose MAP rule is exactly this LRT. Choose prior probabilities in the ratio $$\frac{p_\Theta(\theta_0)}{p_\Theta(\theta_1)}=\xi, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad p_\Theta(\theta_0)=\frac{\xi}{1+\xi},\quad p_\Theta(\theta_1)=\frac{1}{1+\xi},$$ which is a legitimate prior since $\xi>0$ makes both entries positive and they sum to $\frac{\xi+1}{1+\xi}=1$. By the Bayesian rehearsal of §4.2, the MAP rule for this prior declares $H_1$ exactly when $L(x)>\xi$ — it is $R_\xi$.

Now, in this fabricated Bayesian world, any decision rule with rejection region $R$ has overall error probability $$\mathbf{P}(\text{error})=p_\Theta(\theta_0)\,\mathbf{P}(X\in R;H_0) +p_\Theta(\theta_1)\,\mathbf{P}(X\notin R;H_1) =\frac{\xi}{1+\xi}\,\alpha(R)+\frac{1}{1+\xi}\,\beta(R),$$ by the total probability theorem (G1 §2) splitting on which hypothesis is true. G6 §3 says MAP minimizes this quantity over all rules. Applying that to $R_\xi$ versus an arbitrary $R$: $$\frac{\xi}{1+\xi}\alpha+\frac{1}{1+\xi}\beta\ \le\ \frac{\xi}{1+\xi}\alpha(R)+\frac{1}{1+\xi}\beta(R).$$ Multiply through by $1+\xi>0$ and rearrange: $$\beta-\beta(R)\ \le\ \xi\big(\alpha(R)-\alpha\big).$$ If the competitor satisfies $\alpha(R)\le\alpha$, the right side is $\le 0$, so $\beta\le\beta(R)$ — the claim. If moreover $\alpha(R)<\alpha$, the right side is strictly negative and $\beta<\beta(R)$. $\blacksquare$

Interpretation (what the lemma does and does not promise). It is a statement about one $\alpha$ at a time: for the level you chose, no region beats the LRT. It says nothing about which $\alpha$ to choose — that remains a judgement call outside the mathematics. It also requires both hypotheses to be simple; the moment $H_1$ becomes composite (§4.7) there is generally no single test that is best against every alternative simultaneously, which is precisely why significance testing is more art than theorem.
Definition (ROC curve). Plotting the attainable pairs $\big(\alpha(\xi),\,1-\beta(\xi)\big)$ as $\xi$ sweeps from $\infty$ down to $0$ traces the receiver operating characteristic of the LRT family. Neyman–Pearson says the ROC is an upper envelope: no test of any kind has an $(\alpha,\text{power})$ point strictly above it. For the normal-shift family of Example 4.1, with separation $d$ (equal to $\sqrt n$ there), the curve has the closed form $$\text{power}(\alpha)=1-\Phi\big(z_{1-\alpha}-d\big),$$ and the area under it works out to $\Phi(d/\sqrt2)$ — e.g. $0.9873263$ at $d=\sqrt{10}$, confirmed by numerical quadrature as $0.9873263$ (ROC_AUC_FORM_d_sqrt10, ROC_AUC_QUAD_d_sqrt10).

The dots are the $\alpha=0.05$ operating points, with powers $0.126,\,0.260,\,0.639,\,0.935$ for $d=0.5,1,2,\sqrt{10}$ (ROC_d*_POW05). Example 4.6 (the LRT versus a plausible competitor; B&T Example 9.13). Observe two i.i.d. normals $X_1,X_2$ with unit variance. Under $H_0$ their common mean is $0$; under $H_1$ it is $2$. At $\alpha=0.05$, compare the LRT with the "reasonable-looking" test that rejects when $\max\{X_1,X_2\}$ is large.

The LRT. By the computation of Example 4.1 with $n=2$ and mean shift $2$: $$L(x)=\frac{\exp\{-[(x_1-2)^2+(x_2-2)^2]/2\}}{\exp\{-[x_1^2+x_2^2]/2\}} =\exp\{2(x_1+x_2)-4\},$$ since $(x_i-2)^2-x_i^2=-4x_i+4$ and $-\tfrac12\sum_i(-4x_i+4)=2(x_1+x_2)-4$. So the test is $x_1+x_2>\gamma$ with $\gamma=(4+\log\xi)/2$. Under $H_0$, $X_1+X_2\sim N(0,2)$, so $$\gamma=\sqrt2\,z_{0.95}=\sqrt2(1.6448536)=2.3261743$$ (E913_GAMMA), the book's $2.33$. Under $H_1$, $X_1+X_2\sim N(4,2)$, so $$\beta=\mathbf{P}(X_1+X_2\le\gamma;H_1)=\Phi\Big(\frac{2.3261743-4}{\sqrt2}\Big) =\Phi(-1.1835735)=0.1182910,$$ the book's $0.12$ (E913_BETA_LRT, E913_BETA_LRT_ARG).

The competitor. Take $R'=\{\max\{x_1,x_2\}>\zeta\}$. Under $H_0$, using independence and the fact that a maximum is at most $\zeta$ iff both coordinates are, $$0.05=1-\mathbf{P}(X_1\le\zeta;H_0)\mathbf{P}(X_2\le\zeta;H_0)=1-\Phi(\zeta)^2 \Longrightarrow \Phi(\zeta)=\sqrt{0.95}=0.9746794 \Longrightarrow \zeta=1.9545083$$ (E913_SQRT095, E913_ZETA). Under $H_1$ each $X_i-2$ is standard normal, so $$\beta'=\mathbf{P}(\max\le\zeta;H_1)=\big(\Phi(\zeta-2)\big)^2=\big(\Phi(-0.0454917)\big)^2 =(0.4818577)^2=0.2321868$$ (E913_BETA_MAX, E913_BETA_MAX_INNER).

Verdict. At the same $\alpha=0.05$, the LRT misses a true $H_1$ $11.8\%$ of the time; the max-test misses it $23.2\%$ of the time — nearly twice as often. Monte Carlo over $400\,000$ replications gives $\hat\alpha=0.0500225$, $\hat\beta=0.1183825$ for the LRT and $\hat\alpha=0.0501525$, $\hat\beta=0.2316850$ for the max-test (E913_*_MC), confirming both. The max-test wastes evidence: it throws away the smaller observation entirely, whereas the sum uses both.

Carrying full precision, $\zeta=1.9545083$ and $\beta'=0.2321868$; even with the book's $\zeta=1.96$ the exact value is $\Phi(-0.04)^2=0.2343011$ (E913_BOOK_BETA_MAX_ROUND). The book's $0.24$ comes from rounding $\Phi(-0.04)$ to $0.49$ in the tables. This is table-rounding, not a disagreement: the conclusion ($0.12$ versus roughly $0.23$–$0.24$) is unaffected.

Practice 4.8. In Example 4.1 with $n=10$ at $\alpha=0.05$, a colleague insists on the two-sided region $\{|\sum_i x_i|>c\}$, "to be safe against a mean shift in either direction". Calibrate $c$ and compute the resulting $\beta$. Compare with the LRT.
Solution

Calibrate. Under $H_0$, $S=\sum_i X_i\sim N(0,10)$, so $\mathbf{P}(|S|>c;H_0)=2\big(1-\Phi(c/\sqrt{10})\big)=0.05$ gives $\Phi(c/\sqrt{10})=0.975$, hence $c=\sqrt{10}\,z_{0.975}=\sqrt{10}(1.9599640)=6.1979503$ (MEAN_n10_TWOSIDED_C).

The other error. Under $H_1$, $S\sim N(10,10)$, so $$\beta=\mathbf{P}(-c\le S\le c;H_1) =\Phi\Big(\frac{6.1979503-10}{\sqrt{10}}\Big)-\Phi\Big(\frac{-6.1979503-10}{\sqrt{10}}\Big) =0.1146209$$ (MEAN_n10_TWOSIDED_BETA).

Neyman–Pearson predicted this before any arithmetic: the region $\{|S|>c\}$ is not of the form $\{L(X)>\xi\}$, because $L$ is increasing in $S$ and therefore its super-level sets are right rays, never unions of two rays.

Moral. "Being safe in both directions" is only a virtue when $H_1$ actually lives in both directions. Here $H_1$ says the mean is $+1$, period; guarding against $-1$ spends $2.5\%$ of the level on an alternative that is not on the table.

4.7 Composite hypotheses and significance testing (L25 slide 5)

Real questions are rarely two-sided coin flips between fully specified models. "Is my coin fair?" pits $H_0:\theta=1/2$ against $H_1:\theta\neq 1/2$ — and $H_1$ is composite: it is a whole family of distributions, one for each $\theta\neq 1/2$, including $\theta=0.4999$, which no finite experiment can separate from $1/2$. There is no likelihood ratio to compute, because there is no single $p_X(x;H_1)$. The methodology shifts.

Recipe (significance testing; L25 slide 5, B&T §9.4). Before the data arrive:
  1. Choose a statistic $S=h(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ — one scalar summarizing the data in the direction where $H_0$ would fail.
  2. Choose the shape of the rejection region as a function of an undetermined critical value $\xi$: e.g. $|S-\mathbb{E}[S;H_0]|>\xi$ (two-sided) or $S>\xi$ (one-sided).
  3. Choose the significance level $\alpha$ (conventionally $0.10$, $0.05$, $0.01$).
  4. Solve for $\xi$ from $\mathbf{P}(\text{reject }H_0;H_0)=\alpha$. This is the only calculation, and it needs the distribution of $S$ under $H_0$ only — which is why $H_0$ must be simple, and why the CLT is so useful here.
Then observe the data, compute $s=h(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, and reject iff $s$ falls in the region. Notice what is missing: $\beta$ is never computed, because $H_1$ names no distribution to compute it under.
Example 4.7 (Is my coin fair? L25 slide 5, B&T Example 9.15 — filling the slide's CLT gap). A coin is tossed $n=1000$ times independently and $S=472$ heads are observed. Test $H_0:\theta=1/2$ against $H_1:\theta\neq 1/2$ at the $5\%$ level.

Steps 1–3. The statistic is $S=X_1+\dots+X_n$, the number of heads ($X_i=1$ for heads); the region shape is $|S-n/2|>\xi$, symmetric because under $H_0$ the distribution of $S$ is symmetric about $n/2$ and deviations in either direction are equally damning; the level is $\alpha=0.05$.

Step 4 — solve for $\xi$. Under $H_0$, $S\sim\text{Bin}(1000,1/2)$, so $$\mathbb{E}[S;H_0]=n\theta=1000(0.5)=500,\qquad \operatorname{var}(S;H_0)=n\theta(1-\theta)=1000(0.5)(0.5)=250,$$ giving standard deviation $\sqrt{250}=15.8113883$ (COIN_MEAN, COIN_SD). By the CLT (G6 §2), $(S-500)/15.8113883$ is approximately standard normal, so $$0.95=\mathbf{P}(|S-500|\le\xi;H_0)\approx \mathbf{P}\Big(|Z|\le\frac{\xi}{15.8113883}\Big)=2\Phi\Big(\frac{\xi}{15.8113883}\Big)-1 .$$ Hence $\Phi(\xi/15.8113883)=0.975$, so $\xi/15.8113883=z_{0.975}=1.9599640$ and $$\xi=1.9599640\times 15.8113883=30.9897516\ \approx\ 31$$ (COIN_196SD) — the slide's $\xi=31$, now derived rather than asserted. The rejection region is therefore $\{|s-500|>31\}$, and because $S$ is integer-valued that is $$\{s\le 468\}\cup\{s\ge 532\}\qquad\text{i.e. reject iff } |s-500|\ge 32 .$$ Note which way the rounding went. Sliding the boundary one step outward gives $\{s\le 469\}\cup\{s\ge 531\}$ — a larger region, which is the $\xi=30$ test of the next paragraph and busts the $5\%$ budget. The Gotcha of §4.5 (never round a CLT critical value toward a bigger region) applies verbatim.

Sanity check against the exact binomial. Summing the binomial PMF exactly, $\mathbf{P}(|S-500|\le 31;H_0)=0.9537088$, so the true level of the $\xi=31$ test is $\alpha=0.0462912$ (COIN_EXACT_COVER_31, COIN_EXACT_ALPHA_31) — slightly conservative, as integrality forces. Using $\xi=30$ instead would give coverage $0.9463222$, i.e. $\alpha=0.0536778$, overshooting. So $31$ is the right integer.

The decision. $|s-500|=|472-500|=28$, and $28\le 31$, so $H_0$ is not rejected at the $5\%$ level (COIN_OBS_DEV).

Definition (p-value; B&T §9.4). The p-value is the smallest significance level at which the observed data would have led to rejection — equivalently, the probability, computed under $H_0$, of a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. For the coin data, "at least as extreme" means $|S-500|\ge 28$, and $$p=2\,\mathbf{P}(S\le 472;H_0)=0.0819387$$ exactly (COIN_PVALUE_EXACT); the CLT with continuity correction gives $0.0819903$ (COIN_PVALUE_CC) and without it $0.0765814$ (COIN_PVALUE_CLT). Since $0.082>0.05$, $H_0$ survives at the $5\%$ level — but it would not survive at the $10\%$ level.
Gotcha (three things the p-value is not). It is not the probability that $H_0$ is true (again: $H_0$ has no probability). It is not the probability that the result is a fluke. And it is not a measure of effect size: with $n=100\,000$ tosses, a coin with $\theta=0.502$ would produce p-values below $0.05$ routinely, while being, for any practical purpose, fair. Significance and importance are different questions.
Interpretation (why the test failed to reject, and whether that is reassuring). Compute the power of this test against specific alternatives — $\mathbf{P}(\text{reject};\theta)$ with the exact binomial (COIN_POWER_*): $$\theta=0.52:\ 0.2339654,\qquad \theta=0.55:\ 0.8801172,\qquad \theta=0.60:\ 0.9999943 .$$ Against a genuinely, badly biased coin the test is nearly certain to fire. But against a mildly biased one ($\theta=0.52$) it fires less than a quarter of the time. So "not rejected" here means "the coin is not grossly biased", not "the coin is fair" — exactly the reading B&T insist on when they refuse to write "accepted".
Practice 4.9. Repeat the coin test at the $1\%$ level. What is the critical value, and does the conclusion change? Then explain in one sentence why the $1\%$ test is the more "conservative" one, and about what.
Solution

Only the quantile changes: $z_{0.995}=2.5758293$, so $\xi=2.5758293\times 15.8113883=40.7301685$, i.e. reject iff $|s-500|>41$ (rounding up to stay at or below the $1\%$ level). The observed $|472-500|=28$ is far inside, so again not rejected — as it must be, since a test at a smaller $\alpha$ has a smaller rejection region, so anything not rejected at $5\%$ is not rejected at $1\%$ either.

The $1\%$ test is conservative about false rejection specifically: it protects $H_0$ harder. It is correspondingly worse at detecting a biased coin — its $\beta$ against every alternative is larger. "Conservative" always names a direction; here it is the null's direction.

Practice 4.10. How many tosses would you need for the $5\%$ two-sided test to detect a coin with $\theta=0.52$ with probability $0.80$?
Solution

The rejection region at sample size $n$ is $|S-n/2|>z_{0.975}\sqrt{n/4}$. Under $\theta$, $S$ is approximately $N\big(n\theta,\,n\theta(1-\theta)\big)$, so the power is $$1-\Big[\Phi\Big(\tfrac{c-n(\theta-0.5)}{\sqrt{n\theta(1-\theta)}}\Big) -\Phi\Big(\tfrac{-c-n(\theta-0.5)}{\sqrt{n\theta(1-\theta)}}\Big)\Big],\qquad c=z_{0.975}\sqrt{n/4}.$$ Scanning $n$ in steps of $100$ (P46_NSTAR) the requirement is first met at $n=5000$, where the power is $0.8076207$ (P46_POWER_AT_NSTAR). For comparison, the lecture's $n=1000$ has power only $0.2439611$ (P46_POWER_AT_1000) — normal approximation, using the same formula at $n=1000$; the exact binomial value for the integer region $\{s\le 468\}\cup\{s\ge 532\}$ is $0.2339654$, quoted after Example 4.7.

Five times the data for a bias of two percentage points. Detecting small departures is expensive, and the cost grows like the inverse square of the departure — the same $1/\sqrt n$ law that sets confidence-interval widths in §1.

4.8 Goodness of fit: solving the lecture's two open problems (L25 slides 6–7)

Slides 6 and 7 pose two questions and answer neither. Slide 6 sets up "is my die fair?" with a statistic $T$ and a threshold $\xi$ but supplies no data, no $\xi$, and no verdict. Slide 7 poses "do I have the correct pdf?", defines $D_n$ and quotes the single critical value $\mathbf{P}(\sqrt n\,D_n\ge 1.36)\approx 0.05$ — but works no example: no data, no bin choice, no computed $D_n$, no decision, and no derivation of where $1.36$ comes from. We solve both completely.

Where the chi-square statistic comes from

The setup: a random variable takes values in $\{1,\dots,m\}$ with unknown PMF $\theta=(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_m)$. We draw $n$ independent samples and let $N_k$ be the number of times outcome $k$ occurred, so $N_1+\dots+N_m=n$. The hypotheses are $$H_0:\ \theta=\theta^*=(\theta_1^*,\dots,\theta_m^*),\qquad H_1:\ \theta\neq\theta^*,$$ with $H_1$ composite. The device of B&T §9.4 is the generalized likelihood ratio: replace the unavailable $p_X(x;H_1)$ by the largest likelihood achievable under any $\theta$, i.e. the maximum likelihood fit of §2.

Step 1 — ML over the multinomial. The observation $(n_1,\dots,n_m)$ has multinomial PMF $p_X(x;\theta)=c\,\theta_1^{n_1}\cdots\theta_m^{n_m}$, where $c=n!/(n_1!\cdots n_m!)$ does not involve $\theta$. Take logarithms and eliminate $\theta_m=1-\theta_1-\dots-\theta_{m-1}$: $$\log p_X(x;\theta)=\log c+n_1\log\theta_1+\dots+n_{m-1}\log\theta_{m-1} +n_m\log(1-\theta_1-\dots-\theta_{m-1}).$$ Differentiate with respect to $\theta_k$ ($k\le m-1$) and set to zero: $$\frac{n_k}{\theta_k}-\frac{n_m}{1-\theta_1-\dots-\theta_{m-1}}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{n_k}{\theta_k}=\frac{n_m}{\theta_m}.$$ So all the ratios $n_k/\theta_k$ are equal — call the common value $\lambda$. Then $n_k=\lambda\theta_k$ for every $k$; summing over $k$ gives $n=\lambda\cdot 1$, so $\lambda=n$ and $$\hat\theta_k=\frac{n_k}{n},\qquad k=1,\dots,m,$$ the empirical frequencies. (Unsurprising, and reassuring: ML says "believe what you saw".)

Step 2 — the generalized ratio. Compare the best $H_1$-compatible model to $H_0$: $$\frac{p_X(x;\hat\theta)}{p_X(x;\theta^*)} =\frac{c\prod_k(n_k/n)^{n_k}}{c\prod_k(\theta_k^*)^{n_k}} =\prod_{k=1}^m\Big(\frac{n_k}{n\theta_k^*}\Big)^{n_k},$$ and reject $H_0$ when this exceeds $\xi$. Taking logarithms turns the product into the statistic $$S=\sum_{k=1}^m N_k\log\Big(\frac{N_k}{n\theta_k^*}\Big),\qquad \text{reject } H_0 \text{ iff } S>\log\xi .$$ (Convention: $0\log 0=0$.)

Step 3 — the second-order approximation. $S$ has no tabulated distribution, but for large $n$ the observed frequencies are close to $\theta^*$ under $H_0$, so a Taylor expansion is accurate. For $y$ near $y^*>0$, $$y\log\frac{y}{y^*}\approx (y-y^*)+\frac{(y-y^*)^2}{2y^*},$$ (the first-order term of $\log(y/y^*)$ times $y$, expanded to second order). Substituting $y=N_k$, $y^*=n\theta_k^*$ and summing over $k$, the linear terms telescope away because $\sum_k N_k=n=\sum_k n\theta^*_k$, leaving $$S\approx\frac12\sum_{k=1}^m\frac{(N_k-n\theta_k^*)^2}{n\theta_k^*}=\frac{T}{2}, \qquad\text{where}\qquad \boxed{\ T=\sum_{k=1}^m\frac{(N_k-n\theta_k^*)^2}{n\theta_k^*}\ }$$ is the chi-square statistic of L25 slide 6: a sum of (observed $-$ expected)$^2$/expected.

Step 4 — the null distribution and the degrees of freedom (the count the slide omits). Write $W_k=(N_k-n\theta_k^*)/\sqrt{n\theta_k^*}$, so that $T=\sum_k W_k^2$. It is tempting to say "each $W_k$ is standard normal by the CLT, so $T$ is a sum of $m$ squared standard normals, minus one for the constraint $\sum_k N_k=n$" — but that first clause is false, and worth getting right. Marginally $N_k\sim\text{Bin}(n,\theta_k^*)$, so $$\mathbb{E}[N_k;H_0]=n\theta_k^*,\qquad \operatorname{var}(N_k;H_0)=n\theta_k^*(1-\theta_k^*),$$ and the multivariate CLT gives $W_k\to N(0,\,1-\theta_k^*)$, not $N(0,1)$. The correct statement is about the whole vector at once: $W=(W_1,\dots,W_m)$ converges to a multivariate normal with mean zero and covariance matrix $$\Sigma=I-\sqrt{\theta^*}\,\sqrt{\theta^*}^{\!\top},\qquad \sqrt{\theta^*}=\big(\sqrt{\theta_1^*},\dots,\sqrt{\theta_m^*}\big)^{\!\top},$$ whose diagonal entries are indeed $1-\theta_k^*$ and whose off-diagonal entries $-\sqrt{\theta_j^*\theta_k^*}$ encode the constraint. Since $\sqrt{\theta^*}$ is a unit vector ($\sum_k\theta_k^*=1$), $\Sigma$ is the orthogonal projection onto the hyperplane perpendicular to $\sqrt{\theta^*}$ — an idempotent matrix of rank $m-1$. The squared norm of a mean-zero Gaussian with a rank-$r$ projection covariance is exactly $\chi^2_r$ (rotate coordinates: $r$ of them are i.i.d. $N(0,1)$ and the remaining $m-r$ are identically zero). Hence $$T=\|W\|^2\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{approx}\ }\ \chi^2_{m-1}\quad\text{under } H_0 ,$$ and the same holds for $2S$. For a die, $m=6$ and the degrees of freedom are $6-1=5$. The "one constraint" bookkeeping gives the right answer; the projection is the right reason, and it is what makes $\mathbb{E}[T]=m-1$ and $\operatorname{var}(T)=2(m-1)$ come out — for the die, $5$ and $10$, matching the Monte-Carlo check below.

Recipe (the chi-square test; B&T §9.4). (1) Compute the expected counts $n\theta_k^*$ under $H_0$. (2) Compute $T=\sum_k(N_k-n\theta_k^*)^2/(n\theta_k^*)$. (3) Look up the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile $\xi$ of $\chi^2_{m-1}$. (4) Reject $H_0$ iff $T>\xi$; or equivalently report the p-value $\mathbf{P}(\chi^2_{m-1}>T)$. Rule of thumb for the approximation to be trustworthy: every expected count $n\theta_k^*$ should be at least about $5$.
Example 4.8 — SOLVING L25 slide 6's open problem, "Is my die fair?" (data from B&T Example 9.18). A die is rolled $n=600$ times independently, producing counts $$n_1=92,\ n_2=120,\ n_3=88,\ n_4=98,\ n_5=95,\ n_6=107 .$$ Test $H_0:\theta_k=1/6$ for all $k$ at the $5\%$ level.

Step 1 — expected counts. $n\theta_k^*=600\cdot\tfrac16=100$ for every face (DIE_EXPECTED). All six are far above $5$, so the approximation is safe. Note the counts sum to $600$, as they must.

Step 2 — the statistic, term by term (keys DIE_TERM_1…DIE_TERM_6): $$\frac{(92-100)^2}{100}=\frac{64}{100}=0.64,\qquad \frac{(120-100)^2}{100}=\frac{400}{100}=4.00,\qquad \frac{(88-100)^2}{100}=\frac{144}{100}=1.44,$$ $$\frac{(98-100)^2}{100}=\frac{4}{100}=0.04,\qquad \frac{(95-100)^2}{100}=\frac{25}{100}=0.25,\qquad \frac{(107-100)^2}{100}=\frac{49}{100}=0.49 .$$ Summing: $T=0.64+4.00+1.44+0.04+0.25+0.49=6.86$ (DIE_T, cross-checked by scipy.stats.chisquare as DIE_T_SCIPY). The face-$2$ term alone contributes $4.00$, well over half of $T$.

Step 3 — the critical value. With $m-1=5$ degrees of freedom, the $95$th percentile of $\chi^2_5$ is $\xi=11.0704977$ (DIE_CRIT_05), the book's $11.1$.

Step 4 — the verdict. $T=6.86<11.07$, so $H_0$ is not rejected at the $5\%$ level: these counts are entirely ordinary for a fair die. The p-value is $\mathbf{P}(\chi^2_5>6.86)=0.2312622$ (DIE_PVALUE) — roughly one fair die in four produces data at least this uneven.

Cross-checks. (i) The exact generalized log-likelihood-ratio statistic: $$S=\sum_k n_k\log\frac{n_k}{100} =-7.6711080+21.8785868-11.2493367-1.9798653-4.8728630+7.2394754=3.3448892,$$ so $2S=6.6897784$ (DIE_S, DIE_2S, terms DIE_SLOG_*) — the book's $6.68$, and within $3\%$ of $T=6.86$, exactly as the Taylor argument of Step 3 predicts. Its p-value is $0.2447529$. (ii) A direct Monte-Carlo simulation of $200\,000$ fair-die experiments of $600$ rolls gives an empirical $95$th percentile of $T$ equal to $11.08$ against the theoretical $11.07$, an empirical p-value of $0.2301050$ against $0.2312622$, and empirical mean and variance $5.0000891$ and $10.0125251$ against the theoretical $\text{df}=5$ and $2\,\text{df}=10$ (DIE_MC_*). The chi-square approximation is excellent here.

Fig. 4.6 — Solving L25 slide 6. The observed statistic $T=6.86$ falls short of the $5\%$ critical value $11.07$, so the fair-die hypothesis survives. But it exceeds the $25\%$ critical value $6.6256798$ (DIE_CRIT_25) — at the $25\%$ significance level the same data would reject $H_0$, which is the honest way of saying $T=6.86$ is mildly but not decisively unusual (L25 slide 6; B&T Example 9.18).
Gotcha (degrees of freedom, and estimated parameters). The count is $m-1$, not $m$, and only because $\sum_k N_k=n$ is the single constraint. If you had also estimated parameters from the same data before testing (say, fitting a Poisson rate $\lambda$ and then testing the resulting binned PMF), each estimated parameter removes one further degree of freedom. Using $m-1$ there would inflate the critical value and make you reject too rarely.

Continuous data: binning, and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test

Example 4.9 — SOLVING L25 slide 7's open problem, "Do I have the correct pdf?" We have $n=100$ numbers and the hypothesis $H_0$ that they are i.i.d. $N(0,1)$. The slide offers two routes and works neither. Do both.

Route A — bin and run chi-square. Partition the real line into $m$ bins. Under $H_0$ the probability of bin $k$ is $\theta^*_k=\Phi(b_k)-\Phi(b_{k-1})$, computed from the hypothesized CDF, so the expected incidence is $n\theta^*_k$; the observed incidence $N_k$ is just a count. From there it is the die problem verbatim, with $\text{df}=m-1$. Two design points the slide leaves implicit: choose the bins before looking at the data (otherwise the level is meaningless), and choose them coarse enough that every $n\theta_k^*\gtrsim 5$ — with $n=100$ that caps $m$ at roughly $20$. Binning is lossy, which is why the second route is usually preferred for continuous data.

Route B — Kolmogorov–Smirnov. Form the empirical CDF $$\widehat F_X(x)=\frac{1}{n}\#\{i: X_i\le x\}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}\{X_i\le x\},$$ a right-continuous staircase rising by $1/n$ at each data point ($\widehat F_X$ carries a hat because it is estimated; $F_X$ is the hypothesized CDF and is bare). The test statistic is the largest vertical gap between the two curves, $$D_n=\max_x\big|F_X(x)-\widehat F_X(x)\big| ,$$ and the slide quotes the critical value $\mathbf{P}(\sqrt n\,D_n\ge 1.36)\approx 0.05$. Two practical notes the slide omits. First, the maximum is attained at one of the data points, and because $\widehat F_X$ jumps there, you must check both sides: $$D_n=\max_{i}\ \max\Big\{\frac{i}{n}-F_X(x_{(i)}),\ F_X(x_{(i)})-\frac{i-1}{n}\Big\},$$ with $x_{(1)}\le\dots\le x_{(n)}$ the sorted data. Second, $1.36$ is the asymptotic value; at $n=100$ the exact $5\%$ critical value of $D_n$ is $0.1340279$, i.e. $\sqrt n D_n=1.3403$, against the asymptotic $1.36$ (KS_CRIT_D_EXACT, KS_CRIT_D). The asymptotic rule is mildly conservative; and indeed $\mathbf{P}(\sqrt n D_n\ge1.36)=0.0494859$ in the limit (KS_ASYMP_P_AT_136).

Numbers, case 1: data that really are normal. Generating $100$ standard normal values (seeded, saved in computes/g7_s4_kssample.npy) gives $$D_n=0.0572481,\qquad \sqrt{100}\,D_n=0.5724814 ,$$ with the maximum gap occurring at $x=0.7629324$ (KS_DN, KS_SQRTN_DN, KS_ARGMAX_X); the hand computation matches scipy.stats.kstest to machine precision (KS_DN_SCIPY). Since $0.572<1.36$, $H_0$ is not rejected; the exact p-value is $0.8796555$ (KS_PVALUE).

Numbers, case 2: data that are not. Take $100$ values from an exponential distribution with rate $1$, shifted to have mean $0$ — same mean as $N(0,1)$, similar spread, wrong shape. Then $$D_n=0.1596316,\qquad \sqrt{100}\,D_n=1.5963157>1.36 ,$$ so $H_0$ is rejected, with p-value $0.0107999$ (KSBAD_*). The gap sits at the left edge, where the exponential has a hard floor at $-1$ and the normal does not — which is exactly the shape information that binning coarsely would have thrown away.

Fig. 4.7 — Solving L25 slide 7, in the same format as the slide's NIST figure. The red arrow is $D_n$, the largest vertical gap between the hypothesized CDF and the empirical CDF. Left: genuine normal data, $\sqrt n D_n=0.57$, comfortably under $1.36$. Gotcha (the KS critical value assumes a fully specified $F_X$). The $1.36$ is valid only when $H_0$ names the distribution completely, parameters and all. If you estimate the mean and variance from the same data and then test against $N(\hat\mu,\hat\sigma^2)$, $\widehat F_X$ has been bent toward $F_X$, $D_n$ shrinks, and $1.36$ becomes far too lenient — the correct critical value (Lilliefors') is roughly $0.886/\sqrt n$ rather than $1.36/\sqrt n$. Estimating parameters from data you then test against is the single most common way to make a goodness-of-fit test lie to you.
Practice 4.11. A survey asks $100$ people to pick one of three options. Under $H_0$ the split should be $(50,30,20)$; the observed counts are $(48,35,17)$. Test at the $5\%$ level.
Solution

Expected counts are $50$, $30$, $20$ — all above $5$, so the approximation applies. The terms: $$\frac{(48-50)^2}{50}=\frac{4}{50}=0.08,\qquad \frac{(35-30)^2}{30}=\frac{25}{30}=0.8333333,\qquad \frac{(17-20)^2}{20}=\frac{9}{20}=0.45 .$$ So $T=0.08+0.8333333+0.45=1.3633333$ (P45_T).

Degrees of freedom: $m-1=3-1=2$. The $95$th percentile of $\chi^2_2$ is $5.9914645$ (P45_CRIT). Since $1.363<5.991$, do not reject; the p-value is $0.5057733$ (P45_PVALUE). These data are wholly consistent with the hypothesized split.

Practice 4.12. Explain, without computing anything, why the chi-square goodness-of-fit test is a one-sided test on $T$ (reject only for large $T$), even though the alternative "$\theta\neq\theta^*$" is two-sided in every coordinate.
Solution

$T$ is a sum of squares, so it measures distance from $H_0$ without regard to direction: a count that is too high and a count that is too low contribute identically. Every departure from $\theta^*$, in any coordinate and either direction, pushes $T$ up. Small $T$ therefore means "the data look like $H_0$", which is never evidence against $H_0$; there is no lower tail to reject in.

(Worth knowing: suspiciously small $T$ — say a p-value above $0.999$ — is evidence of a different problem entirely, namely fabricated or over-smoothed data. Fisher famously used exactly this argument against some of Mendel's pea counts. But that is a test of a different hypothesis, not of $H_0$.)

4.9 Which test? Putting the section together

Four machines have gone past: the Bayesian MAP test of G6 §3, the likelihood ratio test of §4.2–§4.6, the significance test of §4.7, and the goodness-of-fit tests of §4.8. Under exam conditions the hard part is almost never the arithmetic — it is deciding which of the four a sentence of prose is describing. The whole decision turns on three yes/no questions asked in a fixed order: is there a genuine prior?, then is $H_1$ a single fully specified distribution?, then does $H_0$ pin down one scalar parameter, or a whole PMF/CDF? Each "no" removes one piece of structure and one guarantee. Read the flowchart top to bottom, then drill it on the three descriptions below.

Fig. 4.8 — The decision guide for L25. Each "no" strips away one more piece of structure: first the prior, then the specificity of $H_1$, then the specificity of $H_0$ itself — and the method gets correspondingly less canonical. Only the second box comes with an optimality theorem (L25 slides 2–7; B&T §9.3–9.4).
Practice 4.13. For each description, walk Fig. 4.8 and name the method, the statistic, and the distribution you would need under $H_0$.
  1. A radar returns one voltage $X$. In the absence of a target $X\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$; a target of known reflectivity makes $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with $\mu,\sigma$ both known. Long-run records say a target is present on $2\%$ of sweeps, and a false alarm costs the same as a missed detection.
  2. Same radar, same two noise models, but no records exist and management refuses to name a target-arrival rate. You are told only "keep false alarms at one in a thousand".
  3. A factory claims its widget lifetimes are exponential with rate $1/100$ hours. You have $200$ measured lifetimes and want to know whether that whole distribution is right, not just its mean.
Solution

(1) The first diamond answers yes: $2\%$ is a genuine, frequency-backed prior, and equal costs make the error criterion the plain probability of error. So this is the Bayesian MAP test of G6 §3 — reject $H_0$ when $L(x)>p_\Theta(\theta_0)/p_\Theta(\theta_1)=0.98/0.02=49$ (P413_MAP_ODDS). The statistic is still the likelihood ratio; what the prior supplies is the threshold, so nothing has to be calibrated against a target $\alpha$.

(2) First diamond no (no prior), second diamond yes (both hypotheses name one complete distribution). This is the LRT of §4.2, calibrated at $\alpha=0.001$. By Example 4.1's algebra the region reduces to $X>\sigma z_{0.999}$, and Neyman–Pearson guarantees no other test with $\alpha\le 0.001$ has smaller $\beta$. You need only the $H_0$ distribution $N(0,\sigma^2)$ to set the threshold; the $H_1$ distribution is used afterwards, to report $\beta$.

(3) First diamond no, second no ($H_1$ is "not exponential(1/100)", a composite of everything else), third no as well: $H_0$ specifies an entire continuous CDF, not one scalar. That is the bottom box — goodness of fit. With continuous data prefer Kolmogorov–Smirnov: $D_n=\max_x|F_X(x)-\widehat F_X(x)|$ with $F_X(x)=1-e^{-x/100}$, rejecting when $\sqrt{200}\,D_n\ge 1.36$. Binning and running chi-square with $\text{df}=m-1$ also works but throws away shape information (§4.8, Route A).

Note the pattern: what changes down the chart is not the arithmetic but how much the problem tells you. Method choice is a bookkeeping question about the hypotheses, never about the data.

Practice 4.14. A colleague reports: "I tested $H_0:\theta=1/2$ against $H_1:\theta>1/2$ for a coin, and I computed $\beta=0.11$." Which box of Fig. 4.8 is she in, and what is wrong with the sentence?
Solution

$H_1:\theta>1/2$ is composite — an uncountable family, one distribution per $\theta$ — so the second diamond answers no and she is in the significance-testing box of §4.7. That box has no $\beta$. A single number "$\beta=0.11$" cannot exist, because $\mathbf{P}(X\notin R;H_1)$ needs one distribution under $H_1$ and $H_1$ names infinitely many.

What she can legitimately report is the power function $\theta\mapsto \mathbf{P}(\text{reject};\theta)$ evaluated at one or more specific alternatives — exactly the $0.2339654,\,0.8801172,\,0.9999943$ table after Example 4.7 (COIN_POWER_*). At $\theta=0.55$, for instance, $\beta=1-0.8801172=0.1198828$ (P414_BETA_AT_0p55) — close to her $0.11$, and a perfectly good statement once the "$\theta=0.55$" clause is attached. That clause is the whole missing ingredient.

Practice 4.15. You have $n=400$ rolls of a die and want to test fairness. A friend says "just run the LRT — it's optimal, Neyman–Pearson says so". Why can you not, and what is the closest thing you can do?
Solution

You cannot form $L(x)$ at all: $H_1$ is "the die is not fair", which is a whole five-dimensional family of PMFs, so $p_X(x;H_1)$ is not a single number and the ratio has no denominator — sorry, no numerator. Neyman–Pearson's hypotheses are simple by assumption; the lemma has nothing to say here (§4.6 Interpretation).

The closest legitimate move is §4.8's generalized likelihood ratio: replace the missing $p_X(x;H_1)$ by $\max_\theta p_X(x;\theta)=p_X(x;\hat\theta)$, the ML fit. That gives $S=\sum_k N_k\log(N_k/n\theta_k^*)$, and its second-order Taylor approximation is the chi-square statistic $T$ with $\text{df}=6-1=5$ — reject iff $T>11.07$ at the $5\%$ level. It is the shape of the LRT with no optimality theorem attached, which is the price of a composite alternative.

Recipe (running a simple binary test end to end).
  1. Write both likelihoods $p_X(x;H_0)$ and $p_X(x;H_1)$ at the observed data, as functions of $x$. For i.i.d. data, multiply the marginals.
  2. Form $L(x)$ and simplify. Cancel every factor common to numerator and denominator, then take logarithms. Almost always the data collapse onto one scalar statistic: $\sum_i X_i$ for a mean shift, $\sum_i X_i^2$ for a variance change, a count for Bernoulli data.
  3. Determine the direction. Solve $\log L(x)>\log\xi$ for that statistic, watching the sign of the coefficient — a negative coefficient flips the inequality and puts the rejection region in the left tail (§4.2 Practice 4.3, §4.4 Gotcha).
  4. Find the distribution of the statistic under $H_0$ — exactly if you can (normal, binomial, chi-square), by the CLT if you must.
  5. Solve $\mathbf{P}(\text{statistic in }R;H_0)=\alpha$ for the threshold. If the statistic is discrete, take the smallest threshold whose exact tail is $\le\alpha$, and never round a CLT answer in the direction that enlarges $R$.
  6. Compute $\beta$ using the distribution of the same statistic under $H_1$. Report it: a test with an uncomputed $\beta$ is half a test.
  7. Decide, and phrase the negative outcome as "not rejected", never "accepted".
§4 cheatsheet.
  • Setup. $H_0:X\sim p_X(x;H_0)$ versus $H_1:X\sim p_X(x;H_1)$, both simple; a test is a rejection region $R$; the semicolon means "in model $H$", never a conditioning bar.
  • Errors. $\alpha(R)=\mathbf{P}(X\in R;H_0)$ (false rejection, type I, significance level); $\beta(R)=\mathbf{P}(X\notin R;H_1)$ (false acceptance, type II); power $=1-\beta$.
  • LRT. $L(x)=p_X(x;H_1)/p_X(x;H_0)$; reject iff $L(x)>\xi$; choose $\xi$ from $\mathbf{P}(L(X)>\xi;H_0)=\alpha$. $\xi=1$ is the ML rule; $\xi=\mathbf{P}(H_0)/\mathbf{P}(H_1)$ is the Bayesian MAP rule of G6 §3.
  • Normal mean ($N(0,1)$ vs $N(1,1)$, $n$ i.i.d.): $L=\exp\{\sum_i x_i-n/2\}$, reject iff $\sum_i x_i>\xi'=\sqrt n\,z_{1-\alpha}$, and $\beta=\Phi(z_{1-\alpha}-\sqrt n)$. At $\alpha=0.05$: $\beta=0.7405,\ 0.3612,\ 0.0646,\ 0.0004$ for $n=1,4,10,25$.
  • Normal variance ($N(0,1)$ vs $N(0,4)$): $L=2^{-n}\exp\{\tfrac38\sum_i x_i^2\}$, reject iff $\sum_i x_i^2>\xi'=$ the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of $\chi^2_n$; $\beta=\mathbf{P}(\chi^2_n\le\xi'/4)$. At $n=10$, $\alpha=0.05$: $\xi'=18.3070$, $\beta=0.0824$, and $\xi=0.9358<1$.
  • Tradeoff. $\xi\uparrow$ shrinks $R$: $\alpha\downarrow$, $\beta\uparrow$. If $L(X)$ is discrete, only finitely many $\alpha$ exist — take the largest attainable $\alpha\le$ target.
  • Neyman–Pearson. Among all tests with false rejection probability $\le\alpha$, the LRT minimizes $\beta$. Proof: fabricate the prior with odds $\xi$, apply MAP optimality, rearrange.
  • Composite $H_1$ (significance testing). Pick a statistic $S$, pick the shape of $R$, pick $\alpha$, solve for $\xi$ using the distribution of $S$ under $H_0$ only. Coin example: $n=1000$, $\xi=1.96\sqrt{250}=30.99\approx31$; $|472-500|=28\le31$ so $H_0$ is not rejected, p-value $0.0819$.
  • Goodness of fit. Generalized LRT $\Rightarrow$ $T=\sum_k (N_k-n\theta^*_k)^2/(n\theta^*_k)\approx 2S\sim\chi^2_{m-1}$. Die: $T=6.86$ against $\xi=11.07$ at $\alpha=0.05$ — not rejected (p-value $0.2313$) — but rejected at $\alpha=0.25$, where $\xi=6.63$.
  • Continuous fit. Bin and use $T$, or use Kolmogorov–Smirnov $D_n=\max_x|F_X(x)-\widehat F_X(x)|$ with $\mathbf{P}(\sqrt n D_n\ge 1.36)\approx0.05$. Normal data: $\sqrt n D_n=0.57$, not rejected; centered exponential data: $1.60$, rejected. Valid only if $F_X$ is fully specified in advance.
