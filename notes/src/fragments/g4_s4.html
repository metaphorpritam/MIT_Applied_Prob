§4   Poisson II: merging, splitting, and random incidence

Sources: L15 slides 1–8 (raster p01–p02) · rec15 problems 1–3 · Bertsekas–Tsitsiklis §6.2 (splitting and merging of Poisson processes, Examples 6.13–6.16; the random incidence paradox and its Figure 6.7; B&T Problems 6.14 and 6.15). Every number on this page is recomputed in computes/g4_s4.py (checked against closed forms, numerical integration, and Monte Carlo); every figure is generated by computes/g4_s4_figs.py.

Section 3 built the Poisson process from three defining properties — time homogeneity, independence over disjoint intervals, and the small-interval probabilities $\mathbf{P}(1,\delta)\approx\lambda\delta$, $\mathbf{P}(0,\delta)\approx1-\lambda\delta$ — and read off its three distributions: the count $N_\tau\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda\tau)$, the interarrival time $T\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)$, and the time to the $k$th arrival $Y_k\sim\text{Erlang}(k)$. This section is about what happens when you combine or take apart such processes, and about one genuinely startling consequence of doing so.

Three operations, then one paradox. Merging: superpose two independent Poisson streams and the result is Poisson at the sum of the rates — which immediately answers "whose arrival comes first?" and turns a hard three-exponential integral (rec15 P3) into two fractions. Splitting: label each arrival by an independent coin flip and each label class is itself Poisson — and, far less obviously, the two label classes are independent processes. Random incidence: an observer who shows up at an arbitrary instant and measures the gap he lands in does not see a typical gap; he sees one with twice the mean length. That last result explains why the bus always seems late, and it is the formal version of the bus paradox you met informally in G2.

Interpretation. Merging and splitting are the continuous-time twins of the Bernoulli operations in §2: merging two Bernoulli processes ORs the trial indicators, splitting thins a Bernoulli process by a coin. Every proof below is the same two-line argument — check the three defining properties on an interval of infinitesimal length $\delta$, discarding anything of order $\delta^2$ — so once you have seen it once you have seen it three times.

4.1 Warm-up: Poisson fishing (the review toolkit at work)

Before combining processes, L15 opens (slide 3) with a single-process problem that uses every tool from §3 at once. The slide poses six questions and answers none of them, so we do all six. Every quantity below is a fresh computation in computes/g4_s4.py.

Example 4.1 (Poisson fishing; L15 slide 3, all six parts — the slide leaves every answer blank). Fish arrive at your hook as a Poisson process of rate $\lambda=0.6$ per hour. Your policy: fish for two hours; if you have caught nothing by then, keep fishing until your first catch, and stop the moment it happens. Find (a) $\mathbf{P}(\text{fish for more than two hours})$; (b) $\mathbf{P}(\text{fish for more than two and less than five hours})$; (c) $\mathbf{P}(\text{catch at least two fish})$; (d) $\mathbb{E}[\text{number of fish}]$; (e) $\mathbb{E}[\text{future fishing time}\mid\text{fished for four hours}]$; (f) $\mathbb{E}[\text{total fishing time}]$.

Setting up the notation. Let $N_\tau$ be the number of fish caught in an interval of length $\tau$; by §3, $N_\tau\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda\tau)$, so $$\mathbf{P}(k,\tau)=\mathbf{P}(N_\tau=k)=\frac{(\lambda\tau)^ke^{-\lambda\tau}}{k!},\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots$$ Let $T$ be the total time you spend fishing (a random variable, because the policy is state-dependent) and let $N$ be the total number of fish you catch. With $\lambda=0.6$ and a two-hour block, the relevant Poisson parameter is $\lambda\cdot2=1.2$ (fish_lam_t2).

(a) The policy says: you fish beyond two hours exactly when the first two hours produced no catch. These are the same event, so $$\mathbf{P}(T>2)=\mathbf{P}(N_2=0)=\frac{(1.2)^0e^{-1.2}}{0!}=e^{-1.2}=0.301194 .$$ (fish_a_P_more_than_2.) About $30\%$ of trips run long.

(b) The event $\{2<T<5\}$ needs two things: no catch in $[0,2]$ (otherwise $T=2$), and then a catch somewhere in $(2,5)$ (otherwise $T\ge5$). Those two statements concern the disjoint intervals $[0,2]$ and $(2,5)$, whose length is $2$ and $3$, so by the independence property they multiply: $$\mathbf{P}(2<T<5)=\mathbf{P}(N_2=0)\cdot\mathbf{P}(N_{3}\ge1) =e^{-1.2}\left(1-e^{-0.6\cdot3}\right)=0.301194\cdot\left(1-0.165299\right)$$ $$=0.301194\cdot0.834701=0.251407 .$$ (fish_exp_m18 $=e^{-1.8}=0.165299$, fish_b_between_2_and_5.)

(c) Here is the subtlety the slide hides. If you catch at least one fish in the first two hours, you stop at time $2$ having caught $N_2$ fish. If you catch none, you continue to your first catch and then stop — with exactly one fish. So the total catch is $$N=\max(N_2,1),$$ and "at least two fish" can only happen through the first branch: $\{N\ge2\}=\{N_2\ge2\}$. Therefore $$\mathbf{P}(N\ge2)=1-\mathbf{P}(N_2=0)-\mathbf{P}(N_2=1) =1-e^{-1.2}-1.2\,e^{-1.2}=1-2.2\,e^{-1.2},$$ using $\mathbf{P}(N_2=1)=(1.2)^1e^{-1.2}/1!=1.2e^{-1.2}=0.361433$. Numerically $$\mathbf{P}(N\ge2)=1-0.301194-0.361433=1-0.662627=0.337373$$ (fish_c_at_least_two; a $400{,}000$-trip simulation gives $0.338210$, fish_mc_c_at_least_two).

(d) Write the total catch as $N=\max(N_2,1)=N_2+\mathbf{1}_{\{N_2=0\}}$: the two expressions agree when $N_2=0$ (both give $1$) and when $N_2\ge1$ (both give $N_2$). Expectation is linear, and the expectation of an indicator is the probability of its event (G2 §2), so $$\mathbb{E}[N]=\mathbb{E}[N_2]+\mathbf{P}(N_2=0)=\lambda\cdot2+e^{-1.2}=1.2+0.301194=1.501194$$ (fish_d_E_num_fish; simulation $1.502065$). Note it exceeds $\mathbb{E}[N_2]=1.2$: the "keep going" clause adds the missing fish on the unlucky trips.

(e) You are told you have fished for four hours. Under the policy that means no catch in $[0,4]$. By the fresh-start property, the process after time $4$ is a Poisson process with the same rate $\lambda$, independent of the past, so the additional waiting time until the first catch is $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ regardless of the four barren hours: $$\mathbb{E}[\text{future fishing time}\mid\text{fished 4 hours}]=\frac1\lambda=\frac1{0.6} =1.666667\ \text{hours}=100\ \text{minutes}$$ (fish_e_future_time). This is memorylessness: the fish have no memory of your bad afternoon.

(f) Condition on the first two hours and use the total expectation theorem (§1): $$\mathbb{E}[T]=\mathbf{P}(N_2\ge1)\cdot\mathbb{E}[T\mid N_2\ge1]+\mathbf{P}(N_2=0)\cdot\mathbb{E}[T\mid N_2=0].$$ On the first branch $T=2$ exactly, so $\mathbb{E}[T\mid N_2\ge1]=2$. On the second branch you have already spent $2$ hours and, by memorylessness again, expect $1/\lambda$ more, so $\mathbb{E}[T\mid N_2=0]=2+1/\lambda$. Substituting, with $q=\mathbf{P}(N_2=0)=e^{-1.2}$, $$\mathbb{E}[T]=(1-q)\cdot2+q\left(2+\frac1\lambda\right)=2+\frac{q}{\lambda} =2+\frac{0.301194}{0.6}=2+0.501990=2.501990\ \text{hours}$$ (fish_f_E_total_time; simulation $2.501243$, fish_mc_f_E_total_time). The "$2+$" is the guaranteed block; the "$q/\lambda$" is the expected overtime.

Gotcha. In part (c) it is tempting to write $\mathbf{P}(N\ge2)=1-\mathbf{P}(N=0)-\mathbf{P}(N=1)$ and then to hunt for $\mathbf{P}(N=0)$. But $\mathbf{P}(N=0)=0$: the policy guarantees you never leave empty-handed. Whenever the stopping rule depends on what has happened so far, write the random variable of interest explicitly as a function of the underlying counts — here $N=\max(N_2,1)$ — before computing anything.
Practice 4.1. Same fishing policy, $\lambda=0.6$/hour. (i) What is $\mathbf{P}(\text{you catch exactly one fish})$? (ii) Given that you fish for more than two hours, what is your expected total fishing time?
Solution

(i) Since $N=\max(N_2,1)$, the event $\{N=1\}$ occurs exactly when $N_2=0$ (you catch one fish after the two-hour mark) or $N_2=1$. These are disjoint, so $$\mathbf{P}(N=1)=\mathbf{P}(N_2=0)+\mathbf{P}(N_2=1)=e^{-1.2}+1.2e^{-1.2}=2.2e^{-1.2} =0.301194+0.361433=0.662627$$ (fish_pr_P_exactly_one_fish). Two-thirds of trips end with exactly one fish — as a check, $0.662627+0.337373=1$ together with part (c), and $\mathbf{P}(N=0)=0$.

(ii) Given $T>2$ (equivalently $N_2=0$), memorylessness restarts the process at time $2$, so the remaining time is $\text{Exp}(0.6)$ with mean $1/0.6$: $$\mathbb{E}[T\mid T>2]=2+\frac1{0.6}=2+1.666667=3.666667\ \text{hours}$$ (fish_pr_E_T_given_more_than_2). Compare with the unconditional $\mathbb{E}[T]=2.501990$: knowing the first two hours failed costs you more than an hour of expected extra fishing.

4.2 Merging independent Poisson processes

Two independent Poisson processes are running: a red bulb flashing at rate $\lambda_1$ and a green bulb flashing at rate $\lambda_2$ (L15 slide 4). Record all flashes on one time axis and forget the colors. What kind of process do you get?

Fig. 4.1 — Merging: the merged process holds the union of the two mark sets, and its rate is the sum (L15 slide 4; B&T §6.2, Example 6.14).
Property (merging; L15 slide 4, B&T §6.2, Example 6.14). Let the red process have rate $\lambda_1$ and the green process rate $\lambda_2$, and let the two processes be independent of each other. Then the merged process — an arrival is recorded whenever either process has one — is Poisson with rate $\lambda_1+\lambda_2$. Moreover, each merged arrival comes from the red process with probability $\lambda_1/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$, independently of all other arrivals and of the arrival times.

Derivation (checking the three defining properties on a small interval). Let $\delta>0$ be small and consider the interval $(t,t+\delta]$; "negligible" below always means "of order $\delta^2$ or smaller", which vanishes relative to $\delta$ as $\delta\to0$.

  1. Time homogeneity. $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ are constants, so the probability law of the merged count in an interval depends on the interval's length only, not on where it sits. ✓
  2. Independence over disjoint intervals. Take two disjoint intervals $I$ and $J$. The red counts on $I$ and $J$ are independent (property of the red process); the same for green; and the red process is independent of the green process by hypothesis. The merged count on $I$ is a function of (red on $I$, green on $I$) and the merged count on $J$ is a function of (red on $J$, green on $J$); these two pairs involve four mutually independent random variables, so functions of them are independent (G2 §4). ✓
  3. Small-interval probabilities. For no merged arrival, both processes must be quiet: $$\mathbf{P}(0\text{ merged arrivals in }\delta)=(1-\lambda_1\delta)(1-\lambda_2\delta) =1-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\delta+\lambda_1\lambda_2\delta^2\approx1-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\delta,$$ the product step using independence of the two processes and the last step dropping the $\delta^2$ term. For exactly one merged arrival, either red fires and green does not, or the other way round — disjoint cases, so add: $$\mathbf{P}(1\text{ merged arrival in }\delta)=\lambda_1\delta(1-\lambda_2\delta) +(1-\lambda_1\delta)\lambda_2\delta=(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\delta-2\lambda_1\lambda_2\delta^2 \approx(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\delta .$$ Two or more merged arrivals requires at least two arrivals somewhere, whose probability is of order $\delta^2$: negligible. ✓

These are exactly the defining properties of a Poisson process of rate $\lambda_1+\lambda_2$, so the merged process is Poisson with that rate. Consequently the merged count over an interval of length $\tau$ is $\text{Poisson}\big((\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\tau\big)$ and the merged interarrival times are $\text{Exp}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$.

Whose arrival is it? Competing exponentials

L15 slide 4 asks: what is the probability that the next arrival comes from the first process? — and gives no answer. Here it is, twice.

First derivation (small intervals; the lecturer's argument). Condition on the merged process having exactly one arrival in $(t,t+\delta]$, and ask for the probability it is red. By the definition of conditional probability, $$\mathbf{P}(\text{red}\mid1\text{ merged arrival}) =\frac{\mathbf{P}(\text{red arrival and no green arrival})}{\mathbf{P}(1\text{ merged arrival})} \approx\frac{\lambda_1\delta}{(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\delta}=\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2},$$ where numerator and denominator come from step 3 above and the $\delta$'s cancel — so the answer does not depend on $\delta$ and survives the limit $\delta\to0$. Because distinct arrivals occur in disjoint (infinitesimal) intervals, and a Poisson process treats disjoint intervals independently, the labels of successive merged arrivals are independent Bernoulli trials with success probability $\lambda_1/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$ (B&T §6.2, Example 6.14).

Second derivation (direct integration). Let $T_1\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda_1)$ and $T_2\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda_2)$ be the first arrival times, independent. Then, conditioning on the value of $T_1$ and using the joint density $f_{T_1,T_2}(t_1,t_2)=f_{T_1}(t_1)f_{T_2}(t_2)$ from G3 §2, $$\mathbf{P}(T_1<T_2)=\int_0^\infty f_{T_1}(t)\,\mathbf{P}(T_2>t)\,dt =\int_0^\infty \lambda_1e^{-\lambda_1t}\,e^{-\lambda_2t}\,dt =\lambda_1\int_0^\infty e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)t}\,dt$$ $$=\lambda_1\left[\frac{-e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)t}}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}\right]_0^\infty =\lambda_1\left(0+\frac1{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}\right)=\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}.$$ With $\lambda_1=2$ and $\lambda_2=3$ this is $2/5=0.400000$; numerical quadrature returns $0.400000$ (merge_int_p_first_from_1) and a $400{,}000$-sample simulation returns $0.399985$ (merge_mc_p_first_from_1).

Property (competing exponentials; B&T §6.2, Example 6.15). If $T_1,\dots,T_n$ are independent with $T_i\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda_i)$, then $$Z=\min\{T_1,\dots,T_n\}\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_n),\qquad \mathbf{P}(T_i=Z)=\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_n},$$ and the winner's identity is independent of the winning time.

Derivation of the minimum. For $z\ge0$, the event $\{Z>z\}$ means every $T_i$ exceeds $z$; independence turns the intersection into a product: $$\mathbf{P}(Z>z)=\mathbf{P}(T_1>z,\dots,T_n>z)=\prod_{i=1}^n\mathbf{P}(T_i>z) =\prod_{i=1}^ne^{-\lambda_iz}=e^{-(\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_n)z},$$ which is the exponential tail with parameter $\sum_i\lambda_i$; hence $F_Z(z)=1-e^{-(\sum\lambda_i)z}$ and $Z\sim\text{Exp}(\sum_i\lambda_i)$. This fills the gap flagged in the rec15 P3 solution, where "$\min\{Y,Z\}$ is exponential with rate $\mu+\nu$" is asserted without proof. For $\lambda_1=2,\lambda_2=3$: $\mathbb{E}[Z]=1/5=0.200000$, simulation $0.199639$ (merge_mc_E_min).

Interpretation. Merging says the same thing twice. Read it forward: put two streams on one axis, rates add. Read it backward: a race between independent exponential clocks is settled by a single exponential clock at the summed rate, and the winner is chosen by a rate-weighted coin flip that carries no information about when the race ended. That second reading is what makes long chains of exponential races (light bulbs, queues, Markov chains in G5) tractable.
Gotcha. Merging needs the component processes to be independent. Two Poisson processes that always fire together (say, green copies red) merge into a Poisson process of rate $\lambda$, not $2\lambda$. And the merged process is Poisson only if each component is; merging two arbitrary renewal processes gives something that is only approximately Poisson (B&T §6.2 discusses why superposing many small independent streams — telephone calls in a city — is nonetheless well modeled as Poisson).

The light bulb example

Fig. 4.2 — The three-bulb race as three merged races in sequence (L15 slide 5; B&T §6.2, Example 6.16).
Example 4.2 (three light bulbs; L15 slide 5 — the slide states the problem and gives no solution; B&T Example 6.16). Each light bulb has an independent $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ lifetime. You install three at time $0$. Find the expected time until the last bulb dies out, and its variance.

Solution. Let $T_{\text{last}}$ be the time until the last of the three dies; equivalently $T_{\text{last}}=\max\{X_1,X_2,X_3\}$ where $X_i\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ is the $i$th bulb's lifetime. Attacking the maximum directly means differentiating $F_{\max}(x)=\left(1-e^{-\lambda x}\right)^3$; the merging view is easier and more instructive. Think of each bulb's death as the first arrival of its own Poisson process of rate $\lambda$; the three processes are independent because the lifetimes are.

Stage 1 (three bulbs alive). The first death is the first arrival of the merged process of rate $\lambda+\lambda+\lambda=3\lambda$, so the time $T_1$ until it is $\text{Exp}(3\lambda)$ and $$\mathbb{E}[T_1]=\frac1{3\lambda}.$$ Stage 2 (two bulbs alive). At the instant of the first death, memorylessness says the two surviving bulbs' remaining lifetimes are again independent $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ random variables, independent of everything before. Merging those two, the additional time $T_2$ to the second death is $\text{Exp}(2\lambda)$: $$\mathbb{E}[T_2]=\frac1{2\lambda}.$$ Stage 3 (one bulb alive). Memorylessness once more: the last bulb's remaining lifetime $T_3$ is $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$, so $\mathbb{E}[T_3]=1/\lambda$.

The total time is $T_{\text{last}}=T_1+T_2+T_3$ and expectation is linear: $$\mathbb{E}[T_{\text{last}}]=\frac1{3\lambda}+\frac1{2\lambda}+\frac1\lambda =\frac{2+3+6}{6\lambda}=\frac{11}{6\lambda}.$$ For $\lambda=1$ that is $0.333333+0.500000+1.000000=1.833333$ (bulb_E_total); a $400{,}000$-sample simulation of $\max\{X_1,X_2,X_3\}$ gives $1.832153$ (bulb_mc_E_total).

Variance. The three stage-times are independent — that is exactly what memorylessness buys: each stage restarts the relevant clocks from scratch. So variances add (G2 §3), and $\operatorname{var}(\text{Exp}(\mu))=1/\mu^2$: $$\operatorname{var}(T_{\text{last}})=\frac1{(3\lambda)^2}+\frac1{(2\lambda)^2}+\frac1{\lambda^2} =\frac1{9\lambda^2}+\frac1{4\lambda^2}+\frac1{\lambda^2}=\frac{4+9+36}{36\lambda^2} =\frac{49}{36\lambda^2}.$$ For $\lambda=1$: $1.361111$ (bulb_var_total), simulation $1.356234$.

Gotcha. $\mathbb{E}[\max]\ne\max$ of the means, and the three stage durations are not the three lifetimes. $T_2$ is not "the second bulb's lifetime"; it is the extra time after the first death. Adding the three lifetimes would give $3/\lambda$, which is not $11/(6\lambda)$.
Practice 4.2 (merging). Two servers answer a help line. Server 1 completes jobs as a Poisson process of rate $\lambda_1=1$ per minute, server 2 at $\lambda_2=4$ per minute, independently. (i) What is the merged completion rate, and what is $\mathbf{P}(\text{the next completion is server 1's})$? (ii) What is $\mathbb{E}[\text{time to the next completion}]$? (iii) What is $\mathbf{P}(\text{exactly 2 completions in the next 30 seconds})$? (iv) Given that exactly 2 completions occur in that half-minute, what is the probability both came from server 2?
Solution

(i) Merged rate $\lambda_1+\lambda_2=1+4=5$ per minute (pr1_merged). By the merging property, the next completion is server 1's with probability $\lambda_1/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)=1/5=0.200000$ (pr1_p_first_from_slow).

(ii) The merged interarrival time is $\text{Exp}(5)$, so $\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot\,]=1/5=0.2$ minutes $=12$ seconds (pr1_E_min_sec).

(iii) Thirty seconds is $\tau=0.5$ minute, so the merged count is $\text{Poisson}(5\cdot0.5)=\text{Poisson}(2.5)$ and $$\mathbf{P}(N_{0.5}=2)=\frac{2.5^2e^{-2.5}}{2!}=0.256516$$ (pr1_P_exactly2_in_30s).

(iv) Given the number of merged arrivals, each one is independently server 2's with probability $\lambda_2/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)=4/5=0.8$, so the answer is $0.8^2=0.640000$ (pr1_P_both_from_fast_given_2). Note how the count and the labels decouple: this is the splitting picture of §4.3 read in reverse.

4.3 Splitting a Poisson process

Now run merging backwards. Email leaves MIT as a Poisson process of rate $\lambda$; each message independently goes to a domestic address with probability $p$ and to a foreign one with probability $1-p$ (L15 slide 6). What are the two output streams?

Fig. 4.3 — Splitting: an independent coin at every arrival sends it to one of two output streams (L15 slide 6; B&T §6.2, Example 6.13).
Property (splitting; L15 slide 6, B&T §6.2, Example 6.13). Start with a Poisson process of rate $\lambda$ and label each arrival "kept" with probability $p$ and "discarded" with probability $1-p$, independently of everything else. Then the kept arrivals form a Poisson process of rate $p\lambda$, the discarded arrivals form a Poisson process of rate $(1-p)\lambda$, and — the surprise — these two processes are independent of each other.

Derivation that each stream is Poisson (small intervals again). Consider the kept stream on an interval of length $\delta$; write $q=1-p$.

  1. Time homogeneity. $\lambda$ and $p$ do not vary with time, so neither does the kept-arrival rate. ✓
  2. Independence over disjoint intervals. Whether an arrival lands in interval $I$ is determined by the original process, which treats disjoint intervals independently; the coins are independent of one another and of the arrival times. So kept counts on disjoint intervals are independent. ✓
  3. Small-interval probabilities. A kept arrival in $(t,t+\delta]$ requires an arrival (probability $\approx\lambda\delta$) and a "keep" coin (probability $p$), and the coin is independent of the arrival, so $$\mathbf{P}(1\text{ kept arrival in }\delta)\approx\lambda\delta\cdot p=(p\lambda)\delta,\qquad \mathbf{P}(0\text{ kept arrivals in }\delta)\approx1-(p\lambda)\delta .$$ Two or more kept arrivals requires two or more arrivals: negligible. ✓

These are the defining properties of a Poisson process at rate $p\lambda$. By the identical argument with $p$ replaced by $q=1-p$, the discarded stream is Poisson at rate $q\lambda$. In particular the number of kept arrivals in an interval of length $\tau$ is $\text{Poisson}(p\lambda\tau)$.

Why the two split streams are independent — the surprising part

The slide asserts each output stream is Poisson and stops there; it does not even mention the independence, which is the part worth proving. Fix an interval of length $\tau$. Let $N$ be the total number of arrivals in it, $K$ the number kept, and $M=N-K$ the number discarded. Given $N=n$, the $n$ coins are independent, so $K$ is $\text{Binomial}(n,p)$ (G2 §1). Then for any integers $k,m\ge0$, by the multiplication rule, $$\mathbf{P}(K=k,M=m)=\mathbf{P}(N=k+m)\cdot\mathbf{P}(K=k\mid N=k+m) =\frac{e^{-\lambda\tau}(\lambda\tau)^{k+m}}{(k+m)!}\cdot\binom{k+m}{k}p^kq^m .$$ Expand the binomial coefficient as $(k+m)!/(k!\,m!)$; the $(k+m)!$ cancels: $$\mathbf{P}(K=k,M=m)=e^{-\lambda\tau}\frac{(\lambda\tau)^{k+m}p^kq^m}{k!\,m!} =e^{-p\lambda\tau}e^{-q\lambda\tau}\frac{(p\lambda\tau)^k}{k!}\frac{(q\lambda\tau)^m}{m!},$$ where we split $(\lambda\tau)^{k+m}p^kq^m=(p\lambda\tau)^k(q\lambda\tau)^m$ and used $e^{-\lambda\tau}=e^{-(p+q)\lambda\tau}=e^{-p\lambda\tau}e^{-q\lambda\tau}$ (valid because $p+q=1$). Regrouping, $$\mathbf{P}(K=k,M=m)=\underbrace{\frac{e^{-p\lambda\tau}(p\lambda\tau)^k}{k!}}_{\textstyle p_K(k)} \cdot\underbrace{\frac{e^{-q\lambda\tau}(q\lambda\tau)^m}{m!}}_{\textstyle p_M(m)} =p_K(k)\,p_M(m)\quad\text{for all }k,m,$$ which is precisely the definition of independence for the pair $(K,M)$ (G2 §4). The same factorization holds jointly across any collection of disjoint intervals, which is what makes the two output processes — not just the two counts — independent.

Numerical check (computes/g4_s4.py, keys split_*), with $\lambda=5$, $p=0.3$, $\tau=2$, so $p\lambda\tau=3$ and $q\lambda\tau=7$. At $(k,m)=(2,5)$: $$\mathbf{P}(K=2,M=5)=0.028614,\qquad p_K(2)=0.224042,\quad p_M(5)=0.127717,$$ $$p_K(2)\,p_M(5)=0.224042\cdot0.127717=0.028614,\qquad \text{difference}=0.000000 .$$ A $400{,}000$-run simulation gives sample mean $2.999980$ for $K$ (theory $p\lambda\tau=3$), sample variance $2.998555$ (theory $3$ — mean equals variance, as for any Poisson), and correlation coefficient $\hat\rho(K,M)=0.001203$, i.e. zero to simulation accuracy.

Gotcha (the independence is a Poisson miracle, not a general fact). Everything hinges on $N$ being Poisson. If the total number of messages were fixed at $n=10$, then $K+M=10$ always, so $M=10-K$ and the two counts are perfectly (negatively) dependent: $\operatorname{cov}(K,M\mid N=10)=-npq=-10\cdot0.3\cdot0.7=-2.1$ (split_cov_given_N10). Even in the Poisson case, $K$ and $M$ are dependent conditionally on $N$. Independence appears only after averaging over the Poisson randomness of $N$: the Poisson distribution has exactly the right amount of variability in $N$ to absorb the constraint $K+M=N$. Knowing that $50$ domestic messages went out this hour tells you nothing about how many foreign ones did.
Interpretation. Splitting says a rate is a "flow", and a coin divides the flow in proportion to its bias: $\lambda\to p\lambda$ and $(1-p)\lambda$. Combined with merging you get a complete algebra of Poisson streams: rates add when streams join, multiply by $p$ when a stream is thinned. This is the algebra you will use to model networks (B&T §6.2's data-network example) and, in G5, continuous-time Markov chains.

Two standard consequences you will use constantly

Property (counts and labels; B&T §6.2). For a Poisson process split by a $p$-coin:
  • Binomial labels given the count. Given that $n$ arrivals occurred in an interval, the number of them that were "kept" is $\text{Binomial}(n,p)$ — used in rec15 P2(b).
  • Geometric waiting in labels. Number the merged arrivals $1,2,\dots$; the index of the first "kept" arrival is $\text{Geometric}(p)$, so the number of discarded arrivals before the first kept one has the shifted-geometric PMF $p_{K-1}(k)=p(1-p)^k$, $k=0,1,\dots$ — used in rec15 P2(c) and P1(d).
Both follow because the labels are i.i.d. Bernoulli($p$) trials, independent of the arrival times (§4.2's merging argument read backwards).

4.4 Random incidence for the Poisson process

A Poisson process of rate $\lambda$ has been running forever. You show up at some instant $t^*$ — "a random time", which the lecturer immediately clarifies to mean "an arbitrary time", not a random variable — and you measure the interarrival interval you happen to land in (L15 slide 7). How long is it?

The naive answer is "an interarrival interval, so $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$, mean $1/\lambda$". It is wrong, and the correct answer is exactly twice as large.

Fig. 4.4 — The random incidence picture and its consequence (L15 slide 7; B&T §6.2, its Figure 6.7).
Definition (random incidence). Fix a time instant $t^*$ far from the start of the process. Let $U$ be the time of the last arrival at or before $t^*$ and $V$ the time of the first arrival after $t^*$. The observed interval is $[U,V]$ and its length is $L=V-U$. Assuming the process has run forever guarantees that $U$ exists, so $L$ is well defined.
Property (random incidence for Poisson; L15 slide 7, B&T §6.2). $L\sim\text{Erlang}(2)$ with parameter $\lambda$: $$f_L(\ell)=\lambda^2\ell e^{-\lambda\ell},\quad \ell\ge0,\qquad \mathbb{E}[L]=\frac2\lambda,\qquad\operatorname{var}(L)=\frac2{\lambda^2}.$$ The interval you land in is on average twice as long as a numbered interarrival interval.

Derivation, in three steps. Split the observed interval at your arrival instant: $$L=V-U=\underbrace{(t^*-U)}_{\text{backward wait }B}+\underbrace{(V-t^*)}_{\text{forward wait }F}.$$ Here $B\ge0$ is the elapsed time since the last arrival and $F\ge0$ is the remaining time to the next one; both are nonnegative and $L=B+F$ identically.

Step 1: $F\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)$. For $x\ge0$, the event $\{F>x\}$ says there is no arrival in the interval $(t^*,t^*+x]$, whose length is $x$. By time homogeneity that probability is $$\mathbf{P}(F>x)=\mathbf{P}(0,x)=e^{-\lambda x},$$ which is the exponential tail, so $F\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)$. (This is just the fresh-start property: the process restarts at $t^*$.)

Step 2: $B\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ too. For $x\ge0$, the event $\{B>x\}$ says there is no arrival in the interval $[t^*-x,t^*)$ — again an interval of length $x$ — so, again by time homogeneity, $$\mathbf{P}(B>x)=\mathbf{P}(0,x)=e^{-\lambda x},$$ and $B\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ as well. The intuition B&T offers is that a Poisson process run backwards in time is again a Poisson process with the same rate — the defining properties are symmetric in time — so looking backward from $t^*$ is probabilistically the same as looking forward. (This is where "running forever" matters: it guarantees the interval $[t^*-x,t^*)$ is genuinely inside the process for every $x$.)

Step 3: $B$ and $F$ are independent, so $L$ is Erlang of order 2. $B$ is determined by the arrivals strictly before $t^*$ and $F$ by the arrivals strictly after $t^*$; these are disjoint regions of the time axis, and the Poisson process is independent over disjoint intervals. So $B$ and $F$ are independent $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ random variables, and $L=B+F$ is the sum of two independent exponentials with the same parameter, which G3 §4's convolution identified as Erlang of order 2: $$f_L(\ell)=\int_0^{\ell}f_B(b)f_F(\ell-b)\,db =\int_0^{\ell}\lambda e^{-\lambda b}\lambda e^{-\lambda(\ell-b)}\,db =\lambda^2e^{-\lambda\ell}\int_0^{\ell}db=\lambda^2\ell e^{-\lambda\ell},\qquad\ell\ge0 .$$ The exponentials combined into $e^{-\lambda b}e^{-\lambda(\ell-b)}=e^{-\lambda\ell}$, a constant in $b$, leaving the integral $\int_0^\ell db=\ell$. Its mean and variance are those of a sum of two independent $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$s: $$\mathbb{E}[L]=\frac1\lambda+\frac1\lambda=\frac2\lambda,\qquad \operatorname{var}(L)=\frac1{\lambda^2}+\frac1{\lambda^2}=\frac2{\lambda^2}.$$

Numerical confirmation (computes/g4_s4.py, keys ri_*), with $\lambda=0.5$ so $1/\lambda=2$ and $2/\lambda=4$. A single realization run over a horizon of $400{,}000$ time units, probed at $400{,}000$ instants:

  • mean observed length $\;3.991422$  vs  theory $2/\lambda=4$;
  • variance of observed length $\;7.874191$  vs  theory $2/\lambda^2=8$;
  • mean forward wait $\;1.994859$ and mean backward wait $\;1.996563$  vs  theory $1/\lambda=2$ each;
  • plain average of all interarrival gaps $\;2.006087$  vs  theory $1/\lambda=2$ — the unbiased number;
  • Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance of the observed lengths from Erlang(2): $0.002712$; from $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$: $0.370236$. The observed lengths are Erlang, emphatically not exponential.
Interpretation (why "paradox" is the wrong word). Nothing is contradictory: the two answers describe two different experiments. "Pick the $k$th interval for a fixed $k$ and measure it" gives $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$. "Pick a time instant and measure the interval containing it" gives Erlang(2). The second procedure is biased toward long intervals for a purely geometric reason: long intervals occupy more of the time axis, so a pin dropped on the axis is more likely to land in one. An interval of length $2\ell$ is twice as likely to catch your $t^*$ as one of length $\ell$. Formally, the observed-length density is the interarrival density re-weighted by length: $$f_L(\ell)=\frac{\ell\,f_T(\ell)}{\mathbb{E}[T]} =\frac{\ell\cdot\lambda e^{-\lambda\ell}}{1/\lambda}=\lambda^2\ell e^{-\lambda\ell},$$ reproducing the Erlang(2) density by a completely different route. §4.5 turns this weighting into the general tool.
Interpretation (tie-back to G2's bus paradox). In G2 we noticed informally that "the bus you wait for is always the late one" and blamed variability. Now we can say it exactly. With Poisson buses at rate $\lambda$: the average gap between consecutive buses is $1/\lambda$, but the gap you land in averages $2/\lambda$, and your own wait $F$ averages $1/\lambda$ — a full average headway, not half of one, which is what a naive "you arrive in the middle of a gap of length $1/\lambda$" argument predicts. Both statements are consistent: your wait is half of the observed gap, $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac2\lambda=\tfrac1\lambda$, and the observed gap is the inflated one. The inflation factor for Poisson is exactly $2$ (ri_ratio).
Gotcha. Random incidence does not say the process changed. The arrivals are still Poisson($\lambda$); every numbered interarrival time is still $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ with mean $1/\lambda$. What changed is the selection rule that decides which interval you report. As B&T put it, whenever two calculations disagree, they are answering questions about different probabilistic mechanisms. Concretely: $\mathbf{P}(L>2/\lambda)=3e^{-2}=0.406006$ for the observed interval, but $\mathbf{P}(T>2/\lambda)=e^{-2}=0.135335$ for a numbered one — three times smaller (ri_P_L_gt_2overlam_erlang, ri_P_L_gt_2overlam_expon).
Practice 4.4 (random incidence, Poisson). Buses on a route are Poisson with rate $\lambda=4$ per hour. You walk up to the stop at an arbitrary moment. (i) What is the average gap between consecutive buses, and what is the average length of the gap you land in? (ii) $\mathbf{P}(\text{your gap exceeds }30\text{ minutes})$, and the same probability for a numbered gap? (iii) $\mathbf{P}(\text{the previous bus left more than }15\text{ minutes ago})$?
Solution

(i) A numbered gap is $\text{Exp}(4)$ with mean $1/4$ hour $=15$ minutes (pr3_typical_mean_min). Your gap is Erlang(2) with mean $2/4=1/2$ hour $=30$ minutes (pr3_seen_mean_min).

(ii) With $\ell_0=0.5$ hour and $\lambda\ell_0=2$, the Erlang(2) tail is $$\mathbf{P}(L>\ell_0)=e^{-\lambda\ell_0}(1+\lambda\ell_0)=e^{-2}(1+2)=3e^{-2}=0.406006$$ (pr3_P_seen_gt_30min; the tail formula is the Poisson statement $\mathbf{P}(N_{\ell_0}\le1)$, since an Erlang(2) exceeds $\ell_0$ exactly when fewer than two arrivals occur in $[0,\ell_0]$). For a numbered gap, $\mathbf{P}(T>0.5)=e^{-2}=0.135335$ (pr3_P_typical_gt_30min) — three times less likely.

(iii) The backward wait $B$ is $\text{Exp}(4)$, so with $x=0.25$ hour, $\mathbf{P}(B>0.25)=e^{-4\cdot0.25}=e^{-1}=0.367879$ (pr3_P_backward_gt_15min). Notice you did not need to know anything about the future.

4.5 Random incidence in renewal processes: length-biasing in general

L15 slide 8 drops the exponential assumption. A renewal process is a sequence of successive arrivals whose interarrival times $X_1,X_2,\dots$ are i.i.d. — but not necessarily exponential. The Poisson process is the special case $X_i\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)$. The length-biasing effect survives, in a form you can compute for any interarrival distribution.

Fig. 4.5 — Length-biasing made visible: wide blocks capture more pins (L15 slide 8; B&T §6.2).
Property (length-biased selection; L15 slide 8, B&T §6.2). Let the interarrival times be i.i.d. with PMF $p_X$ (or PDF $f_X$) and finite mean $\mathbb{E}[X]$. An observer arriving at an arbitrary instant lands in an interval whose length $L$ has $$p_L(\ell)=\frac{\ell\,p_X(\ell)}{\mathbb{E}[X]}\qquad\text{(discrete)},\qquad f_L(\ell)=\frac{\ell\,f_X(\ell)}{\mathbb{E}[X]}\qquad\text{(continuous)},$$ and, given $L=\ell$, the observer's position inside the interval is uniform on $[0,\ell]$, so the expected time to the next arrival is $$\mathbb{E}[\text{time to next arrival}]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{L}{2}\right] =\frac{\mathbb{E}[X^2]}{2\,\mathbb{E}[X]}\;\ge\;\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{2}.$$

Derivation (counting time, not intervals). Run the process over a long horizon of length $\mathcal{T}$. Since the mean interarrival time is $\mathbb{E}[X]$, the horizon contains about $n=\mathcal{T}/\mathbb{E}[X]$ intervals. Of those, a fraction $p_X(\ell)$ have length $\ell$, i.e. about $n\,p_X(\ell)$ of them, and together they occupy total time $$\ell\cdot n\,p_X(\ell)=\frac{\mathcal{T}\,\ell\,p_X(\ell)}{\mathbb{E}[X]}.$$ An instant chosen uniformly in the horizon lands in one of those intervals with probability equal to the fraction of the horizon they occupy: $$\mathbf{P}(L=\ell)=\frac{\mathcal{T}\ell p_X(\ell)/\mathbb{E}[X]}{\mathcal{T}} =\frac{\ell\,p_X(\ell)}{\mathbb{E}[X]},$$ and $\mathcal{T}$ cancels, so the answer does not depend on the horizon. The weights sum to one, as they must: $\sum_\ell \ell p_X(\ell)/\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X]/\mathbb{E}[X]=1$. Given that the observer landed in a particular interval of length $\ell$, his instant is uniform inside it (all positions in that block of time are equally likely), so the remaining time is $U(0,\ell)$ with mean $\ell/2$; averaging over $L$ with the biased weights gives $$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{L}{2}\right]=\sum_\ell\frac{\ell}{2}\cdot\frac{\ell p_X(\ell)}{\mathbb{E}[X]} =\frac{\mathbb{E}[X^2]}{2\mathbb{E}[X]},$$ and since $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=\operatorname{var}(X)+(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\ge(\mathbb{E}[X])^2$, this is at least $\mathbb{E}[X]/2$, with equality only if $\operatorname{var}(X)=0$ (a perfectly punctual timetable). The more irregular the schedule, the longer you wait.

Consistency check with §4.4. For $X\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)$: $\mathbb{E}[X]=1/\lambda$ and $f_L(\ell)=\ell\lambda e^{-\lambda\ell}/(1/\lambda)=\lambda^2\ell e^{-\lambda\ell}$ — Erlang(2), as derived. And $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=2/\lambda^2$, so the expected wait is $(2/\lambda^2)/(2/\lambda)=1/\lambda$, matching the memoryless forward wait. ✓

Interpretation (a second consistency check, B&T Problem 6.27). Take a renewal process whose interarrival times are $\text{Erlang}(2)$ with parameter $\lambda$ — every second arrival of a Poisson process — so $f_X(x)=\lambda^2xe^{-\lambda x}$ and $\mathbb{E}[X]=2/\lambda$. Length-biasing gives $$f_L(\ell)=\frac{\ell\cdot\lambda^2\ell e^{-\lambda\ell}}{2/\lambda} =\frac{\lambda^3\ell^2e^{-\lambda\ell}}{2}=\frac{\lambda^3\ell^2e^{-\lambda\ell}}{2!},$$ which is exactly the $\text{Erlang}(3)$ density. B&T Problem 6.27 derives the same answer the long way, by tracking whether the observer's Poisson-interval index is even or odd. The mean rises from $2/\lambda$ to $3/\lambda$ (a $400{,}000$-interval simulation with $\lambda=1$ gives observed mean $2.999771$ and Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance $0.001345$ from Erlang(3), erl_mc_seen_mean, erl_ks_vs_erlang3): less bias than the Poisson case's factor of $2$, because Erlang(2) interarrivals are less variable than exponential ones.
Example 4.3 (buses 5 or 10 minutes; L15 slide 8 — the slide poses both questions and answers neither). Bus interarrival times are equally likely to be 5 or 10 minutes, independently across intervals. You arrive at a random time. (i) What is the probability that you selected a 5-minute interarrival interval? (ii) What is the expected time to the next arrival?

Solution. The interarrival PMF is $p_X(5)=p_X(10)=1/2$, so $$\mathbb{E}[X]=5\cdot\tfrac12+10\cdot\tfrac12=2.5+5=7.5\ \text{minutes}$$ (bus_mean_interarrival).

(i) Apply the length-biased PMF: $$\mathbf{P}(L=5)=\frac{5\cdot\frac12}{7.5}=\frac{2.5}{7.5}=\frac13=0.333333,\qquad \mathbf{P}(L=10)=\frac{10\cdot\frac12}{7.5}=\frac{5}{7.5}=\frac23=0.666667$$ (bus_P_pick_5, bus_P_pick_10; they sum to $1$). Even though the two interval types are equally common, two-thirds of all time sits inside the long ones, so two-thirds of arriving passengers land in a long one. A $300{,}000$-pin simulation gives $0.333063$ (bus_mc_P_pick_5).

The expected length of the selected interval is $$\mathbb{E}[L]=5\cdot\tfrac13+10\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac53+\tfrac{20}3=\tfrac{25}3=8.333333\ \text{minutes},$$ noticeably larger than the true mean headway $7.5$ (bus_E_selected_len; simulation $8.334683$).

(ii) Given the interval type, your position inside it is uniform, so the expected remaining time is half its length: $5/2=2.5$ minutes in a short interval, $10/2=5$ minutes in a long one. Weighting by the biased probabilities from (i), $$\mathbb{E}[\text{time to next arrival}]=\frac13\cdot\frac52+\frac23\cdot\frac{10}2 =\frac56+\frac{10}3=\frac{5}{6}+\frac{20}{6}=\frac{25}6=4.166667\ \text{minutes}$$ (bus_E_wait; simulation $4.170692$). Cross-check with the formula: $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=25\cdot\frac12+100\cdot\frac12=62.5$, so $\mathbb{E}[X^2]/(2\mathbb{E}[X])=62.5/15=4.166667$. ✓

The wrong answer for comparison. "Average headway is $7.5$, I arrive in the middle, so I wait $3.75$" (bus_E_wait_naive) understates the true wait of $25/6$ by $(25/6-3.75)/(25/6)=10\%$ (bus_naive_shortfall_pct) — equivalently, the true wait is $11.1\%$ larger than the naive answer.

Example 4.4 (the textbook's extreme version; B&T §6.2). Buses are equally likely to be $5$ or $55$ minutes apart. Then $\mathbb{E}[X]=30$ minutes, but $$\mathbf{P}(L=5)=\frac{5\cdot\frac12}{30}=\frac1{12}=0.083333,\qquad \mathbf{P}(L=55)=\frac{55\cdot\frac12}{30}=\frac{11}{12}=0.916667,$$ $$\mathbb{E}[L]=5\cdot\frac1{12}+55\cdot\frac{11}{12}=\frac{5+605}{12}=\frac{610}{12}=50.833333 \ \text{minutes},$$ "considerably larger than $30$", and the expected wait is $\mathbb{E}[L]/2=25.416667$ minutes rather than $15$ (bt_bus_*). Raising the variance while holding the mean fixed inflates the observed interval without bound.
Interpretation (the same bias, three disguises). B&T close §6.2 with a third version: survey bus riders about how crowded their bus was and you will overestimate the average crowding, because a crowded bus contributes more riders to your sample. Class sizes reported by students, queue lengths reported by customers, and interarrival gaps reported by passengers are all length-biased in exactly this way — the sampling unit (a rider, a student, a time instant) is drawn in proportion to the size of the object you are trying to measure.
Gotcha. The uniform-position step needs the observer's instant to be spread over time, not over intervals. If you instead pick an interval at random by its index and then pick a uniform instant inside it, there is no bias: you get $\mathbb{E}[X]/2=3.75$ minutes in Example 4.3. Also, the length-biased formula needs $\mathbb{E}[X]<\infty$; heavy-tailed interarrival distributions can make $\mathbb{E}[L]$ infinite even when every interval is finite.
Recipe (which Poisson tool for which question).
  1. Several independent streams, one observer? Merge: rates add, $\lambda=\sum_i\lambda_i$; each arrival is from stream $i$ with probability $\lambda_i/\sum_j\lambda_j$, independently across arrivals.
  2. One stream, each arrival labeled by an independent coin? Split: the label-$i$ stream is Poisson at rate $p_i\lambda$, and the label streams are mutually independent.
  3. "Which happens first?" or a minimum of exponentials? Competing exponentials: $\min\sim\text{Exp}(\sum_i\lambda_i)$ and the winner is $i$ with probability $\lambda_i/\sum_j\lambda_j$, independently of the winning time.
  4. Given a count, how are arrivals distributed among types? Binomial (two types) or multinomial; the index of the first type-$i$ arrival is geometric.
  5. "I showed up at an arbitrary instant — how long is my interval / my wait?" Random incidence: length-bias. Poisson: $L\sim\text{Erlang}(2)$, $\mathbb{E}[L]=2/\lambda$, wait $1/\lambda$. General renewal: $p_L(\ell)\propto\ell\,p_X(\ell)$, wait $\mathbb{E}[X^2]/(2\mathbb{E}[X])$.
  6. Stuck? Ask whether the randomness you are conditioning on is the process's or the observer's. Steps 1–4 are about the process; step 5 is about the observer.

4.6 Recitation 15, fully worked

Recitation 15 is where the merging/splitting algebra pays off: two of the three problems have an integral-free solution once you see the Poisson structure. Statements are as given in rec15; every gap flagged in the official solutions is filled in below.

Example 4.5 (rec15 P1 — type-A / type-B light bulbs; B&T Problem 6.14). Beginning at time $t=0$ we use bulbs one at a time to light a room, replacing each immediately on failure. Each new bulb is independently equally likely to be type A or type B. Lifetimes are independent of everything else, with $$\text{type A: } f_X(x)=e^{-x}\ (x\ge0),\qquad \text{type B: } f_X(x)=3e^{-3x}\ (x\ge0),$$ i.e. $\text{Exp}(\lambda_A)$ with $\lambda_A=1$ and $\text{Exp}(\lambda_B)$ with $\lambda_B=3$. (a) Expected time until the first failure. (b) $\mathbf{P}(\text{no failure before time }t)$. (c) Given no failure until $t$, the conditional probability that the first bulb is type A. (d) $\mathbf{P}(\text{the first two type-B bulbs together outlast the first type-A bulb})$. (e) If the process stops after exactly 12 failures, the mean and variance of the total illumination time provided by type-B bulbs. (f) Given no failure until $t$, the expected time until the first failure.
Solution

(a) Let $X$ be the time until the first failure and let $A$ (resp. $B$) be the event that the first bulb is type A (resp. B); $\mathbf{P}(A)=\mathbf{P}(B)=1/2$. Given $A$, $X\sim\text{Exp}(1)$ with mean $1/\lambda_A=1$; given $B$, $X\sim\text{Exp}(3)$ with mean $1/\lambda_B=1/3$. The total expectation theorem (§1) gives $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X\mid A]\mathbf{P}(A)+\mathbb{E}[X\mid B]\mathbf{P}(B) =1\cdot\frac12+\frac13\cdot\frac12=\frac12+\frac16=\frac{3+1}{6}=\frac23=0.666667$$ (p1_a_E_first_failure). Note $X$ is a mixture of two exponentials, not an exponential: its density is $\frac12e^{-x}+\frac32e^{-3x}$.

(b) Let $D=\{\text{no failure before time }t\}=\{X>t\}$. Total probability over the bulb type, using the exponential tail $\mathbf{P}(\text{Exp}(\lambda)>t)=e^{-\lambda t}$: $$\mathbf{P}(D)=\mathbf{P}(D\mid A)\mathbf{P}(A)+\mathbf{P}(D\mid B)\mathbf{P}(B) =\frac12e^{-t}+\frac12e^{-3t}.$$ At $t=1$: $\frac12(0.367879)+\frac12(0.049787)=0.208833$ (p1_b_PD_at_t1).

(c) Bayes' rule (G1 §2), with $\mathbf{P}(A\cap D)=\mathbf{P}(D\mid A)\mathbf{P}(A) =\frac12e^{-t}$: $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid D)=\frac{\mathbf{P}(A\cap D)}{\mathbf{P}(D)} =\frac{\frac12e^{-t}}{\frac12e^{-t}+\frac12e^{-3t}} =\frac{e^{-t}}{e^{-t}+e^{-3t}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-2t}},$$ the last step dividing numerator and denominator by $e^{-t}$. At $t=1$: $1/(1+e^{-2})=1/(1+0.135335)=0.880797$ (p1_c_PA_given_D_at_t1). Survival is evidence of longevity: the longer the room stays lit, the more likely the bulb is the long-lived type A. As $t\to\infty$ this tends to $1$; at $t=0$ it is $1/2$, the prior.

(d) — the Poisson-splitting solution (no integrals). Treat the failure times of the type-A bulb and of the type-B bulbs as first arrivals of independent Poisson processes with rates $\lambda_A=1$ and $\lambda_B=3$. Merge them into one process of rate $\lambda_A+\lambda_B=4$; by §4.2 each arrival of the merged process is of type A with probability $$\mathbf{P}(A)=\frac{\lambda_A}{\lambda_A+\lambda_B}=\frac14=0.25,\qquad \mathbf{P}(B)=\frac{\lambda_B}{\lambda_A+\lambda_B}=\frac34=0.75,$$ independently across arrivals. The event of interest, $G=\{Y\ge X_A\}$ where $Y$ is the total lifetime of the first two type-B bulbs and $X_A$ that of the first type-A bulb, says: the first type-A arrival occurs no later than the second type-B arrival. Looking at the merged sequence of labels, that happens exactly when the first label is A, or the first is B and the second is A. These are disjoint, and the labels are independent, so $$\mathbf{P}(G)=\mathbf{P}(A)+\mathbf{P}(B)\mathbf{P}(A)=\frac14+\frac34\cdot\frac14 =\frac{4}{16}+\frac{3}{16}=\frac7{16}=0.437500$$ (p1_d_answer; the official solution states $7/16$ without this substitution). Simulation: $0.438128$ (p1_d_mc).

(d) — the integral solution, with the omitted steps. $X_A\sim\text{Exp}(1)$ and, as a sum of two independent $\text{Exp}(3)$ lifetimes, $Y\sim\text{Erlang}(2)$ with $\lambda=3$: $f_Y(y)=9ye^{-3y}$ for $y\ge0$ (G3 §4). Conditioning on $Y=y$ and integrating the inner density, $$\mathbf{P}(G)=\int_0^\infty f_Y(y)\left[\int_0^y f_{X_A}(x)\,dx\right]dy =\int_0^\infty 9ye^{-3y}\Big[-e^{-x}\Big]_{x=0}^{x=y}dy =\int_0^\infty 9ye^{-3y}\left(1-e^{-y}\right)dy$$ $$=9\int_0^\infty\left(ye^{-3y}-ye^{-4y}\right)dy .$$ The official solution now writes down an antiderivative; here is the integration by parts. For $a>0$, take $u=y$, $dv=e^{-ay}dy$, so $du=dy$ and $v=-e^{-ay}/a$: $$\int ye^{-ay}\,dy=-\frac{y e^{-ay}}{a}+\frac1a\int e^{-ay}\,dy =-\frac{ye^{-ay}}{a}-\frac{e^{-ay}}{a^2}.$$ With $a=3$ and $a=4$, $$9\int_0^\infty\left(ye^{-3y}-ye^{-4y}\right)dy =9\left[-\frac{y e^{-3y}}{3}-\frac{e^{-3y}}{9}+\frac{ye^{-4y}}{4}+\frac{e^{-4y}}{16}\right]_0^\infty .$$ As $y\to\infty$ every term contains $e^{-3y}$ or $e^{-4y}$ and therefore vanishes (exponentials beat polynomials), giving $0$ at the upper limit. At $y=0$ the two terms with a factor $y$ vanish and the bracket equals $-\frac19+\frac1{16}$. Subtracting, $$\mathbf{P}(G)=9\left[0-\left(-\frac19+\frac1{16}\right)\right]=9\left(\frac19-\frac1{16}\right) =9\cdot\frac{16-9}{144}=9\cdot\frac{7}{144}=\frac7{16}=0.437500,$$ agreeing with the splitting argument (numerical quadrature: $0.437500$, p1_d_integral). Two remarks on the printed solution: the event $G$ is used but never defined (it is $\{Y\ge X_A\}$), and the problem says "longer than" ($Y>X_A$) while the solution computes $\mathbf{P}(Y\ge X_A)$ — immaterial, since $\mathbf{P}(Y=X_A)=0$ for continuous random variables.

(e) Let $N$ be the number of type-B bulbs among the first $12$, let $X_i$ be the $i$th type-B bulb's lifetime, and let $V=X_1+\cdots+X_N$ be the total type-B illumination time (with $V=0$ if $N=0$). Each of the $12$ bulbs is type B independently with probability $1/2$, so $N\sim\text{Binomial}(12,1/2)$ and $$\mathbb{E}[N]=12\cdot\tfrac12=6,\qquad\operatorname{var}(N)=12\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=3 .$$ Each $X_i\sim\text{Exp}(3)$, so $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=1/3$ and $\operatorname{var}(X_i)=1/9$. $N$ is independent of the lifetimes (bulb type is chosen independently of how long bulbs last), so the random-sum formulas of §2 apply: $$\mathbb{E}[V]=\mathbb{E}[N]\,\mathbb{E}[X]=6\cdot\frac13=2,$$ $$\operatorname{var}(V)=\operatorname{var}(X)\,\mathbb{E}[N]+(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N) =\frac19\cdot6+\frac19\cdot3=\frac69+\frac39=\frac99=1 .$$ (p1_e_EV, p1_e_varV; simulation gives $2.001431$ and $1.002604$.) The printed solution has a typo — the summands are introduced as $X_i$ but the sum is written with $Y$'s.

(f) Let $T$ be the time until the first failure (called $X$ in parts (a)–(c); the change of name is the recitation's own). We want $\mathbb{E}[T\mid D]$ where $D=\{T>t\}$. Write $T=t+(T-t)$ and condition further on the bulb type: $$\mathbb{E}[T\mid D]=t+\mathbb{E}[T-t\mid D\cap A]\,\mathbf{P}(A\mid D) +\mathbb{E}[T-t\mid D\cap B]\,\mathbf{P}(B\mid D).$$ The two conditional expectations are where memorylessness enters — the step the official solution omits. Given $A$ and $T>t$, the bulb is exponential with rate $1$ and has already survived $t$; by memorylessness its remaining life is again $\text{Exp}(1)$, so $\mathbb{E}[T-t\mid D\cap A]=1/\lambda_A=1$. Likewise $\mathbb{E}[T-t\mid D\cap B]=1/\lambda_B=1/3$. With $\mathbf{P}(A\mid D)=\frac1{1+e^{-2t}}$ from (c) and $\mathbf{P}(B\mid D)=1-\frac1{1+e^{-2t}}$, $$\mathbb{E}[T\mid D]=t+1\cdot\frac1{1+e^{-2t}}+\frac13\left(1-\frac1{1+e^{-2t}}\right).$$ Now the algebra the solution also skips: group the two terms containing $\frac1{1+e^{-2t}}$, $$\frac1{1+e^{-2t}}-\frac13\cdot\frac1{1+e^{-2t}}=\left(1-\frac13\right)\frac1{1+e^{-2t}} =\frac23\cdot\frac1{1+e^{-2t}},$$ leaving the constant $\frac13$ behind, so $$\mathbb{E}[T\mid D]=t+\frac13+\frac23\cdot\frac1{1+e^{-2t}} .$$ At $t=1$: $1+0.333333+0.666667\cdot0.880797=1+0.333333+0.587198=1.920531$ (p1_f_ET_given_D_t1, cross-checked against the unsimplified expression, p1_f_check_direct). Sanity: at $t=0$ it gives $\frac13+\frac23\cdot\frac12=\frac23$, matching part (a) as it must, and for large $t$ it behaves like $t+1$ — conditioning on long survival makes type A nearly certain, and a type-A bulb has mean residual life $1$.

Example 4.6 (rec15 P2 — service station; B&T Problem 6.15). A service station handles jobs of two types. Type-A arrivals are Poisson at $\lambda_A=3$ per minute and type-B arrivals Poisson at $\lambda_B=4$ per minute, independently. (a) Mean, variance, and PMF of the total number of jobs arriving in a given three-minute interval. (b) Given that exactly $10$ jobs arrived during a 10-minute interval, the probability that exactly $3$ were type A. (c) The PMF of the number of type-B jobs that arrive before the first type-A job.
Solution

(a) The total arrival process is the merge of two independent Poisson processes, hence Poisson with rate $$\lambda=\lambda_A+\lambda_B=3+4=7\text{ per minute}$$ (§4.2). Over an interval of length $\tau=3$ minutes the count $N$ is $\text{Poisson}(\lambda\tau)=\text{Poisson}(21)$, so $$\mathbb{E}[N]=21,\qquad\operatorname{var}(N)=21,\qquad p_N(n)=\frac{21^ne^{-21}}{n!},\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots$$ (p2_a_mean, p2_a_var; as a spot check $p_N(21)=0.086712$, p2_a_pmf_at_21). The mean equals the variance, the signature of a Poisson count.

(b) By the merging property, each arrival of the merged process is type A with probability $$p=\frac{\lambda_A}{\lambda_A+\lambda_B}=\frac37=0.428571,$$ independently of the others and of the arrival times. So, conditioned on $10$ arrivals, the number of type-A ones is $\text{Binomial}(10,3/7)$ and $$\mathbf{P}(3\text{ of the }10\text{ are type A})=\binom{10}{3}\left(\frac37\right)^3 \left(\frac47\right)^7=120\cdot0.078717\cdot0.019895=0.187925$$ (p2_b_answer). Note the 10-minute window length never enters: the labels are independent of the timing.

(c) Again by merging, the successive arrivals are independently type A with probability $3/7$. Let $K$ be the index of the first type-A arrival; $K$ is geometric with parameter $3/7$ on $\{1,2,\dots\}$. The number of type-B arrivals before the first type-A arrival is $K-1$, whose PMF is the geometric shifted one unit to the left: $$p_{K-1}(k)=\left(\frac37\right)\left(\frac47\right)^{k},\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots$$ Numerically $p_{K-1}(0)=0.428571$, $p_{K-1}(1)=0.244898$, $p_{K-1}(2)=0.139942$ (p2_c_pmf_at_0 … p2_c_pmf_at_2), and the mean is $\mathbb{E}[K-1]=\frac{1-p}{p}=\frac{4/7}{3/7}=\frac43=1.333333$ (p2_c_mean). The printed solution labels this PMF $p_K(k)$, but by its own text it is the PMF of $K-1$; $K$ itself lives on $\{1,2,\dots\}$. Also note the geometric service times of type-B jobs are a red herring for all three parts — nothing here depends on how long jobs stay.

Example 4.7 (rec15 P3 — $\mathbf{P}(X<Y<Z)$ for three independent exponentials). Let $X,Y,Z$ be independent exponential random variables with parameters $\lambda$, $\mu$, $\nu$. Find $\mathbf{P}(X<Y<Z)$.
Solution

Rewrite the event. Saying $X<Y<Z$ is the same as saying two things at once: $X$ is smaller than both $Y$ and $Z$, and $Y$ is smaller than $Z$. Formally $$\{X<Y<Z\}=\{X<\min\{Y,Z\}\}\cap\{Y<Z\}.$$ (If $Y<Z$ then $\min\{Y,Z\}=Y$, so the right side reduces to $X<Y<Z$; conversely $X<Y<Z$ implies both statements.)

Second factor. View $Y$ and $Z$ as first arrival times of independent Poisson processes with rates $\mu$ and $\nu$. Merge them: $Y<Z$ exactly when the first arrival of the merged process belongs to the $\mu$-process, so by §4.2 $$\mathbf{P}(Y<Z)=\frac{\mu}{\mu+\nu}=\frac{2}{2+3}=0.400000$$ for the illustrative values $\lambda=1,\mu=2,\nu=3$ (p3_P_Y_lt_Z).

First factor. The official solution uses without proof that $\min\{Y,Z\}$ is exponential with parameter $\mu+\nu$; §4.2 derived it: $\mathbf{P}(\min\{Y,Z\}>t)=e^{-\mu t}e^{-\nu t}=e^{-(\mu+\nu)t}$. So $\min\{Y,Z\}$ is the first arrival of a Poisson process of rate $\mu+\nu$, and racing it against $X$'s process of rate $\lambda$ (merging once more), $$\mathbf{P}\big(X<\min\{Y,Z\}\big)=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu+\nu}=\frac{1}{1+2+3}=\frac16 =0.166667$$ (p3_P_X_lt_min).

Independence of the two factors. This is the step that makes multiplication legal. In the merged three-way process, "who wins the race" is independent of "when the race is won" and of the labels of the other arrivals (§4.2). The event $\{X<\min\{Y,Z\}\}$ says the first arrival of the three-way merge is the $\lambda$-arrival; the event $\{Y<Z\}$ says that, among the $\mu$- and $\nu$-processes, the $\mu$-process arrives first — a statement about a different, independent piece of the labeling. Hence $$\mathbf{P}(X<Y<Z)=\mathbf{P}\big(X<\min\{Y,Z\}\big)\cdot\mathbf{P}(Y<Z) =\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu+\nu}\cdot\frac{\mu}{\mu+\nu} =\frac{\lambda\mu}{(\lambda+\mu+\nu)(\mu+\nu)} .$$ With $\lambda=1,\mu=2,\nu=3$: $\frac{1\cdot2}{6\cdot5}=\frac2{30}=0.066667$ (p3_answer); a $400{,}000$-sample simulation gives $0.066348$ (p3_mc).

Brute-force check. Integrating directly, conditioning on $Y=y$ and requiring $X<y$ and $Z>y$, $$\mathbf{P}(X<Y<Z)=\int_0^\infty\mu e^{-\mu y}\left(1-e^{-\lambda y}\right)e^{-\nu y}\,dy =\mu\left[\frac1{\mu+\nu}-\frac1{\lambda+\mu+\nu}\right] =\frac{\lambda\mu}{(\mu+\nu)(\lambda+\mu+\nu)},$$ using $\int_0^\infty e^{-ct}dt=1/c$ twice and combining the fractions over the common denominator $(\mu+\nu)(\lambda+\mu+\nu)$, whose numerator is $(\lambda+\mu+\nu)-(\mu+\nu)=\lambda$. Numerical quadrature: $0.066667$ (p3_integral). Same answer, far more work — the merging view is the point of the problem.

Practice 4.6 (synthesis). Customers arrive at a shop as a Poisson process of rate $\lambda=12$ per hour. Independently of everything, each customer buys with probability $1/3$. (i) What is the process of buyers, and what is the expected time until the third buyer? (ii) You walk past the shop at an arbitrary instant; what is the expected length of the gap between consecutive buyers that you land in? (iii) A second, independent shop next door has buyers arriving at rate $2$ per hour. What is the probability that the next buyer anywhere on the street is from your shop?
Solution

(i) Splitting (§4.3): buyers are Poisson at rate $p\lambda=\frac13\cdot12=4$ per hour. The time until the third buyer is $\text{Erlang}(3)$ with parameter $4$, so its mean is $3/4=0.75$ hour $=45$ minutes (the Erlang mean $k/\lambda$ from §3).

(ii) Random incidence (§4.4) applied to the buyer process, which is Poisson(4): the gap you land in is Erlang(2) with parameter $4$, mean $2/4=0.5$ hour $=30$ minutes — twice the mean gap between buyers, $1/4$ hour $=15$ minutes.

(iii) Merging (§4.2) of two independent buyer processes at rates $4$ and $2$: the merged rate is $6$ per hour and the next buyer is yours with probability $4/(4+2)=2/3\approx0.666667$. Note how all three tools compose without any new computation.

§4 cheatsheet — merging, splitting, random incidence.
  • Merging (L15 slide 4). Independent Poisson($\lambda_1$), Poisson($\lambda_2$) superposed $\Rightarrow$ Poisson($\lambda_1+\lambda_2$). Each merged arrival is from stream 1 with probability $\lambda_1/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$, independently across arrivals and independently of the times.
  • Competing exponentials (B&T Example 6.15). $T_i\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda_i)$ independent $\Rightarrow\min_iT_i\sim\text{Exp}(\sum_i\lambda_i)$ and $\mathbf{P}(T_i\text{ smallest})=\lambda_i/\sum_j\lambda_j$.
  • Three bulbs (L15 slide 5). $\mathbb{E}[\max]=\frac1{3\lambda}+\frac1{2\lambda}+\frac1\lambda =\frac{11}{6\lambda}$; $\operatorname{var}=\frac{49}{36\lambda^2}$.
  • Splitting (L15 slide 6). $p$-coin on each arrival $\Rightarrow$ Poisson($p\lambda$) and Poisson($(1-p)\lambda$), and the two streams are independent: $\mathbf{P}(K=k,M=m)=p_K(k)p_M(m)$ exactly. Given the total count $n$, the split is Binomial($n,p$); the index of the first kept arrival is Geometric($p$).
  • Random incidence, Poisson (L15 slide 7). $L=B+F$ with $B,F$ independent $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$; $f_L(\ell)=\lambda^2\ell e^{-\lambda\ell}$ (Erlang of order 2), $\mathbb{E}[L]=2/\lambda$, $\operatorname{var}(L)=2/\lambda^2$; your wait is $1/\lambda$.
  • Random incidence, renewal (L15 slide 8). $p_L(\ell)=\ell\,p_X(\ell)/\mathbb{E}[X]$; $\mathbb{E}[\text{wait}]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]/(2\mathbb{E}[X])\ge\mathbb{E}[X]/2$, with equality only for a deterministic timetable. Buses of $5$ or $10$ minutes: $\mathbf{P}(L=5)=1/3$, $\mathbb{E}[\text{wait}]=25/6=4.166667$ minutes, not $3.75$.
  • Poisson fishing numbers ($\lambda=0.6$/hr): $0.301194$, $0.251407$, $0.337373$, $1.501194$, $1.666667$ hr, $2.501990$ hr.
