§2 built the Bernoulli process: a clock that ticks once per slot, and at each tick an arrival happens with probability $p$, independently of every other tick. That model needs a slot length. Real arrival streams — emails landing in an inbox, photons hitting a detector, cars reaching an intersection, customers walking into a bank — do not come with one. You could discretize into minutes, but then two accidents in the same minute count as one; discretize into seconds and the model changes; discretize into milliseconds and it changes again. Rather than make an arbitrary choice, we take the slot width to zero and keep the rate fixed. What survives that limit is the Poisson process (B&T §6.2, L14 slide 3).

This section does four things, in the order L14 does them. First it states the three defining properties of a Poisson process and says what each one means. Second it derives — every step, no hand-waving — the fact that the number of arrivals in a window of length $\tau$ is a Poisson random variable with parameter $\lambda\tau$, as the limit of binomial PMFs. Third it derives the distribution of the time until the first arrival (exponential) and the memorylessness that goes with it. Fourth it derives the density of the time until the $k$th arrival (Erlang), twice. It closes with the Bernoulli/Poisson dictionary of L14 slide 7, and works recitation 14 in full — three problems that live on the Bernoulli side of that dictionary and so let you read the correspondence in both directions.

Interpretation. Everything in this section follows from one sentence: a Poisson process is a Bernoulli process whose slots have become infinitesimally short, with the number of slots per unit time growing in exact compensation so that $\lambda = p/\delta$ stays fixed. The three named distributions of the discrete world — binomial, geometric, Pascal — each have a continuous limit, and those limits are the Poisson, the exponential and the Erlang. If you remember the discrete facts from G2 and §2, you already know the shape of every answer here; what remains is to do the limits carefully.

3.1 The three defining properties

An arrival process in continuous time is a random collection of instants $0 < Y_1 < Y_2 < \cdots$ on the half-line, called arrival times. Rather than describe the process by listing those instants, we describe it by the probabilities of counts. Write

$$P(k,\tau)=\mathbf{P}\big(\text{exactly }k\text{ arrivals in an interval of length }\tau\big),$$

where $k=0,1,2,\dots$ is a nonnegative integer and $\tau>0$ is a duration (not a location — the notation already builds in the first property below). The single parameter of the model is $\lambda>0$, the arrival rate or intensity, measured in arrivals per unit time.

Definition (Poisson process; L14 slide 3, B&T §6.2 definition box, p. 310). An arrival process is a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$ if:
  1. (Time homogeneity) The probability $P(k,\tau)$ of $k$ arrivals is the same for every interval of length $\tau$, wherever that interval sits on the time axis.
  2. (Independence) The number of arrivals during any interval is independent of the history of arrivals outside that interval.
  3. (Small-interval probabilities) For a duration $\delta$ shrinking to zero, $$P(0,\delta)=1-\lambda\delta+o(\delta),\qquad P(1,\delta)=\lambda\delta+o_1(\delta),\qquad P(k,\delta)=o_k(\delta)\ \ (k\ge 2),$$ where $o(\delta)$, $o_1(\delta)$, $o_k(\delta)$ denote functions of $\delta$ that are negligible compared with $\delta$ itself: $\lim_{\delta\to0}o(\delta)/\delta=0$, and likewise for the others.

Counts in the disjoint windows $t_1,t_2,t_3$ are independent, and each depends on its window only through the window's length. Blown up below: a window so short that it can hold at most one arrival — the bridge back to Bernoulli (L14 slide 3; B&T §6.2).

What each property is saying

Interpretation (property 1, time homogeneity). Arrivals are "equally likely at all times". Formally, the count in $[t,t+\tau]$ has the same distribution for every $t$; the answer depends on $\tau$ and not on $t$. This is the exact counterpart of the Bernoulli assumption that the success probability $p$ is the same on every trial. It does not say arrivals are evenly spaced — an actual sample path is ragged, with clumps and long empty stretches (look at Fig. 3.1: three arrivals in $t_1$, one in $t_2$). It says only that the probability law does not drift with the clock.
Interpretation (property 2, independence). Fix an interval $[t,t']$. The unconditional probability of $k$ arrivals in it is $P(k,t'-t)$. Property 2 says that if somebody hands you complete information about the arrivals outside $[t,t']$ — how many there were before $t$, exactly when they occurred, how many came after $t'$ — the conditional probability of $k$ arrivals in $[t,t']$ is still $P(k,t'-t)$. Nothing outside the window is informative about the inside. This is the continuous version of "trials are independent", and it is what will let us multiply probabilities freely below.
Interpretation (property 3, small intervals). This is the property that pins down the shape of the whole distribution, and it is worth reading slowly. It says three things about a very short window of length $\delta$. (i) Almost certainly nothing happens; the chance of nothing happening falls away from $1$ linearly, at slope $\lambda$. (ii) The chance of exactly one arrival grows linearly from $0$, also at slope $\lambda$ — that is what makes $\lambda$ "the rate": $\lambda = \lim_{\delta\to0}P(1,\delta)/\delta$, arrivals per unit time. (iii) Two or more arrivals in the same tiny window is not merely unlikely, it is negligible even relative to $\delta$: $P(k\ge2,\delta)/\delta\to0$. Arrivals do not come in simultaneous bunches.

Property 3 is exactly what makes the Bernoulli comparison legitimate: a window of length $\delta$ behaves like one Bernoulli trial with success probability $p=\lambda\delta$, because it produces one arrival or none, and the "or more" case has been legislated away in the limit. That observation is the engine of §3.2.

Solution

(a) Independence fails hardest — knowing a bus just came tells you the next is 10 minutes away. (Time homogeneity also fails at the level of the timetable.) 3.2 The number of arrivals: binomial $\to$ Poisson, derived

Fix a window length $t>0$ and let $N_t$ be the number of arrivals in it. We compute the PMF of $N_t$ by discretizing, using the binomial answer we already have, and taking a limit. This is L14 slide 4 and B&T §6.1, its Figure 6.5.

Step 1 — the discrete approximation

Chop $[0,t]$ into $n$ slots each of length $\delta=t/n$. Here $n$ is a large positive integer, our only free knob; $\delta$ is the slot width. By property 3, each slot contains one arrival with probability approximately

$$p=\lambda\delta=\lambda\frac tn,$$

no arrival with probability approximately $1-p$, and two or more with probability negligible compared with $\delta$. By property 2, distinct slots are independent, and by property 1 they all carry the same $p$. So the $n$ slots are $n$ independent Bernoulli trials, and the count

$$\tilde N_n=\#\{\text{slots containing an arrival}\}\ \sim\ \text{binomial}(n,p),\qquad \mathbf{P}(\tilde N_n=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}.$$

Two things to notice before we take the limit. First, $\tilde N_n$ undercounts $N_t$ whenever a slot holds two or more arrivals — and property 3 says that source of error washes out as $n\to\infty$. Second, the product

$$np=n\cdot\lambda\frac tn=\lambda t$$

does not depend on $n$ at all. That is the whole trick: refine the discretization and the success probability shrinks exactly as fast as the number of trials grows, so the expected number of arrivals stays pinned at $\lambda t$. Write $\mu=\lambda t$ for that fixed product; it is the only quantity that will survive.

Step 2 — the limit, every factor accounted for

Fix $k$ and substitute $p=\mu/n$:

$$\mathbf{P}(\tilde N_n=k)=\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}\left(\frac\mu n\right)^k\left(1-\frac\mu n\right)^{n-k}.$$

Expand $n!/(n-k)!=n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)$ — a product of exactly $k$ factors — and split the last power as $(1-\mu/n)^{n-k}=(1-\mu/n)^{n}\,(1-\mu/n)^{-k}$:

$$\mathbf{P}(\tilde N_n=k) =\underbrace{\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k}}_{A_n} \cdot\underbrace{\frac{\mu^k}{k!}}_{\text{no }n} \cdot\underbrace{\left(1-\frac\mu n\right)^{n}}_{B_n} \cdot\underbrace{\left(1-\frac\mu n\right)^{-k}}_{C_n},$$

where the $n^k$ in $A_n$ came out of $(\mu/n)^k=\mu^k/n^k$. Now take $n\to\infty$ with $k$ and $\mu$ held fixed, one factor at a time.

Factor $A_n$. Divide each of the $k$ numerator factors by one copy of $n$: $$A_n=\frac nn\cdot\frac{n-1}n\cdots\frac{n-k+1}n=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\Big(1-\frac jn\Big).$$ This is a product of a fixed number $k$ of terms, each of which tends to $1$, so $A_n\to1$. (It matters that $k$ is fixed; if $k$ grew with $n$ the argument would collapse.)

Factor $B_n$. Take logarithms and expand: $$\ln B_n=n\ln\Big(1-\frac\mu n\Big) =n\left(-\frac\mu n-\frac{\mu^2}{2n^2}-\frac{\mu^3}{3n^3}-\cdots\right) =-\mu-\frac{\mu^2}{2n}-\frac{\mu^3}{3n^2}-\cdots\ \longrightarrow\ -\mu,$$ using the series $\ln(1-x)=-x-x^2/2-x^3/3-\cdots$, valid for $|x|<1$, which applies once $n>\mu$. Every term after the first carries a positive power of $1/n$ and dies. Exponentiating (the exponential is continuous, so limits pass through it), $B_n\to e^{-\mu}$. This is the familiar $\lim_{n\to\infty}(1-\mu/n)^n=e^{-\mu}$, as B&T note in the footnote to §6.1.

Factor $C_n$. $(1-\mu/n)^{-k}$ is a fixed power $-k$ of something tending to $1$, so $C_n\to1$.

Multiplying the four limits (the product of convergent sequences converges to the product of the limits):

$$\boxed{\ \mathbf{P}(N_t=k)=P(k,t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}(\tilde N_n=k) =\frac{\mu^k e^{-\mu}}{k!}=\frac{(\lambda t)^k e^{-\lambda t}}{k!},\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots\ }$$

which is L14 slide 4's formula, written there with $\tau$ in place of $t$. The intermediate values at $n=100$, $k=2$, $\mu=2.5$ make the four factors concrete (lim_factor_*): $A_{100}=\frac{100\cdot99}{100^2}=0.99$, $\mu^k/k!=2.5^2/2=3.125$, $B_{100}=(1-0.025)^{100}=0.0795173$ against the limit $e^{-2.5}=0.0820850$, and $C_{100}=(1-0.025)^{-2}=1.0519395$. Their product is $0.99\times3.125\times0.0795173\times1.0519395=0.2587841$, which is exactly the binomial value $\binom{100}{2}(0.025)^2(0.975)^{98}=0.2587841$; the Poisson limit is $0.2565156$.

Property (number of arrivals; L14 slide 4, B&T §6.2, p. 311). In a Poisson process of rate $\lambda$, the number $N_\tau$ of arrivals in any window of total length $\tau$ is a Poisson random variable with parameter $\lambda\tau$: $$P(k,\tau)=\frac{(\lambda\tau)^ke^{-\lambda\tau}}{k!},\qquad \mathbb{E}[N_\tau]=\lambda\tau,\qquad \operatorname{var}(N_\tau)=\lambda\tau .$$

Step 3 — the mean and the variance (L14 slide 4's second [DERIVATION GAP])

The slide asserts $\mathbb{E}[N_t]=\lambda t$ and $\operatorname{var}(N_t)=\lambda t$ without argument. Both drop out of the same limit. From G2 the binomial $\tilde N_n$ has

$$\mathbb{E}[\tilde N_n]=np=\lambda t\ \ \text{(exactly, for every }n),\qquad \operatorname{var}(\tilde N_n)=np(1-p)=\lambda t\Big(1-\frac{\lambda t}{n}\Big).$$

The mean needs no limit at all — it is $\lambda t$ for every discretization, which is the sanity check that $\lambda$ deserves the name "rate". The variance carries a correction factor $1-\lambda t/n$ that tends to $1$, so $\operatorname{var}(N_t)=\lambda t$. Equivalently, verify it directly from the PMF; computes/g4_s3.py sums the Poisson PMF numerically and gets $\sum_k P(k,\tau)=1.000000$, mean $=\lambda\tau$ and variance $=\lambda\tau$ to eight decimals for $\lambda\tau=0.5,\,2.5,\,5$ (pois_sum_*, pois_mean_*, pois_var_*).

Interpretation. Mean equals variance is the fingerprint of the Poisson. The standard deviation is $\sqrt{\lambda\tau}$, so the relative spread $\sqrt{\lambda\tau}/(\lambda\tau)=1/\sqrt{\lambda\tau}$ shrinks as the window grows: over a long window the count is close to $\lambda\tau$ in percentage terms, over a short one it is wildly variable. Doubling $\tau$ doubles the mean but only multiplies the standard deviation by $\sqrt2$ — the same $\sqrt{n}$ effect that runs through the whole course.

The window does not have to be an interval

Property (any time set of total length $\tau$; B&T §6.2, Example 6.9). If $S$ is any union of finitely many disjoint intervals with total length $\tau$, the number of arrivals in $S$ is Poisson with parameter $\lambda\tau$.

Why. Suppose $S=[a_1,b_1]\cup[a_2,b_2]$ with the two intervals disjoint, of lengths $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$, $\tau_1+\tau_2=\tau$. By property 2 the two counts $M$ and $N$ are independent, and by property 1 and the theorem above they are Poisson with parameters $\lambda\tau_1$ and $\lambda\tau_2$. Rather than convolve (G3 §4 would let you), use B&T's shifting argument: slide the second interval so that it sits immediately to the right of the first. Time homogeneity says the shifted count $\tilde N$ has the same distribution as $N$, and independence says $\tilde N$ is still independent of $M$; so $M+N$ has the same distribution as $M+\tilde N$. But $M+\tilde N$ counts arrivals in one interval of length $\tau_1+\tau_2=\tau$, hence is Poisson $(\lambda\tau)$. B&T Example 6.9 does exactly this with customers arriving at rate $10$ per minute: $M$ over $9{:}00$–$9{:}10$ is Poisson$(100)$, $N$ over $9{:}30$–$9{:}35$ is Poisson$(50)$, and $M+N$ is Poisson$(150)$ (bank_mu_*) even though the time set is not an interval.

Example 3.2 (L14 slide 5 — the email example, left blank on the slide [UNSOLVED EXAMPLE]). You get email according to a Poisson process at a rate of $\lambda=5$ messages per hour. You check your email every thirty minutes. Find the probability of no new messages and the probability of exactly one new message.

Solution. Step 1 — get the units right. The rate is per hour, so express the window in hours: $\tau=30\text{ min}=\tfrac12$ hour. Then $$\lambda\tau=5\times\tfrac12=2.5 ,$$ the expected number of messages per check.

Step 2 — apply the PMF. With $\mu=\lambda\tau=2.5$, $$\mathbf{P}(\text{no new messages})=P(0,\tfrac12)=\frac{2.5^0e^{-2.5}}{0!}=e^{-2.5}=0.082085,$$ $$\mathbf{P}(\text{one new message})=P(1,\tfrac12)=\frac{2.5^1e^{-2.5}}{1!}=2.5\,e^{-2.5}=0.205212,$$ using $0!=1!=1$ and $2.5^0=1$ (mail_P0, mail_P1).

Recipe (counting questions in a Poisson process).
  1. Identify $\lambda$ and its time unit. Rewrite every duration in that unit.
  2. Add up the total length $\tau$ of the time set you care about — it need not be one interval, only the total matters.
  3. Form $\mu=\lambda\tau$. This single number is the whole distribution: mean, variance, and parameter.
  4. Answer with $P(k,\tau)=\mu^ke^{-\mu}/k!$, summing over $k$ for "at least/at most" questions and using the complement when that is shorter.
  5. If several disjoint windows appear, treat their counts as independent and multiply.

Practice 3.4. Same buses ($\lambda=6$/hour). Given that exactly two buses came in a particular $20$-minute window, what is the probability that exactly one came in each $10$-minute half?
Solution

Let $A$ be the first half and $B$ the second, each of length $\tfrac16$ hour so $\lambda\tau_A=\lambda\tau_B=1$. The counts $N_A,N_B$ are independent Poisson$(1)$ by properties 1–2. $$\mathbf{P}(N_A=1,N_B=1)=\big(1\cdot e^{-1}\big)^2=e^{-2}=0.135335,\qquad \mathbf{P}(N_A+N_B=2)=\frac{2^2e^{-2}}{2}=2e^{-2}=0.270671 .$$ Dividing, the conditional probability is $e^{-2}/(2e^{-2})=\tfrac12=0.5$ exactly (pq_32_cond). So the split is binomial$(2,\tfrac12)$ and the answer is $\binom21(\tfrac12)^2=\tfrac12$. This "condition on the total, positions are uniform" fact is worth remembering; it is the seed of the splitting results in §4.

3.3 The first interarrival time: the exponential

Counts answer "how many by time $t$". The complementary question is "how long until the next one". Let $T$ (equivalently $Y_1$, the time of the first arrival) be the time from an arbitrary starting instant, say $0$, to the first arrival after it. Because $T$ is a continuous random variable, we go through the CDF — the G3 §4 recipe — since a CDF is the probability of an event, and events we can compute with $P(k,\tau)$.

The key event identity. For $t>0$, $$\{T>t\}=\{\text{no arrival in }[0,t]\}=\{N_t=0\},$$ because the first arrival happening after $t$ says exactly that the interval $[0,t]$ is empty, and conversely. This is an equality of events, not an approximation. Hence

$$\mathbf{P}(T>t)=P(0,t)=\frac{(\lambda t)^0e^{-\lambda t}}{0!}=e^{-\lambda t},$$

using the count PMF at $k=0$. Therefore the CDF is

$$F_T(t)=\mathbf{P}(T\le t)=1-\mathbf{P}(T>t)=1-e^{-\lambda t},\qquad t\ge0,$$

and $F_T(t)=0$ for $t<0$ since $T$ is nonnegative. Differentiating (the PDF is the derivative of the CDF, G3 §1):

$$f_T(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\big(1-e^{-\lambda t}\big)=-\big(-\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\big)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad t\ge0,$$

by the chain rule on $e^{-\lambda t}$. So $T$ is exponential with parameter $\lambda$ — L14 slide 6, the $k=1$ case of the Erlang. From G3 §1 we already know $\mathbb{E}[T]=1/\lambda$ and $\operatorname{var}(T)=1/\lambda^2$.

Property (interarrival times; L14 slide 6, B&T §6.2, pp. 311, 315–316). In a Poisson process of rate $\lambda$ the interarrival times $T_1,T_2,T_3,\dots$ (with $T_1=Y_1$ and $T_k=Y_k-Y_{k-1}$ for $k\ge2$) are independent and each is exponential with parameter $\lambda$: $$f_{T_k}(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\ (t\ge0),\qquad \mathbb{E}[T_k]=\frac1\lambda,\qquad \operatorname{var}(T_k)=\frac1{\lambda^2}.$$ Conversely, a process built by drawing i.i.d. exponential gaps and recording arrivals at $T_1$, $T_1+T_2$, $T_1+T_2+T_3,\dots$ is a Poisson process (B&T's "alternative description", p. 316).

Why the gaps are independent and identically distributed. The derivation above used only that the process starts fresh at time $0$. By property 2 the process after any time $t$ is independent of everything before $t$ and, by property 1, obeys the same law — so it is again a Poisson process of rate $\lambda$ started at $t$. Apply the identity $\{T>s\}=\{N=0 \text{ on a window of length } s\}$ to that restarted process and $T_2$ is exponential$(\lambda)$ too, and is determined entirely by the post-$Y_1$ future, hence independent of $T_1$. Repeating gives the whole i.i.d. sequence. (B&T flag in a footnote that restarting at the random time $Y_1$ rather than a deterministic $t$ needs an extra conditioning argument; it is true, and intuitive, and 6.041 uses it freely.)

Memorylessness, and why it is the same statement

Property (memorylessness; L14 slide 6, B&T §6.2, p. 314). For any $t,s\ge0$, $$\mathbf{P}(T>t+s\mid T>t)=\mathbf{P}(T>s)=e^{-\lambda s}.$$ The time still to wait, given that you have already waited $t$, has the same exponential distribution as a fresh wait.

Derivation. The event $\{T>t+s\}$ is contained in $\{T>t\}$ (waiting past $t+s$ entails waiting past $t$), so their intersection is $\{T>t+s\}$ itself. By the definition of conditional probability (G1 §2),

$$\mathbf{P}(T>t+s\mid T>t)=\frac{\mathbf{P}(T>t+s)}{\mathbf{P}(T>t)} =\frac{e^{-\lambda(t+s)}}{e^{-\lambda t}}=e^{-\lambda(t+s)+\lambda t}=e^{-\lambda s},$$

using the law of exponents. With $\lambda=5$, $t=0.4$, $s=0.2$ the two sides are both $0.367879$, agreeing to $0$ in double precision (mem_cond, mem_fresh, mem_devi). Alternatively — and more revealingly — condition on the process rather than the random variable: $\mathbf{P}(T-t>s\mid \text{no arrival in }[0,t])$ concerns only the window $[t,t+s]$, which property 2 declares independent of $[0,t]$; so the answer is $P(0,s)=e^{-\lambda s}$ with no algebra at all.

Interpretation. Memorylessness is the continuous-time twin of the Bernoulli fact from G2 §3 that a geometric random variable "forgets" past failures. Both say the process carries no state: at every instant, the future looks statistically identical to the future at time zero. Practically it means you may restart your clock whenever you like — a licence used constantly in problems ("given that 10 minutes have passed with no bus…" is not extra information about the wait remaining).
Gotcha. Memorylessness says the remaining wait has the same distribution, not that the total wait does. Given $T>t$, the total wait $T$ has mean $t+1/\lambda$, not $1/\lambda$. Also: memorylessness is a statement about a single exponential gap, not about $Y_k$ for $k\ge2$ — the Erlang is emphatically not memoryless (its density is zero at $0$, so a fresh Erlang wait and a partially elapsed one look different).
Example 3.4 (B&T Examples 6.10 and 6.11 — memorylessness at work).

(a) The tennis court. You arrive at a court and must wait for the current pair to finish; their total playing time is exponential. How long will you wait? By memorylessness, the remaining playing time has that same exponential PDF, no matter how long they have already been playing. The information "they started 40 minutes ago" is worthless.

(b) The three tellers. All three tellers are busy, no queue, and all service times are i.i.d. exponential. You start service the moment one finishes. What is the probability that you are the last of the three of you to leave? Focus on the instant your service begins. At that moment your own service time is fresh exponential; by memorylessness the two other customers' remaining times are also exponential with the same parameter. The three remaining times are therefore i.i.d., so by symmetry each is equally likely to be the largest: the answer is $1/3$. Note how the argument never computes an integral — memorylessness converts a messy "partially elapsed" situation into a symmetric one.

3.4 The $k$th arrival time: the Erlang distribution

Let $Y_k$ be the time of the $k$th arrival, measured from time $0$. Since $Y_k=T_1+T_2+\cdots+T_k$ is a sum of $k$ i.i.d. exponential$(\lambda)$ gaps, its mean and variance come free from G2/G3's linearity and independence rules:

$$\mathbb{E}[Y_k]=\sum_{i=1}^k\mathbb{E}[T_i]=k\cdot\frac1\lambda=\frac k\lambda,\qquad \operatorname{var}(Y_k)=\sum_{i=1}^k\operatorname{var}(T_i)=k\cdot\frac1{\lambda^2}=\frac k{\lambda^2},$$

the variance step using independence of the $T_i$ (variances of independent random variables add; without independence we would need the covariance terms of G3 §4). The density needs real work, and L14 slide 6 states it without any [DERIVATION GAP]. Here are both standard derivations.

Derivation 1 — the small-interval argument (B&T §6.2, p. 317)

Fix $y>0$ and a tiny $\delta>0$. By the meaning of a density (G3 §1), $\delta\,f_{Y_k}(y)\approx\mathbf{P}(y\le Y_k\le y+\delta)$, with the approximation exact in the limit $\delta\to0$. Now, ignoring events of probability $o(\delta)$ (two arrivals in the same tiny slot), the $k$th arrival falls in $[y,y+\delta]$ exactly when both of the following happen:

  • $A$: there is an arrival during $[y,y+\delta]$ — probability $\mathbf{P}(A)\approx\lambda\delta$ by property 3;
  • $B$: there are exactly $k-1$ arrivals before time $y$ — probability $\mathbf{P}(B)=P(k-1,y)=\dfrac{(\lambda y)^{k-1}e^{-\lambda y}}{(k-1)!}$ by §3.2.

$A$ concerns $[y,y+\delta]$ and $B$ concerns $[0,y]$, disjoint intervals, so property 2 makes them independent and the probabilities multiply:

$$\delta f_{Y_k}(y)\approx\mathbf{P}(A\cap B)=\mathbf{P}(A)\mathbf{P}(B) \approx\lambda\delta\cdot\frac{(\lambda y)^{k-1}e^{-\lambda y}}{(k-1)!}.$$

Cancel the common factor $\delta$ and let $\delta\to0$:

$$\boxed{\ f_{Y_k}(y)=\frac{\lambda^k y^{k-1}e^{-\lambda y}}{(k-1)!},\qquad y\ge0\ }$$

where $\lambda\cdot(\lambda y)^{k-1}=\lambda^k y^{k-1}$. This is the Erlang density of order $k$, exactly as printed on L14 slide 6.

Derivation 2 — via the CDF, no approximations (B&T §6.2 footnote, p. 317)

The heuristic above is convincing but uses "$\approx$" twice. Here is the airtight version, and it also produces an identity you will use constantly. The key is another event identity: for $y\ge0$,

$$\{Y_k\le y\}=\{\text{at least }k\text{ arrivals in }[0,y]\}=\{N_y\ge k\},$$

since the $k$th arrival has already happened by time $y$ precisely when $[0,y]$ contains $k$ or more arrivals. Therefore

$$F_{Y_k}(y)=\mathbf{P}(N_y\ge k)=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}P(n,y)=1-\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}P(n,y) =1-\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}\frac{(\lambda y)^n e^{-\lambda y}}{n!},$$

the middle step because the $P(n,y)$ over all $n\ge0$ sum to $1$. Differentiate term by term (a finite sum, so no convergence issues). Write $a_n(y)=\dfrac{(\lambda y)^ne^{-\lambda y}}{n!}$. By the product rule,

$$a_n'(y)=\frac{n\lambda(\lambda y)^{n-1}e^{-\lambda y}}{n!} -\frac{\lambda(\lambda y)^{n}e^{-\lambda y}}{n!} =\lambda\frac{(\lambda y)^{n-1}e^{-\lambda y}}{(n-1)!}-\lambda\frac{(\lambda y)^{n}e^{-\lambda y}}{n!} =\lambda a_{n-1}(y)-\lambda a_n(y),$$

where the first term used $\frac{d}{dy}(\lambda y)^n=n\lambda(\lambda y)^{n-1}$ and $n/n!=1/(n-1)!$, the second used $\frac{d}{dy}e^{-\lambda y}=-\lambda e^{-\lambda y}$, and we set $a_{-1}\equiv0$ so the $n=0$ case reads $a_0'=-\lambda a_0$, which is correct. Hence

$$f_{Y_k}(y)=F_{Y_k}'(y)=-\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}a_n'(y) =-\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}\big(\lambda a_{n-1}(y)-\lambda a_n(y)\big) =\lambda\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}\big(a_n(y)-a_{n-1}(y)\big)=\lambda a_{k-1}(y),$$

because the sum telescopes: every $a_n$ for $n\le k-2$ appears once with $+$ and once with $-$, and $a_{-1}=0$. Substituting $a_{k-1}(y)=\frac{(\lambda y)^{k-1}e^{-\lambda y}}{(k-1)!}$ gives $f_{Y_k}(y)=\lambda^k y^{k-1}e^{-\lambda y}/(k-1)!$ — the same Erlang density, with no approximation anywhere. As a numerical check, integrating the order-3 Erlang density with $\lambda=5$ from $0$ to $0.7$ gives $0.679153$, and $1-\sum_{n=0}^{2}P(n,0.7)$ gives $0.679153$; the difference is $0$ to machine precision (erl_cdf_lhs, erl_cdf_rhs, erl_cdf_dev). Each order-$k$ density also integrates to $1.000000$ (erl_norm_k1..3).

Property (the $k$th arrival time; L14 slide 6, B&T §6.2, p. 316). $$f_{Y_k}(y)=\frac{\lambda^ky^{k-1}e^{-\lambda y}}{(k-1)!}\ (y\ge0),\qquad \mathbb{E}[Y_k]=\frac k\lambda,\qquad \operatorname{var}(Y_k)=\frac k{\lambda^2},$$ and the tail identity $$\mathbf{P}(Y_k>y)=\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}P(n,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}\frac{(\lambda y)^ne^{-\lambda y}}{n!}.$$ For $k=1$ this is the exponential; the tail identity then reads $\mathbf{P}(Y_1>y)=e^{-\lambda y}$, as in §3.3.
Fig. 3.4 — The Erlang family, regenerated cleanly from the OCW figure on L14 slide 6 (which plots orders $r=1,2,3$; the shape parameter $r$ there is our $k$). Order $1$ is the exponential, with its maximum at $y=0$; every higher order starts at $0$, peaks at $(k-1)/\lambda$ and flattens as $k$ grows. Right: why — $Y_k$ is the sum of $k$ independent exponential gaps (L14 slide 6; B&T §6.2, p. 316).
Interpretation. Setting $f_{Y_k}'(y)=0$ gives the peak at $y=(k-1)/\lambda$ — one gap short of the mean $k/\lambda$. With $\lambda=5$ the peaks sit at $0,\,0.2,\,0.4$ for $k=1,2,3$ (erl_mode_k1..3), while the means are $0.2,\,0.4,\,0.6$. The density is right-skewed for every $k$, but the skew fades: since $Y_k$ is a sum of $k$ i.i.d. terms, the central limit theorem (G5) says it becomes bell-shaped, with mean $k/\lambda$ and standard deviation $\sqrt k/\lambda$. The relative spread $(\sqrt k/\lambda)/(k/\lambda)=1/\sqrt k$ again shrinks like $1/\sqrt k$.
Example 3.5 (B&T Example 6.12 — the IRS hotline). You call a hotline and are told you are the 56th person in line, excluding the person currently being served. Callers depart according to a Poisson process at rate $\lambda=2$ per minute. How long will you wait on average until your service starts, and what is the probability you wait more than 30 minutes?

Solution. Step 1 — model. Your service starts when the $56$th departure occurs: $55$ people ahead of you finish, and so does the one currently being served. By memorylessness the current customer's remaining service time is exponential$(2)$ just like a fresh one, so your wait is $Y=Y_{56}$, the $56$th arrival time of a Poisson process of rate $\lambda=2$ per minute: Erlang of order $k=56$.

Step 2 — the mean. $\mathbb{E}[Y]=k/\lambda=56/2=28$ minutes. Also $\operatorname{var}(Y)=k/\lambda^2=56/4=14$, so the standard deviation is $\sqrt{14}=3.741657$ minutes (irs_mean, irs_var, irs_sd).

Step 3 — the tail probability. B&T set up the integral $\mathbf{P}(Y\ge30)=\int_{30}^\infty \frac{\lambda^{56}y^{55}e^{-\lambda y}}{55!}dy$ and call it "quite tedious". Use the tail identity instead — no integration at all: $$\mathbf{P}(Y_{56}>30)=\sum_{n=0}^{55}P(n,30)=\sum_{n=0}^{55}\frac{60^ne^{-60}}{n!}=0.285491,$$ since $\lambda\cdot30=2\times30=60$ is the expected number of departures in 30 minutes. (In words: you are still waiting after 30 minutes exactly when fewer than 56 departures have occurred.) Independently evaluated with scipy.stats.erlang.sf the answer agrees to machine precision (irs_tail_exact, irs_tail_scipy, difference $0$).

Step 4 — the normal approximation B&T suggests. $Y$ is a sum of $56$ i.i.d. terms, so the central limit theorem applies: $$z=\frac{30-28}{\sqrt{14}}=\frac{2}{3.741657}=0.534522,\qquad \mathbf{P}(Y>30)\approx 1-\Phi(0.534522)=0.296490 .$$ That is off by $0.010999$ from the exact $0.285491$ (irs_z, irs_tail_clt, irs_clt_err) — about $3.9\%$ relative error, respectable for $k=56$ and a reminder that the Erlang is still slightly skewed at this order.

Example 3.7 (rec14 P1 — mosquito and tick bites; Bernoulli processes and merging). You are visiting the rainforest and your insect repellent has run out. At each second, a mosquito lands on your neck with probability $0.5$; if one lands, with probability $0.2$ it bites you and with probability $0.8$ it never bothers you, independently of other mosquitoes. (a) What is the expected time between successive mosquito bites, and its variance? (b) In addition, at each second a tick lands with probability $0.1$; if one lands it bites with probability $0.7$, independently of everything else. Now what are the expected time and variance between successive bug bites?
Solution

(a) A "mosquito bite at second $i$" needs two independent things: a mosquito lands and it bites. So the per-second bite probability is $$p=\mathbf{P}(\text{lands})\cdot\mathbf{P}(\text{bites}\mid\text{lands})=0.5\times0.2=0.1$$ by the multiplication rule (G1 §2). Different seconds are independent and carry the same $p$, so mosquito bites form a Bernoulli process with parameter $p=0.1$ — this is the splitting row of Fig. 3.5, read on the Bernoulli side: thinning a Bernoulli$(0.5)$ process by keeping each arrival with probability $0.2$ gives Bernoulli$(0.5\times0.2)$.

Let $X$ be the time between successive bites — by memorylessness this is the same as the time until the next bite starting from now. It is geometric with parameter $p$, so from G2 §3 $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac1p=\frac1{0.1}=10\ \text{seconds},\qquad \operatorname{var}(X)=\frac{1-p}{p^2}=\frac{1-0.1}{0.1^2}=\frac{0.9}{0.01}=90 .$$ (r1_EX, r1_varX.) The standard deviation is $\sqrt{90}\approx9.5$ seconds — nearly as large as the mean, the geometric's signature.

(b) Tick bites form their own Bernoulli process, independent of the mosquitoes, with parameter $$q=0.1\times0.7=0.07 .$$ Bug bites are the merge of the two: a bug bite occurs in a second if a mosquito bites, or a tick bites, or both. By the inclusion–exclusion formula of G1 §1, $$r=\mathbf{P}(M\cup T)=\mathbf{P}(M)+\mathbf{P}(T)-\mathbf{P}(M\cap T)=p+q-pq,$$ where $\mathbf{P}(M\cap T)=pq=0.1\times0.07=0.007$ uses the independence of the two processes. Hence $$r=0.1+0.07-0.007=0.163 .$$ Equivalently and more memorably, $r=1-(1-p)(1-q)=1-0.9\times0.93=1-0.837=0.163$ — "not a bug bite" needs both processes to be quiet (r1_r, r1_r_alt, both $0.163$).

Merging preserves the Bernoulli structure (independent seconds, constant probability), so the time $Y$ between successive bug bites is geometric with parameter $r$: $$\mathbb{E}[Y]=\frac1r=\frac1{0.163}=6.134969\ \text{seconds},\qquad \operatorname{var}(Y)=\frac{1-r}{r^2}=\frac{0.837}{0.026569}=31.502879$$ (r1_EY, r1_varY; the official solution's $\approx6.135$ and $\approx31.503$). Sanity check: adding a second source of bites must shorten the wait, and $6.13<10$.

The Poisson twin. Had the problem been in continuous time with rates $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, the merged rate would be exactly $\lambda_1+\lambda_2$ with no $-pq$ correction, and the wait would be exponential with mean $1/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$. The correction term $-pq=-0.007$ is precisely the probability of a "collision" — both a mosquito and a tick biting in the same second — which is the discrete artifact that property 3 of §3.1 legislates away.

Example 3.8 (rec14 P2 — Al's and Bob's coin-flipping trials). Al performs a series of independent trials; on each trial he simultaneously flips three fair coins. (a) Given that Al has just had a trial with 3 tails, what is the probability that both of the next two trials also give 3 tails? (b) Al calls a trial a success whenever all three coins land the same side. (i) Find the PMF of $K$, the number of trials up to but not including the second success. (ii) Find the expectation and variance of $M$, the number of tails occurring before the first success. (c) Bob runs the same kind of experiment but starts with 4 coins, and whenever all his coins land the same side he permanently removes one coin and continues. He stops the third time he removes a coin. Find $\mathbb{E}[N]$, the number of trials in Bob's experiment.
Solution

(a) The trials are independent, so the conditioning information is irrelevant — this is the whole point of the question. For one trial, three fair coins give $2^3=8$ equally likely outcome strings, of which exactly one is TTT, so $\mathbf{P}(\text{3 tails})=\tfrac18$. Two specified consecutive trials both giving 3 tails has probability $$\Big(\tfrac18\Big)^2=\tfrac1{64}=0.015625$$ (r2a) by independence.

(b)(i) First the success probability, which the official solution never computes [SOLUTION GAP filled]: "all three the same" means HHH or TTT, two of the eight equally likely strings, so $$p_s=\mathbf{P}(\text{success})=\frac28=\frac14=0.25 .$$ Let $N$ be the trial on which the second success occurs. From §2.5 (L13 slide 6), $N$ is Pascal of order $2$: $$p_N(n)=\binom{n-1}{1}p_s^2(1-p_s)^{n-2},\qquad n=2,3,\dots$$ (choose which of the first $n-1$ trials held the first success, then demand success on trial $n$ and failure on the other $n-2$). The question asks for $K$ = trials up to but not including the second success, i.e. $K=N-1$. Substituting $n=k+1$: $$\boxed{\,p_K(k)=p_N(k+1)=\binom{k}{1}\Big(\frac14\Big)^2\Big(\frac34\Big)^{k-1} =k\cdot\frac1{16}\Big(\frac34\Big)^{k-1},\qquad k=1,2,3,\dots}$$ matching the recitation's answer. Note $p_K(0)=0$: the second success cannot arrive on trial 1. Checks: summing $p_K(k)$ numerically over $k$ gives $1.000000$, and $\mathbb{E}[K]=\mathbb{E}[N]-1=2/p_s-1=8-1=7$, confirmed numerically as $7.000000$ (r2_pK_check, r2_EK, r2_EK_formula).

(b)(ii) Write $M$ as a random sum (§2): $$M=X_1+X_2+\cdots+X_N,$$ where $X_i$ is the number of tails on the $i$th unsuccessful trial and $N$ is the number of unsuccessful trials before the first success. The $X_i$ are i.i.d. and independent of $N$ — knowing how many failures occurred says nothing about how the tails were arranged inside any one of them.

The distribution of $X$ [SOLUTION GAP filled]. Condition on the trial being a failure. Of the eight strings, the six failures are HHT, HTH, THH (one tail each) and HTT, THT, TTH (two tails each). All six are equally likely, so $X$ is uniform on $\{1,2\}$: $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{1+2}2=\frac32,\qquad \mathbb{E}[X^2]=\frac{1^2+2^2}{2}=\frac52,\qquad \operatorname{var}(X)=\frac52-\Big(\frac32\Big)^2=\frac{10-9}{4}=\frac14 .$$

The distribution of $N$ [SOLUTION GAP filled]. Let $R$ be the trial number of the first success: $R$ is geometric with parameter $p_s=\tfrac14$, so $\mathbb{E}[R]=1/p_s=4$ and $\operatorname{var}(R)=(1-p_s)/p_s^2=(3/4)/(1/16)=12$. The number of failures is $N=R-1$, a shift, so $$\mathbb{E}[N]=4-1=3,\qquad \operatorname{var}(N)=\operatorname{var}(R)=12$$ (subtracting a constant moves a distribution but does not change its spread — G2 §3).

Apply the random-sum formulas of §2. $$\mathbb{E}[M]=\mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[N]=\frac32\times3=\frac92=4.5,$$ $$\operatorname{var}(M)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)+\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2\operatorname{var}(N) =3\times\frac14+\Big(\frac32\Big)^2\times12=\frac34+\frac94\times12=\frac34+27 .$$ [SOLUTION GAP filled — the recitation prints only the final fraction] Putting the two terms over a common denominator, $\frac34+27=\frac{3}{4}+\frac{108}{4}=\frac{111}{4}=27.75$ (r2_EM, r2_varM_term1, r2_varM_term2, r2_varM). Reading: almost all of the variance ($27$ of $27.75$) comes from not knowing how many failures there will be, not from the tails within a failure — the random-sum lesson of §2 in one number.

(c) Bob's experiment splits into three independent phases. Let $X$ be the number of trials until the first coin is removed (played with 4 coins), $Y$ the additional number until the second removal (3 coins), $Z$ the additional number until the third (2 coins); then $N=X+Y+Z$. [SOLUTION GAP filled] With $m$ fair coins there are $2^m$ equally likely strings and exactly two of them — all heads, all tails — are "all the same", so $$\mathbf{P}(\text{all }m\text{ same})=\frac{2}{2^m}=2^{-(m-1)}:\qquad m=4\Rightarrow\tfrac{2}{16}=\tfrac18,\quad m=3\Rightarrow\tfrac{2}{8}=\tfrac14,\quad m=2\Rightarrow\tfrac{2}{4}=\tfrac12 .$$ Each phase is a fresh sequence of independent trials with a constant success probability, so $X$, $Y$, $Z$ are geometric with parameters $\tfrac18$, $\tfrac14$, $\tfrac12$, and by linearity of expectation (no independence needed, though it holds): $$\mathbb{E}[N]=\mathbb{E}[X]+\mathbb{E}[Y]+\mathbb{E}[Z]=8+4+2=14$$ (r2c_EN). Bonus. Because the phases are independent, variances add: $\operatorname{var}(N)=\frac{7/8}{1/64}+\frac{3/4}{1/16}+\frac{1/2}{1/4}=56+12+2=70$ (r2c_varN) — a standard deviation of $\sqrt{70}\approx8.4$ trials, dominated entirely by the slow first phase.

Example 3.9 (rec14 P3 — signing $n$ papers drawn with replacement; the coupon collector). There are $n$ papers in a drawer. You draw a paper, sign it, and put it back. Every paper is equally likely on every draw, independently. What is the expected number of draws until every paper has been signed? (An answer as a summation is acceptable.)
Solution

Set-up. Let $M$ be the total number of draws until all $n$ papers are signed, and let $T_i$ be the number of draws made while exactly $i$ distinct papers are already signed, i.e. the number of draws needed to obtain the $(i+1)$st distinct paper. Then the draws partition into $n$ consecutive phases and $$M=T_0+T_1+\cdots+T_{n-1}.$$ $T_0=1$ always — the very first draw is necessarily new.

Each phase is geometric. Suppose $i$ papers are signed. A draw is a "success" if it lands on one of the $n-i$ unsigned papers, which happens with probability $$p_i=\frac{n-i}{n},$$ the same on every draw of the phase and independent of the others; so $\mathbf{P}(T_i=k)=(1-p_i)^{k-1}p_i$ for $k=1,2,\dots$, a geometric PMF.

Expected phase length [SOLUTION GAP filled]. The recitation jumps straight to $n/(n-i)$. The step is the geometric mean from G2 §3: $\mathbb{E}[T_i]=1/p_i$, hence $$\mathbb{E}[T_i]=\frac1{p_i}=\frac{n}{n-i}.$$

Add up. By linearity of expectation — which needs no independence, and is the reason this decomposition is so much easier than computing the distribution of $M$ — $$\mathbb{E}[M]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}T_i\Big]=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[T_i] =\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{n}{n-i}.$$

Re-index [SOLUTION GAP filled]. Put $k=n-i$. As $i$ runs $0,1,\dots,n-1$, $k$ runs $n,n-1,\dots,1$ — the same set of values in reverse, and a finite sum does not care about order. So $$\boxed{\ \mathbb{E}[M]=n\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac1k=nH_n\ }$$ where $H_n$ is the $n$th harmonic number.

Asymptotics [SOLUTION GAP filled]. Since $1/x$ is decreasing, $\int_1^{n+1}\frac{dx}{x}\le H_n\le 1+\int_1^{n}\frac{dx}{x}$, i.e. $\ln(n+1)\le H_n\le 1+\ln n$: the sum is squeezed between two logarithms, so $H_n=\ln n+O(1)$ and $$\mathbb{E}[M]=nH_n\sim n\ln n .$$ More precisely $H_n=\ln n+\gamma+\frac1{2n}+O(n^{-2})$ with $\gamma=0.577216$ the Euler–Mascheroni constant.

Numbers (r3_*): for $n=10$, $H_{10}=2.928968$ so $\mathbb{E}[M]=29.289683$, against the crude $n\ln n=23.025851$ (ratio $1.272$) and the refined $n(\ln n+\gamma+\frac1{2n})=29.298008$ — three decimal places for free. A Monte-Carlo simulation of $200{,}000$ runs at $n=10$ averages $29.289280$, confirming the formula (r3_sim_n10). The ratio $nH_n/(n\ln n)$ falls to $1.126$ at $n=100$ and $1.084$ at $n=1000$: the $\gamma$ term matters for a long time.

Interpretation. The last phase alone costs $\mathbb{E}[T_{n-1}]=n$ draws — chasing the final missing paper takes as long as all $n$ papers' worth of drawing. That single term is why the answer is $n\ln n$ and not $n$.

Example 3.10 (the coupon collector on a fair die — Example 3.9 with $n=6$). You roll a fair six-sided die repeatedly. Let $M$ be the number of rolls until every one of the six faces has appeared at least once. Find $\mathbb{E}[M]$ and $\operatorname{var}(M)$.

Practice 3.8 (the surprising pair). A fair coin is tossed repeatedly. Find the expected number of tosses until the pattern HTH first appears, and until HHH first appears. Both patterns have probability $\tfrac18$ in any three specified tosses — do they take the same time?
Solution

No, and that is the point. Use the row-3 method: a state records the length of the longest suffix of the tosses so far that is a prefix of the target pattern; $E_s$ is the expected number of further tosses from state $s$; the target state has $E=0$; and each state satisfies $E_s=1+\sum_{s'}\mathbf{P}(s\to s')E_{s'}$, the $1$ counting the toss you are about to make (G2 §3.8).

HHH. States $S_0,S_1,S_2$ = number of trailing heads banked. From $S_0$: heads (probability $\tfrac12$) $\to S_1$, tails $\to S_0$. From $S_1$: heads $\to S_2$, tails $\to S_0$ (the tail wipes out the run). From $S_2$: heads $\to$ done, tails $\to S_0$. So $$E_0=1+\tfrac12E_1+\tfrac12E_0,\qquad E_1=1+\tfrac12E_2+\tfrac12E_0,\qquad E_2=1+\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12E_0 .$$ Substituting the third into the second, $E_1=1+\tfrac12(1+\tfrac12E_0)+\tfrac12E_0=\tfrac32+\tfrac34E_0$; substituting that into the first, $E_0=1+\tfrac12(\tfrac32+\tfrac34E_0)+\tfrac12E_0 =\tfrac74+\tfrac78E_0$, so $\tfrac18E_0=\tfrac74$ and $\mathbb{E}=14$. This is the $k=3$ case of the formula $(1+p+\cdots+p^{k-1})/p^k$ of G2 §3.8 (pq_38_HHH).

HTH. States $S_0$ (nothing useful), $S_1$ (last toss H), $S_2$ (last two tosses HT). From $S_0$: H $\to S_1$, T $\to S_0$. From $S_1$: H $\to S_1$ (a second head is still a single useful trailing H, not a reset), T $\to S_2$. From $S_2$: H $\to$ done, T $\to S_0$. So $$E_0=1+\tfrac12E_1+\tfrac12E_0,\qquad E_1=1+\tfrac12E_1+\tfrac12E_2,\qquad E_2=1+\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12E_0 .$$ From the first, $E_0=2+E_1$. From the third, $E_2=1+\tfrac12E_0=1+\tfrac12(2+E_1)=2+\tfrac12E_1$. Putting that in the second, $E_1=1+\tfrac12E_1+\tfrac12(2+\tfrac12E_1)=2+\tfrac34E_1$, so $\tfrac14E_1=2$, $E_1=8$ and $\mathbb{E}=E_0=2+8=10$ (pq_38_HTH).

Why HTH is faster by $4$ tosses (pq_38_diff). Compare what a failure costs. In HHH, one tail at any point destroys all banked progress and sends you to $S_0$. In HTH, the failure in state $S_1$ is a head — but a head is itself the start of a fresh HTH, so you stay in $S_1$ and lose nothing at all. Patterns that overlap themselves badly (HHH agrees with a shift of itself in two places) wait longer than patterns that recover cheaply. Monte Carlo over $300{,}000$ runs each: $13.988203$ for HHH and $9.991020$ for HTH (pat_sim_HHH, pat_sim_HTH). For the record, HHT and THH both take $8$ (pat_E_HHT, pat_E_THH) — the answer depends on the pattern, not just on its length or its probability.

Practice 3.9 (one fair die, four questions). Roll a fair six-sided die repeatedly. Find the expected number of rolls until (a) the first $6$; (b) the third $6$; (c) two $6$s in a row; (d) all six faces have appeared. Identify which row of the zoo each one is.
Solution

Throughout, $p=\mathbf{P}(\text{a specified face})=\tfrac16$ and $1-p=\tfrac56$.

(a) Row 1, geometric. $\mathbb{E}=1/p=6$ rolls (pq_39_first_six).

(b) Row 2, Pascal of order $3$. The waits for the first, second and third $6$ are i.i.d. geometric, so $\mathbb{E}=k/p=3\times6=18$ rolls (pq_39_three_sixes).

(c) Row 3, first-step analysis. With $\mathbb{E}[N]=(1+p)/p^2$ from G2 §3.8, $$\mathbb{E}[N]=\frac{1+\tfrac16}{(\tfrac16)^2}=\frac{7/6}{1/36}=\frac{7\times36}{6}=42$$ rolls (pq_39_two_in_row). Note $42\ne 2\times6=12$ and $42>18$: with a low-$p$ trial, a broken run is catastrophic, so the in-a-row question is far harder than the total-count question — the same gap as $6$ versus $4$ for the coin, but hugely amplified.

(d) Row 4, coupon collector. $\mathbb{E}[M]=6H_6=14.7$ rolls, from Example 3.10 (pq_39_all_faces).

Practice 3.10 (progress already made). You are rolling the fair die for all six faces. (a) You have seen five distinct faces so far. §3 cheatsheet — the Poisson process.
  • Definition (L14 slide 3). Time homogeneity ($P(k,\tau)$ depends only on $\tau$), independence across disjoint intervals, and small-interval probabilities $P(0,\delta)=1-\lambda\delta+o(\delta)$, $P(1,\delta)=\lambda\delta+o_1(\delta)$, $P(k\ge2,\delta)=o_k(\delta)$. One parameter: the rate $\lambda$, in arrivals per unit time.
  • Counts. $N_\tau\sim$ Poisson$(\lambda\tau)$: $P(k,\tau)=(\lambda\tau)^ke^{-\lambda\tau}/k!$, $\mathbb{E}=\operatorname{var}=\lambda\tau$. Derived as the $n\to\infty$ limit of binomial$(n,\lambda t/n)$ with $np=\lambda t$ pinned. Only the total length of the time set matters.
  • Waits. $T\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)$: $\mathbf{P}(T>t)=e^{-\lambda t}$, $f_T(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}$, $\mathbb{E}=1/\lambda$, $\operatorname{var}=1/\lambda^2$. Interarrival times are i.i.d. exponential and memoryless: $\mathbf{P}(T>t+s\mid T>t)=\mathbf{P}(T>s)$.
  • $k$th arrival. $Y_k\sim\text{Erlang}(k,\lambda)$: $f_{Y_k}(y)=\lambda^ky^{k-1}e^{-\lambda y}/(k-1)!$, $\mathbb{E}=k/\lambda$, $\operatorname{var}=k/\lambda^2$, peak at $(k-1)/\lambda$.
  • The bridge identity. $\{Y_k>y\}=\{N_y\le k-1\}$, so $\mathbf{P}(Y_k>y)=\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}(\lambda y)^ne^{-\lambda y}/n!$ — this converts every waiting-time question into a counting question and kills the Erlang integral.
  • Dictionary. binomial$\to$Poisson, geometric$\to$exponential, Pascal$\to$Erlang, via $n=t/\delta$, $p=\lambda\delta$, $np=\lambda t$, $\delta\to0$. Bernoulli merging is $p_1+p_2-p_1p_2$; Poisson merging is $\lambda_1+\lambda_2$, with the next arrival coming from process 1 with probability $\lambda_1/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$ (developed in §4).
  • The waiting-time zoo (fair coin, $p=\tfrac12$). First success $\to$ geometric, $1/p=2$ (G2 §3). The $k$th success total $\to$ Pascal, $k/p=4$ for $k=2$ (§2.5). A pattern such as HH $\to$ first-step analysis, $(1+p)/p^2=6$ (G2 §3.8; G5 §4 for the general machinery); HTH takes $10$ and HHH takes $14$. All $n$ categories $\to$ coupon collector, $nH_n$; for a fair die $6H_6=14.7$ rolls with $\operatorname{var}=38.99$ (Example 3.10). A broken run discards progress; a banked success never does — that is the whole $6$-versus-$4$ story.
