§0   Orientation: from a model to a conclusion

Sources: L19 slides 1, 5–8 · L20 slide 1 · L21 slides 1–5 · L22 slide 1 · rec20 P1 · rec22 P1 · Bertsekas–Tsitsiklis (cited throughout as B&T) Chapter 5 preamble and §5.1–5.4, Chapter 8 preamble and §8.1 · numbers from computes/g6_s0.py. A bare "§N.k" always means this note's own subsection; every reference to the textbook carries the "B&T" prefix.

Notes G1–G5 all played the same game. Somebody handed you a fully specified probabilistic model — a sample space and probability law (G1), a PMF or a PDF (G2, G3), a Bernoulli or Poisson process (G4), a transition matrix (G5) — and you computed. B&T describe that game exactly: "In probabilistic reasoning, we assume a fully specified probabilistic model that obeys these axioms. We then use mathematical methods to quantify the consequences of this model… every unambiguous question has a unique correct answer" (B&T Chapter 8 preamble). Note G6 is where the game changes, and it changes in two independent ways, each of which gets its own half of the note.

Arc A — the model stays, but $n$ grows. Take $X_1,X_2,\ldots$ independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with common mean $\mu=\mathbb{E}[X_i]$ and common variance $\sigma^2=\operatorname{var}(X_i)$, both assumed finite, and form the sum $S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n$ and the sample mean $M_n=S_n/n$. L19 slide 1 opens with a single question: what happens as $n\to\infty$? The answers — the weak law of large numbers (§1) and the central limit theorem (§2) — are the two theorems that let you convert "I flipped the coin 10,000 times and got 5,113 heads" into a defensible statement about the coin. They are the bridge from probability to statistics.

Arc B — the model itself is uncertain. L21 slide 1 opens the second half of the course with the picture of Reality and a Model jointly producing Data, and asks you to run the arrows backwards: given the data, what was the model? The move that makes this a probability problem again is startling in its simplicity — declare the unknown to be a random variable $\Theta$, give it a prior distribution, and turn the crank of Bayes' rule from G1 §2. That is the Bayesian turn (§3), and squeezing a single number out of the resulting posterior is estimation (§4).

The two arcs meet in §5: the limit theorems of Arc A are what tell you whether the estimate of Arc B can be trusted, and they are also what make the rival classical philosophy — previewed below — work at all.

0.1 Arc A: what happens to the sample mean

Everything in §1 and §2 is a statement about the same three objects, and L19 slide 7 puts them side by side. Let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be i.i.d. with finite mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$. Because expectation is linear (G2 §2) and because independence makes variances add (G2 §4, G3 §4), $$\mathbb{E}[S_n]=\mathbb{E}[X_1]+\cdots+\mathbb{E}[X_n]=n\mu,\qquad \operatorname{var}(S_n)=\operatorname{var}(X_1)+\cdots+\operatorname{var}(X_n)=n\sigma^2 .$$ Here $S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n$ is the sum of the first $n$ observations; there are $n$ identical terms in each sum, which is where the factor $n$ comes from. Now scale. For any constant $a$, $\mathbb{E}[aY]=a\mathbb{E}[Y]$ and $\operatorname{var}(aY)=a^2\operatorname{var}(Y)$ (G2 §3), so with $a=1/n$,

$$\mathbb{E}[M_n]=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[S_n]=\frac{n\mu}{n}=\mu, \qquad \operatorname{var}(M_n)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\operatorname{var}(S_n)=\frac{n\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n},$$

which fills in the two blanks left on L19 slide 5. And with $a=1/\sqrt{n}$ applied to the centered sum, $$\operatorname{var}\!\left(\frac{S_n-n\mu}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{var}(S_n)=\frac{n\sigma^2}{n}=\sigma^2,$$ a variance that does not move with $n$ at all. Three scalings, three completely different fates:

  • $S_n$ — spreads out. Standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{n}\to\infty$. B&T say it plainly: "the distribution of $S_n$ spreads out as $n$ increases, and cannot have a meaningful limit" (B&T Chapter 5 preamble).
  • $M_n=S_n/n$ — collapses. Standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}\to0$, so the whole distribution piles up on the single number $\mu$. Quantifying that collapse is the weak law of large numbers (§1).
  • $(S_n-n\mu)/\sqrt{n}$ — stabilizes. Constant standard deviation $\sigma$: divide by too little and it explodes, by too much and it collapses; $\sqrt{n}$ is the unique exponent in between. What is left over is a shape, and the central limit theorem (§2) says the shape is always the normal bell, whatever the $X_i$ were.

For a concrete case take $X_i$ uniform on $[0,1]$, so $\mu=1/2$ and $\sigma^2=1/12$, i.e. $\sigma=0.288675$. Then $\operatorname{sd}(S_n)$ runs $0.288675,\ 0.577350,\ 1.154701,\ 2.309401,\ 4.618802$ at $n=1,4,16,64,256$ while $\operatorname{sd}(M_n)$ runs $0.288675,\ 0.144338,\ 0.072169,\ 0.036084,\ 0.018042$ over the same $n$, and $\operatorname{sd}\!\big((S_n-n\mu)/\sqrt n\big)=0.288675$ throughout (all from computes/g6_s0.py). The densities themselves are drawn below.

Fig. 0.1 — The three scalings of L19 slide 7, drawn with the exact densities of sums of i.i.d. uniform$[0,1]$ variables (numerical convolution, no simulation; computes/g6_s0_fig.py). Interpretation — why $\sqrt{n}$ is the interesting exponent. Read the middle and right panels together. The middle panel says the error $M_n-\mu$ goes to zero; the right panel says that if you magnify that error by the factor $\sqrt{n}$ — exactly the rate at which it is shrinking — you see a stable picture instead of a blur. So §1 and §2 are not competing theorems about $M_n$; they are the zeroth-order and first-order terms of the same expansion. §1 tells you the sample mean lands on $\mu$; §2 tells you the shape and size of the miss, and it is the miss that a confidence interval is made of.

"Why bother?" (L19 slide 1). The pollster's problem of L19 slide 6 answers it. Let $f$ be the unknown fraction of a population that answers yes, let $X_i=1$ if the $i$th randomly polled person says yes and $X_i=0$ otherwise, so $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=f$ and $\operatorname{var}(X_i)=f(1-f)\le 1/4$, the bound because $\max_{0\le f\le1}f(1-f)=0.25$ at $f=1/2$. The sample fraction is $M_n$, and the specification "95% confidence of at most 1% error" is $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-f|\ge0.01)\le0.05$. §1 turns Chebyshev's inequality on this and gets $n=50{,}000$, at which $1/(4n(0.01)^2)=0.05$ exactly. §2 turns the central limit theorem on the same specification and gets $n=9604$, since $(1.959964\times0.5/0.01)^2=9603.6471$ rounds up to $9604$ — a factor of $5.2062$ fewer people for the same guarantee. Two theorems, one question, one of them five times cheaper: that is the whole argument for reading §2 after §1.

Gotcha — "limit theorem" does not mean "large-$n$ only". Both theorems are statements about $n\to\infty$, but both are used at a fixed finite $n$, and there they behave very differently. At $n=10{,}000$ and $\epsilon=0.01$ with $f=1/2$, the Chebyshev bound of §1 gives $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-f|\ge0.01)\le0.250000$, the CLT approximation of §2 gives $0.045500$, and the exact binomial answer is $0.046586$. Chebyshev is correct — it is a bound and the truth does lie under it — but it is off by a factor of more than five, whereas the CLT is a two-percent-relative approximation with no guarantee at all attached to it. §1 gives you inequalities you can trust and shouldn't believe; §2 gives you approximations you should believe and can't prove. §5 turns that trade-off into a decision rule.

0.2 Arc B: the inference turn

The second half of the note reverses the arrow. B&T open Chapter 8 with the definition — "Statistical inference is the process of extracting information about an unknown variable or an unknown model from available data" — and immediately warn that unlike probability, "it involves an element of art. For any particular problem, there may be several reasonable methods, yielding different answers."

L21 slide 2 sorts the whole field along two independent axes, and it is worth fixing them before any formula appears.

  • What kind of unknown? If the unknown takes one of a few possible values, you are doing hypothesis testing and you aim at a small probability of an incorrect decision. If it ranges over a continuum, you are doing estimation and you aim at a small estimation error. The distinction matters because it decides what "good" means, and therefore which estimator is optimal — §3 covers the first, §4 the second.
  • What is the unknown, philosophically? In the classical view, the unknown $\theta$ is a fixed constant that merely happens to be unknown — "$\theta$: unknown parameter (not a r.v.)", L21 slide 2, whose example is the mass of the electron. You then face not one model but a whole family $p_X(x;\theta)$, one per value of $\theta$; the semicolon is doing real work, marking $\theta$ as a label on the model rather than something conditioned upon. In the Bayesian view, the unknown is a random variable $\Theta$ carrying a prior $p_\Theta(\theta)$ or $f_\Theta(\theta)$ that encodes what you believed before seeing data, and there is exactly one model — a joint distribution of $(\Theta,X)$ — so that ordinary conditioning applies and the vertical bar in $f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$ is a genuine conditioning bar.
Fig. 0.2 — The two block diagrams of L21 slide 2, redrawn. The wiring is identical; only the status of the leftmost symbol differs, and that one difference decides whether you may write a posterior at all. $N$ denotes the noise entering the measurement, $X$ the observation, $\hat\Theta$ the estimator's output. B&T Chapter 8 preamble.
Interpretation — what the Bayesian move buys, and what it costs. B&T put the gain crisply: "The Bayesian approach essentially tries to move the field of statistics back to the realm of probability theory, where every question has a unique answer" (B&T Chapter 8 preamble). Once $\Theta$ is a random variable, you have a joint distribution, and every inference question — a point estimate, an interval, the probability that $\Theta$ exceeds $0.5$ — is an ordinary conditional-probability computation of the sort you have been doing since G1 §2. Nothing new is required mathematically. The cost is the prior: you must write down $f_\Theta$ before you have seen anything, and a classical statistician will object to its arbitrariness. The Bayesian reply, also from the preamble: every statistical procedure contains hidden choices, and "by locating all of the assumptions in one place, in the form of a prior… these assumptions are brought to the surface and are amenable to scrutiny."

Concretely, the machinery of §3 is one line of G1 §2 with new letters on it: posterior $\propto$ prior $\times$ likelihood, normalized. L21 slide 3 writes the four cases (discrete or continuous unknown, discrete or continuous data) and they differ only in whether each factor is a $p$ or an $f$. As a preview of what comes out, take the coin of L21 slide 4: an unknown bias $\Theta$ with a uniform prior on $[0,1]$, and $x=7$ heads observed in $n=10$ tosses. §3 shows that the posterior is a Beta density with parameters $(8,4)$, that the normalizing constant is $p_X(7)=1/(n+1)=0.0909090909$, and that the two standard "single answers" of L21 slide 5 disagree: the MAP estimate (the value maximizing the posterior) is $x/n=0.700000$, while the LMS estimate (the posterior mean $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=7]$, §4) is $8/12=0.666667$. The posterior standard deviation is $0.130744$, down from the prior's $\sqrt{1/12}=0.288675$ — a variance reduction of $4.8750\times$ from ten tosses. All values from computes/g6_s0.py; §3 and §4 derive them.

Gotcha — the posterior is the answer; a single number is a lossy summary. L21 slide 5 ends with the warning "Single answers can be misleading!", and the coin shows why. Reporting $0.700000$ or $0.666667$ hides that the posterior still assigns $\mathbf{P}(\Theta>0.5\mid X=7)=0.886719$ — so $\mathbf{P}(\Theta\le0.5\mid X=7)=0.113281$, an $11\%$ chance the coin is actually biased against heads — and that the central $95\%$ of the posterior stretches from $0.390257$ to $0.890737$. Note also that MAP and LMS are answers to different questions (most probable value versus mean-square-optimal value) and generally differ; here by $0.033333$ at $n=10$, shrinking to $0.003922$ at $n=100$ with the same proportion $x/n=0.7$, where the posterior standard deviation has fallen to $0.045320$. The gap between estimators is a small-data phenomenon; §4 says exactly when it matters.
Recipe — which arc am I in? (orientation only; §5 gives the full decision rules).
  1. Is there an unknown constant to be learned from data, with a prior supplied or assumed? If yes, you are in Arc B: write the prior and the likelihood, form the posterior by Bayes' rule (§3), and only then ask for a point estimate (§3 for MAP, §4 for LMS and linear LMS).
  2. Is the model fully specified, with the question being about a sum or an average of many i.i.d. terms? If yes, you are in Arc A. Then ask what the question demands: a guarantee (an inequality valid for every $n$ and every distribution with the given moments) sends you to §1; a number (an approximate probability, a sample size, a tail) sends you to §2.
  3. Both at once? Common in practice and on exams: estimate first, then use a limit theorem to say how far off the estimate can be. rec20 P1 is exactly this shape — pick an estimator for a Bernoulli parameter, then bound its error and solve for the sample size.

0.3 The map, and the roadmap

Fig. 0.3 — The map of note G6. Left column: one model, growing $n$ (L19, L20). Right column: fixed data, unknown model (L21, L22). The horizontal link is the point of §5 — a Bayesian estimate is only as good as the concentration result that bounds its error.
  • §1 — Inequalities and the weak law of large numbers (L19 slides 2–6, rec20, B&T §5.1–5.3). Markov's inequality, Chebyshev's inequality and its $k\sigma$ form, the meaning of convergence in probability and how it differs from the deterministic limit of L19 slide 3, the WLLN proved by applying Chebyshev to $M_n$, convergence in mean square, and the pollster's $n=50{,}000$. Hook: these bounds ask for nothing but a mean and a variance, which is precisely why they are so weak and so widely applicable.
  • §2 — The central limit theorem (L20, rec21, B&T §5.4). The standardized sum $Z_n=(S_n-n\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt n)$, the theorem $\mathbf{P}(Z_n\le c)\to\Phi(c)$, how to run a normal approximation in practice, the $1/2$ continuity correction for integer-valued sums, the de Moivre–Laplace form for binomials, and the same pollster's $n=9604$. Hook: the limiting shape does not remember the distribution you started from — only $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ survive.
  • §3 — The Bayesian framework: prior, posterior, MAP (L21 slides 1–5, rec22 posterior and MAP parts, B&T §8.1–8.2). All four versions of the Bayes formula, the coin with a uniform prior worked in full, the Romeo-and-Juliet posterior of rec22 P1, hypothesis testing and the MAP rule, and why MAP minimizes the probability of error. Hook: nothing here is new probability; what is new is deciding to call the unknown a random variable.
  • §4 — LMS and linear LMS estimation (L22, rec22 estimation parts, B&T §8.3–8.4). The proof that $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ minimizes the mean squared error over all estimators, the conditional mean squared error, why the optimal estimator can be intractable, and the linear estimator $\hat\Theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+\frac{\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)}{\operatorname{var}(X)}(X-\mathbb{E}[X])$ that needs only means, variances and one covariance. Hook: linear LMS is the estimation counterpart of Chebyshev — deliberately ignore most of the model in exchange for a formula you can actually evaluate.
  • §5 — Synthesis and bridge (all four lectures, rec20–rec22). Choosing among Markov, Chebyshev, and the CLT; choosing among MAP, LMS, and linear LMS; the standard failure modes of each; and the bridge to the classical inference of the remaining lectures, where the prior is dropped and the limit theorems of §1–§2 must do all the work alone. Hook: by the end, "which tool" is the only question left, because each individual computation is short.

Behind you: G1 §2 (Bayes' rule and the total probability theorem — §3 is literally these with new notation), G2 §2–§4 (expectation, variance, and the variance of a sum of independent variables), G3 §2–§3 (conditional densities and the continuous Bayes variations), G3 §4 (covariance and correlation, which reappear as the only inputs to linear LMS), and G4 §1 (iterated expectations, the engine of the LMS optimality proof). Ahead: classical statistical inference, which keeps the estimation questions of §3–§4 but refuses the prior of Fig. 0.2.
