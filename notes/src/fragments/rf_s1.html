Everything in this section already exists somewhere in the seven notes. That is exactly the problem it solves. The distribution facts are scattered: the audit behind §0 counts seven fragments carrying a table of distribution facts (g2_s1, g2_s5, g3_s5, g5_s5, g6_s2, g6_s3, g7_s0 — rf_s0.json, key fragments_with_distribution_tables), of which four are the full zoos a reader would actually look a family up in: the discrete zoo at the end of G2 (G2 §5.2), the continuous zoo at the end of G3, the Bernoulli-versus-Poisson correspondence at the end of G4, and the hypergeometric, stranded in the counting note at G1 §4. The other three are local, single-purpose tables. Each of the four has its own column headings, its own notation for the parameters, and its own idea of what belongs in a table. When you are mid-problem and want to know whether the Pascal variance carries a factor $k$ or a factor $k^2$, four tables in four files is three tables too many. So this section reprints all of it once, with a single column scheme, in first-appearance order of usefulness, with a pointer back to the note that derives each row. Nothing here is new mathematics; if a row and its home note ever disagree, the home note wins and this page is wrong.

Read the tables in two directions. Left to right is lookup: you know the family, you want the variance. Right to left is modeling, and it is the harder and more valuable direction: you have a sentence in a problem statement, and the column headed "the story" tells you which row that sentence is describing. Most errors in this course are made before any algebra begins, by picking the wrong row — counting successes with a Pascal, or calling a without-replacement draw binomial. The story column is where you check yourself.

1.1 The discrete distributions

Notation, fixed once for the whole table. $p\in(0,1]$ is the success probability of a single trial and $q=1-p$ its failure probability; $n$ is a fixed number of trials; $k$ is a count of successes, except in the Pascal row where $k$ is the fixed order and $t$ is the (random) trial index; $a\le b$ are integers bounding a uniform range, all integers between them included; $\lambda$ is a Poisson arrival rate and $\tau$ the length of the observation window, so that $\lambda\tau$ — never $\lambda$ alone — is the Poisson parameter; and in the hypergeometric row the urn holds $n$ balls of which $m$ are red, $k$ are drawn without replacement, and $i$ of the drawn balls are red. In the multivariate row at the foot of the table, $r$ is the number of categories, $\theta_k$ the probability that one trial lands in category $k$ (so $\sum_{k=1}^r\theta_k=1$), and $N_k$ the count of trials that did.

A $400{,}000$-draw Monte-Carlo gives $\mathbb{E}[N_1]\approx5.002298$, $\operatorname{var}(N_1)\approx2.502057$ and $\operatorname{cov}(N_1,N_2)\approx-1.496960$.

Interpretation — the finite-population correction. The last row is worth one extra sentence, because it is the only row whose variance is not a formula you could guess. Its mean $km/n$ is exactly the binomial mean with $p=m/n$: linearity of expectation does not care that the draws are dependent, so drawing with or without replacement gives the same expected number of red balls. The variance does care. The with-replacement (binomial) variance would be $k\frac{m}{n}(1-\frac{m}{n})=1.2$ for the instance above; the hypergeometric shaves it by the factor $\frac{n-k}{n-1}=\frac{15}{19}=0.789474$, giving $0.947368$. The shave is the whole content of "without replacement": each ball you remove tells you something about what is left, so the total is less variable than free sampling. The factor is $1$ when $k=1$ (one draw cannot be affected by dependence) and $0$ when $k=n$ (drawing every ball leaves nothing random at all). Derived in G1 §4.
Interpretation — why every multinomial covariance is negative. There are only $n$ trials to go around. A trial that lands in category $i$ is a trial that did not land in category $j$, so learning that $N_i$ came out high is evidence that $N_j$ came out low: the categories compete for a fixed budget. The formula $\operatorname{cov}(N_i,N_j)=-n\theta_i\theta_j$ puts a number on that competition, and the constraint $\sum_kN_k=n$ audits it. Since a constant has zero variance, the variance-of-a-sum row of §1.4 block B must give $$0=\operatorname{var}\Big(\sum_kN_k\Big)=\sum_kn\theta_k(1-\theta_k)+\sum_{i\ne j}(-n\theta_i\theta_j),$$ and it does: for $(10;0.5,0.3,0.2)$ the variances sum to $2.5+2.1+1.6=6.2$ and the six ordered cross terms to $2(-1.5-1.0-0.6)=-6.2$, total $0$ to machine precision (F_mn_var_of_total). That identity is the fastest check that you have written the covariance with the right sign. The correlations themselves are modest — $\rho_{12}=-0.654654$, $\rho_{13}=-0.5$, $\rho_{23}=-0.327327$ — and shrink toward $0$ as $r$ grows and each category's share falls.

1.2 The continuous distributions

Notation. $a<b$ bound a uniform range; $\lambda>0$ is a rate, with units of $1/\text{time}$, so $1/\lambda$ is a mean waiting time; $k$ is a positive integer order; $\mu$ and $\sigma>0$ are a normal's mean and standard deviation, and $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF; $\alpha,\beta>0$ are beta shape parameters with normalizing constant $B(\alpha,\beta)=\int_0^1q^{\alpha-1}(1-q)^{\beta-1}dq$. Recall throughout that a PDF is a density, not a probability: $f_X(x)$ may exceed $1$, and only $\int_a^bf_X$ is a probability (G3 §1).

Interpretation — the Gamma family, of which Erlang is the integer slice. The Erlang density $\lambda^ky^{k-1}e^{-\lambda y}/(k-1)!$ only makes sense for integer $k$, because $(k-1)!$ does. Replacing $(k-1)!$ by the gamma function $\Gamma(a)=\int_0^\infty u^{a-1}e^{-u}du$, which satisfies $\Gamma(k)=(k-1)!$ at integers, extends it to any real shape $a>0$: $$f(y)=\frac{\lambda^{a}y^{\,a-1}e^{-\lambda y}}{\Gamma(a)},\qquad y\ge0,\qquad \mathbb{E}[Y]=\frac{a}{\lambda},\qquad \operatorname{var}(Y)=\frac{a}{\lambda^{2}} .$$ The mean and variance formulas are the same two expressions with $k$ replaced by $a$ — checked in the computes at $a=2.5$, $\lambda=2$: mean $1.25$, variance $0.625$, and at $a=3$ it reproduces the Erlang mean $1.5$ exactly. This general Gamma is not used anywhere in 6.041 (every arrival-time question here asks for the $k$th arrival, and $k$ is a count), but you will meet it immediately in any Bayesian course, where it is the conjugate prior for a Poisson rate, and it is what the $\chi^2_\nu$ row above secretly is — $\chi^2_\nu=\Gamma(\nu/2,\,1/2)$, which is exactly where the half-integer shapes earn their keep.
Practice 1.1 (reading the story column backwards). For each sentence, name the distribution and write its mean, using only the tables above. (a) "A machine produces defective parts independently with probability $0.02$; a batch of $500$ is inspected — how many defectives?" (b) "…how many parts must be inspected to find the first defective?" (c) "…to find the third defective?" (d) "A crate holds $500$ parts of which $10$ are defective; $20$ are drawn without replacement — how many defectives among them?" (e) "Defects appear along a wire at a rate of $0.02$ per metre — how many in $500$ metres?" (f) "…how far to the third defect?"
Solution

(a) Binomial$(500,0.02)$ — a fixed number of independent identical trials, count the successes. Mean $np=500\times0.02=10$.

(b) Geometric$(0.02)$ — wait for the first success, unbounded. Mean $1/p=50$ parts. Note this is the trials-until convention of §1.3; a source using failures-before would say $49$.

(c) Pascal of order $3$ at $p=0.02$ — the trial index of the third success. Mean $k/p=3/0.02=150$ parts, variance $k(1-p)/p^2=3(0.98)/0.0004=7350$.

(d) Hypergeometric with $n=500$, $m=10$, $k=20$ — without replacement, so not binomial. Mean $km/n=20\times10/500=0.4$, the same as the binomial with $p=10/500$; the variance is smaller by the factor $(n-k)/(n-1)=480/499=0.961924$.

(e) Poisson with parameter $\lambda\tau=0.02\times500=10$ — a count in a continuous window, mean and variance both $10$. Compare (a): same mean, but the variance is $10$ here against $np(1-p)=9.8$ there, because the binomial's $(1-p)$ factor has been killed by the limit.

(f) Erlang of order $3$ at $\lambda=0.02$ — the position of the third arrival in continuous space. Mean $k/\lambda=150$ metres, variance $k/\lambda^2=7500$. Set (c) beside (f): the same story told on the two clocks of block D, with the Pascal variance $7350$ approaching the Erlang $7500$ as the slot shrinks.

1.3 The one convention clash: which "geometric"?

Gotcha — two incompatible definitions share one name. This course, following B&T §2.2, counts trials up to and including the first success. So $X\in\{1,2,\ldots\}$, $p_X(k)=(1-p)^{k-1}p$, and $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac1p .$$ A great many other sources — including the Chen–Blitzstein Probability Cheatsheet, the standard companion to Harvard's Stat 110 — define the geometric as the number of failures before the first success. That is $Y=X-1\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ with $p_Y(j)=(1-p)^jp$ and $$\mathbb{E}[Y]=\frac1p-1=\frac{1-p}{p} .$$ (That cheatsheet reserves the separate name "First Success" for our $X$.) These are the same distribution shifted by one trial, so:
  • The means differ by exactly $1$, always. At $p=0.3$: $3.333333$ against $2.333333$. At $p=0.2$: $5$ against $4$. At $p=0.5$: $2$ against $1$.
  • The variances are identical, $(1-p)/p^2$, because adding or subtracting a constant does not change a variance (G2 §3). At $p=0.3$ both are $7.777778$; at $p=0.2$ both are $20$.
  • Every downstream formula inherits the shift. The Pascal/negative-binomial has the same split — "trial of the $k$th success", mean $k/p$ as in §1.1, versus "failures before the $k$th success", mean $k(1-p)/p$.
The software follows this course, not the cheatsheet. scipy.stats.geom has $\texttt{pmf}(0,p)=0$, $\texttt{pmf}(1,0.3)=0.3$ and mean $1/p=3.333333$; to get the failures-before version you must shift deliberately, scipy.stats.geom(p, loc=-1) (mean $2.333333$) or scipy.stats.nbinom(1, p) (also $2.333333$). All five values verified in computes/rf_s1.py block C. Whenever you import a geometric or negative-binomial formula from outside these notes, read its support first: if $0$ is in the support, it is the failures convention and its mean is one less than ours. This matches the gotcha in G2 §3, where the convention is first flagged.
Practice 1.2 (spotting the convention in a quoted formula). A reference you are reading defines its geometric by $p_Y(j)=(1-p)^jp$ for $j=0,1,2,\ldots$ and quotes "mean $q/p$, variance $q/p^2$", with $q=1-p$. (i) Which convention is that, and what is the one-second test? (ii) Your problem asks for the expected number of tosses of a fair coin up to and including the first head — what do you report? (iii) The same reference gives the negative binomial mean as $kq/p$; you need the trial index of the third success at $p=0.2$ — what is it? (iv) Do you have to adjust the quoted variance as well?
Solution

(i) Failures-before-the-first-success, i.e. our $Y=X-1$. Two tests, either instantly decisive: is $0$ in the support? — it is, and our $X$ starts at $1$; or set $p=1$ — their mean is $q/p=0$, while a sure success still costs one trial, so ours must be $1$. Whenever $0$ is in the support the quoted mean is one less than ours (§1.3).

(ii) $\mathbb{E}[X]=1/p=2$ tosses. Their formula returns $q/p=1$ — the number of failures before the head — so add the successful toss back: $1+1=2$ (E_p12conv_trials, E_p12conv_failures).

(iii) Add $k$, not $1$: the Pascal of order $k$ counts $k$ successes as well as the failures, so $\mathbb{E}[T_k]=k/p=3/0.2=15$ trials, against their $kq/p=3(0.8)/0.2=12$ failures; $12+3=15$ ✓ (E_p12conv_pascal_trials, E_p12conv_pascal_failures). This is the shift of §1.3 applied $k$ times, once per geometric gap.

(iv) No. $Y=X-1$ is a shift, and a shift never changes a variance (affine-map row, block B), so both conventions give $(1-p)/p^2$ — at $p=0.2$, $20$ (E_p12conv_var_both). Only means need repair, which is exactly why the mistake survives a variance check.

1.4 The master formula sheet

The whole course in one table, grouped by theme, each line pointing at the note section that derives it. The grouping is deliberate: within a block the rows are ordered so that each is a consequence of, or a refinement of, the ones above it. Where a row needs a hypothesis to be true — independence, most often — the hypothesis is stated in the row and not left implied, because silently assuming independence is the single most expensive error in this material.

Bayes' rule$\mathbf{P}(A_i\mid B)=\dfrac{\mathbf{P}(A_i)\mathbf{P}(B\mid A_i)}{\sum_j\mathbf{P}(A_j)\mathbf{P}(B\mid A_j)}$Read as posterior $\propto$ prior $\times$ likelihood; the denominator is only the normalizer. Reverses the direction of conditioning.G1 §2; B&T §1.4
Bayes in odds form$\dfrac{\mathbf{P}(A\mid B)}{\mathbf{P}(A^c\mid B)}=\dfrac{\mathbf{P}(A)}{\mathbf{P}(A^c)}\times\dfrac{\mathbf{P}(B\mid A)}{\mathbf{P}(B\mid A^c)}$ — posterior odds $=$ prior odds $\times$ likelihood ratioTwo hypotheses. The normalizer cancels, so no denominator is ever computed; recover a probability by $\text{odds}/(1+\text{odds})$. Radar instance: $0.052632\times9.9=0.521053$, hence $0.342561$.G1 §2; also G1 §3
Independence$\mathbf{P}(A\cap B)=\mathbf{P}(A)\mathbf{P}(B)$; for a collection, the product identity for every subset of distinct indicesA modeling claim, never a default. Pairwise independence does not imply independence of a collection.G1 §3; B&T §1.5
Conditional independence$\mathbf{P}(A\cap B\mid C)=\mathbf{P}(A\mid C)\mathbf{P}(B\mid C)$Neither implies nor is implied by unconditional independence — the two-coin example is dependent unconditionally and independent given the coin.G1 §3; B&T §1.5
Uniform lawsDiscrete: $\mathbf{P}(A)=|A|/|\Omega|$. Continuous: $\mathbf{P}(A)=\operatorname{area}(A)/\operatorname{area}(\Omega)$Triggered by "fair", "at random", "well shuffled". Then the whole problem is counting — and numerator and denominator must count the same kind of object.G1 §1, G1 §4; B&T §1.2, §1.6
Counting$n^k$ (ordered, repeats); $\dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}$ ($k$-permutations); $\dbinom nk$ (combinations); $\dfrac{n!}{n_1!\cdots n_r!}$ (partitions)Decide two things first: does order matter, are repeats allowed.G1 §4; B&T §1.6
B. Expectation and variance
Definition$\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_xx\,p_X(x)$   (continuous: $\int x f_X(x)\,dx$)The center of gravity of the distribution. It need not be a value $X$ can take.G2 §2; G3 §1; B&T §2.4, §3.1
Expected-value rule (LOTUS)$\mathbb{E}[g(X)]=\sum_xg(x)p_X(x)$; two variables: $\mathbb{E}[g(X,Y)]=\sum_x\sum_yg(x,y)p_{X,Y}(x,y)$You never need the distribution of $g(X)$. And $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]\ne g(\mathbb{E}[X])$ in general — the standing trap.G2 §2, G2 §4; G3 §1; B&T §2.4, §3.1
Linearity$\mathbb{E}[\alpha X+\beta]=\alpha\mathbb{E}[X]+\beta$;   $\mathbb{E}[X_1+\cdots+X_n]=\sum_i\mathbb{E}[X_i]$No independence needed — the most useful fact in the course, and the engine of the indicator method.G2 §4; B&T §2.7
Indicator method$X=\sum_iX_i$ with $X_i\in\{0,1\}\Rightarrow\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_i\mathbf{P}(X_i=1)$Turns a hard count into a list of easy probabilities. Delivers the binomial mean, the hat problem, the hypergeometric mean.G2 §4; B&T §2.7
Products$\mathbb{E}[XY]=\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$, and $\mathbb{E}[g(X)h(Y)]=\mathbb{E}[g(X)]\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]$Independence required. This is the row people use when they should be using linearity.G2 §4; B&T §2.7
Variance$\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2\big]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\ \ge0$;   $\sigma_X=\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(X)}$The second form is how you compute; the first is what it means. A negative answer means arithmetic error.G2 §3; B&T §2.4
Affine map$\operatorname{var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{var}(X)$, so $\sigma_{aX+b}=|a|\,\sigma_X$Shifts do nothing; scalings act on the square. $b$ never appears.G2 §3; B&T §2.4
Variance of a sum$\operatorname{var}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{var}(X_i)+\sum_{(i,j)\,:\,i\ne j}\operatorname{cov}(X_i,X_j)$Always true. The second sum runs over ordered pairs, so each unordered pair appears twice — that is the familiar $2\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)$ for $n=2$. It vanishes under independence, and only then.G3 §4; B&T §4.2
Covariance$\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y])\big]=\mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$Bilinear: $\operatorname{cov}(X,A+B)=\operatorname{cov}(X,A)+\operatorname{cov}(X,B)$, and $\operatorname{cov}(X,X)=\operatorname{var}(X)$. Independence $\Rightarrow$ zero covariance; the converse is false.G3 §4; B&T §4.2
Correlation coefficient$\rho=\dfrac{\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}$, and $-1\le\rho\le1$Dimensionless, unchanged by increasing linear rescaling. $|\rho|=1$ exactly when $X$ and $Y$ are linearly related with probability $1$. The bound is Cauchy–Schwarz; read $\rho$ as "how close to a straight line", never "how related".G3 §4; B&T §4.2
C. Conditioning
Two objects, two types$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$ is a number, one per $y$; $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ is a random variable, the function $g(Y)$ with $g(y)=\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$Almost every G4 error is a violation of this distinction. Same split for $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)$ versus $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)$.G4 §1; B&T §4.3
Total expectation$\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_i\mathbf{P}(A_i)\,\mathbb{E}[X\mid A_i]$$\{A_i\}$ a partition. The tool that cracks the geometric mean in one line.G2 §3; B&T §2.6
Law of iterated expectations$\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]=\mathbb{E}[X]$The same statement with a random conditioning variable instead of a partition.G4 §1; B&T §4.3
Law of total variance$\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]+\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)$"Average variability within" plus "variability between". Both terms are $\ge0$, so $\operatorname{var}(X)$ is at least either one — dropping the second term is the classic understatement of risk.G4 §1; B&T §4.3
Random sum, conditional$Y=X_1+\cdots+X_N$:   $\mathbb{E}[Y\mid N]=N\,\mathbb{E}[X]$,   $\operatorname{var}(Y\mid N)=N\operatorname{var}(X)$$X_i$ i.i.d. and independent of the count $N$. Both are random variables — functions of $N$.G4 §2; B&T §4.5
Random sum, unconditional$\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[N]\,\mathbb{E}[X]$;   $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)+\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2\operatorname{var}(N)$Same hypotheses. The mean needs two numbers, the variance needs four: $Y$ is random both because each term is and because how many terms there are is.G4 §2; B&T §4.5
Joint CDF and marginals$F_{X,Y}(x,y)=\mathbf{P}(X\le x,\ Y\le y)$;   $f_{X,Y}=\dfrac{\partial^2F_{X,Y}}{\partial x\,\partial y}$;   $F_X(x)=\lim_{y\to\infty}F_{X,Y}(x,y)$,   $f_X(x)=\int f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dy$ (discrete: $p_X(x)=\sum_yp_{X,Y}(x,y)$)The CDF needs no density — it is defined for discrete, continuous and mixed pairs alike, which is what makes it the tool of choice for a $\min$ or a $\max$. Independence has a CDF form too: $F_{X,Y}=F_XF_Y$ for all $x,y$.G3 §2 (§2.1 the joint CDF, §2.2 the marginals); B&T §3.4
Derived distributionsDiscrete: $p_Y(y)=\sum_{x:g(x)=y}p_X(x)$. Continuous: get $F_Y(y)=\mathbf{P}(g(X)\le y)$ first, then differentiate, $f_Y=dF_Y/dy$Never differentiate a PDF directly through a nonlinear $g$. Linear special case: $f_Y(y)=\frac{1}{|a|}f_X\big(\frac{y-b}{a}\big)$ for $Y=aX+b$.G3 §4; B&T §4.1
Convolution$W=X+Y$ independent:   $p_W(w)=\sum_xp_X(x)p_Y(w-x)$;   $f_W(w)=\int f_X(x)f_Y(w-x)\,dx$Independence required. Closure facts that follow: two independent normals sum to a normal; $k$ independent Exp$(\lambda)$ sum to Erlang$(k,\lambda)$; and, in the discrete sum, Binomial$(n,p)+$Binomial$(m,p)=$Binomial$(n+m,p)$ — the same $p$ in both, since the sum is then just $n+m$ trials of one Bernoulli process. Convolving Binomial$(7,0.3)$ with Binomial$(5,0.3)$ reproduces Binomial$(12,0.3)$ to $1.4\times10^{-16}$ (F_binom_closure_maxdev); with mismatched $p=0.3$ and $0.6$ the sum has mean $5.1$ but variance $2.67$, not the $2.9325$ a binomial with that mean would have, so it is not binomial at all.G3 §4; B&T §4.1
D. Arrival processes
Bernoulli processCount in $n$ trials: Binomial$(n,p)$, mean $np$, variance $np(1-p)$. Interarrival: geometric$(p)$, mean $1/p$. Time of the $k$th success: Pascal of order $k$, mean $k/p$, variance $k(1-p)/p^2$Independent trials, same $p$ every trial. Memoryless: given no success in the first $m$ trials, the remaining wait is again geometric$(p)$.G4 §2; B&T §6.1
Poisson processCount in a window of length $\tau$: Poisson$(\lambda\tau)$, mean $=$ variance $=\lambda\tau$. Interarrival: Exp$(\lambda)$, mean $1/\lambda$. Time of the $k$th arrival: Erlang$(k,\lambda)$, mean $k/\lambda$, variance $k/\lambda^2$Time homogeneity, independence over disjoint intervals, and $\mathbf{P}(\text{arrival in }\delta)\approx\lambda\delta$ with $\mathbf{P}(\ge2)\approx0$. Memoryless in continuous time.G4 §3; B&T §6.2
The bridge between themSlots of width $\delta$, $p=\lambda\delta$, $n=\tau/\delta$, so $np=\lambda\tau$ is pinned as $\delta\to0$: binomial $\to$ Poisson, geometric $\to$ exponential, Pascal $\to$ Erlang$p$ is dimensionless, $\lambda$ has units $1/\text{time}$; only the product $\lambda\delta$ converts. A slot-count answer must be multiplied by $\delta$ to become a time.G4 §2, G4 §3; B&T §6.2
MergingPoisson: rates add, $\lambda=\lambda_1+\lambda_2$, and the next arrival came from stream $1$ with probability $\lambda_1/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$. Bernoulli: $p=1-(1-p_1)(1-p_2)=p_1+p_2-p_1p_2$Independent streams. The Bernoulli form is not $p_1+p_2$ because simultaneous arrivals in one slot count once.G4 §4 (Poisson), G4 §2 (Bernoulli); B&T §6.1–§6.2
SplittingRoute each arrival by an independent coin of bias $q$: two Poisson processes of rates $q\lambda$ and $(1-q)\lambda$, and they are independent of each otherThe independence clause is special to the Poisson case; the two split Bernoulli processes are not independent of each other.G4 §4 (Poisson), G4 §2 (Bernoulli); B&T §6.2
Competing exponentialsIndependent $T_i\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda_i)$: $\min_iT_i\sim\text{Exp}\big(\sum_i\lambda_i\big)$, and $\mathbf{P}(\text{$i$ wins})=\lambda_i/\sum_j\lambda_j$, independent of the winning time"Who arrives first?" is settled by one clock at the summed rate; the winner is chosen by rate-weighting. The independence of winner and time is what makes sequential problems collapse.G4 §4; B&T §6.2, Example 6.15
Random incidenceArriving at an arbitrary instant, the interval you land in has $f_L(\ell)=\dfrac{\ell f_X(\ell)}{\mathbb{E}[X]}$ (discrete: $p_L(\ell)=\ell p_X(\ell)/\mathbb{E}[X]$); for a Poisson process $L\sim\text{Erlang}(2,\lambda)$ with $\mathbb{E}[L]=2/\lambda$, twice the typical gapNeeds $\mathbb{E}[X]<\infty$. You are more likely to land in a long interval simply because it is long. Given $L=\ell$ your position in it is uniform, so the forward wait has mean $\mathbb{E}[X^2]/(2\mathbb{E}[X])\ge\mathbb{E}[X]/2$.G4 §4; B&T §6.2
E. Markov chains
Markov property$\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i,\ \text{all past})=p_{ij}$, with $\sum_jp_{ij}=1$ for every $i$The state must carry everything about the past that matters. If it does not, enlarge the state.G5 §1; B&T §7.1
Chapman–Kolmogorov$r_{ij}(n)=\sum_kr_{ik}(n-1)\,p_{kj}$, $r_{ij}(0)=\delta_{ij}$; in matrix form $R(n)=P^n$Probabilities of specific paths multiply: $\mathbf{P}(X_1=a,X_2=b\mid X_0=i)=p_{ia}p_{ab}$.G5 §1; B&T §7.2
ClassificationA state is recurrent if it is reachable from everything it reaches, transient otherwise; a class is periodic if its states can be grouped into $d>1$ cyclically visited subsetsThe limit $\lim_nr_{ij}(n)$ exists only for a single aperiodic recurrent class. Periodicity kills the limit but not the balance equations.G5 §2; B&T §7.2
Steady stateBalance $\pi_j=\sum_k\pi_kp_{kj}$ together with normalization $\sum_j\pi_j=1$Balance alone is singular — it always has the all-zero solution and determines $\pi$ only up to scale. $\pi_j=0$ on every transient state; mean recurrence time $t^*_j=1/\pi_j$.G5 §3; B&T §7.3
Two structural shortcutsDoubly stochastic ($P$'s columns also sum to $1$) $\Rightarrow\pi_j=1/m$ uniform. Detailed balance $\pi_ip_{ij}=\pi_jp_{ji}$ for all $i,j$ $\Rightarrow\pi$ is stationary (sum over $i$) and the chain is reversibleBoth are sufficient, neither necessary — a stationary $\pi$ need not be reversible. Birth–death chains satisfy detailed balance as the cut equation $\pi_ip_i=\pi_{i+1}q_{i+1}$.G5 §3; B&T §7.3
Absorption probability$a_i=\sum_jp_{ij}a_j$, with $a=1$ at the target absorbing state and $a=0$ at every other absorbing stateFirst-step analysis: condition on one transition, then recurse. One linear equation per transient state.G5 §4; B&T §7.4
Expected time to absorption$\mu_i=1+\sum_jp_{ij}\mu_j$, with $\mu=0$ at every absorbing stateSame recursion with a $+1$ for the step just taken. Also gives mean first passage and mean recurrence times.G5 §4; B&T §7.4
F. Waiting times — the four classics
Wait for the first success$T\sim$ geometric$(p)$,   $\mathbb{E}[T]=\dfrac1p$,   $\operatorname{var}(T)=\dfrac{1-p}{p^{2}}$Independent trials, same $p$. Method: total expectation on the first trial — $\mathbb{E}[T]=1+(1-p)\mathbb{E}[T]$, using memorylessness to say the remaining wait after a failure is again $T$. Fair coin, $p=\tfrac12$: $\mathbb{E}[T]=2$ tosses. Convention warning in §1.3.G2 §3; B&T §2.6
Wait for the $k$th success$T_k\sim$ Pascal of order $k$,   $\mathbb{E}[T_k]=\dfrac kp$,   $\operatorname{var}(T_k)=\dfrac{k(1-p)}{p^{2}}$Method: $T_k$ is the sum of $k$ independent geometric gaps, so linearity gives the mean and independence gives the variance — no new derivation. Fair coin, $k=3$: $\mathbb{E}[T_3]=6$ tosses. Continuous twin: the $k$th Poisson arrival, Erlang$(k,\lambda)$, mean $k/\lambda$ (G4 §3).G4 §2; B&T §6.1
Wait for a pattern (HH)$\mathbb{E}[N_{\text{HH}}]=\dfrac{1+p}{p^{2}}$,   $=6$ for a fair coin;   $k$ heads in a row: $\dfrac{1+p+\cdots+p^{k-1}}{p^{k}}$, $=14$ for HHH fairNot a named distribution — solve it. Method: first-step analysis. States are "how much of the pattern is already banked", i.e. the longest suffix of what you have seen that is a prefix of the target; write $E_s=1+\sum_{s'}\mathbf{P}(s\to s')E_{s'}$ with $E_{\text{stop}}=0$, one equation per state. Here $A=1+pB+(1-p)A$, $B=1+(1-p)A$, giving $A=6$ at $p=\tfrac12$ — a $200{,}000$-toss simulation averages $5.993920$. A tail wipes out the banked head, which is why HH costs $6$ while a single head costs $2$.G2 §3 (Example 3.8, with the state diagram); the identical recursion is the expected-time-to-absorption equation of G5 §4
Wait to collect all $n$ (coupon collector)$\mathbb{E}[M]=n\,H_n=n\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac1j\ \sim\ n\ln n$Draws with replacement, all $n$ items equally likely. Method: split the wait into $n$ phases; with $i$ items already collected the next new one arrives with probability $p_i=(n-i)/n$, so that phase is geometric with mean $n/(n-i)$, and linearity — no independence needed — adds them up. Fair die, $n=6$: $6H_6=6\cdot\tfrac{49}{20}=\tfrac{147}{10}=14.7$ rolls, against a $200{,}000$-run simulation averaging $14.705005$. The last item alone costs $n$ draws, which is the whole $\ln n$.G4 §3 (Example 3.9, rec14 P3); geometric mean from G2 §3
G. The random walk on $\{0,1,\ldots,m\}$ (gambler's ruin)
The model$X_{n+1}=X_n\pm1$ with $\mathbf{P}(+1)=p$; states $0$ and $m$ absorbing; $\rho=\dfrac{1-p}{p}$ is the odds ratioThe walk is a Markov chain — the next fortune depends on the past only through the current one — so nothing here is a special theory: every tool of block E applies unchanged, and the two closed forms below are just the absorption equations of block E solved in a case where the answer happens to be elementary. $\rho>1$ means an unfavorable game, $\rho=1$ a fair one.G5 §1 (Markov property), G5 §4 (this chain); B&T §7.4, Example 7.11
Reaching the target before ruin$a_i=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1-\rho^{\,i}}{1-\rho^{\,m}}, & p\ne\tfrac12,\\[1.4ex] \dfrac im, & p=\tfrac12,\end{cases}$   and $\mathbf{P}(\text{ruin})=1-a_i$$a_i$ starts from fortune $i$. Method: first-step analysis $a_i=p\,a_{i+1}+(1-p)a_{i-1}$ with $a_0=0$, $a_m=1$; write $a_i=p\,a_i+(1-p)a_i$, so the successive differences satisfy $\delta_i=\rho\,\delta_{i-1}$, then telescope. The symmetric answer $i/m$ is linear in the stake; a small edge bends it into an exponential. At $m=20$, $i=10$: $a=0.5$ when $p=\tfrac12$ but only $0.118501$ at $p=0.45$ — five points of edge turn a coin flip into a $1$-in-$8$ shot.G5 §4 (§4.3, Example 4.4); B&T §7.4
Expected duration$D_i=\begin{cases}\dfrac{i-m\,a_i}{1-2p}, & p\ne\tfrac12,\\[1.4ex] i\,(m-i), & p=\tfrac12\end{cases}$Same recursion with a $+1$ for the step taken: $D_i=1+p\,D_{i+1}+(1-p)D_{i-1}$, $D_0=D_m=0$. The symmetric case is the memorable one — $i(m-i)$, a parabola maximized at the midpoint, and the fair game lasts longest. At $m=20$, $i=10$: $D=100$ rounds symmetric (simulation $99.482440$) against $76.299894$ at $p=0.45$.G5 §4 (§4.3); B&T §7.4
Unbounded targetLet $m\to\infty$: if $p\le\tfrac12$ then $a_i\to0$ — ruin is certain; if $p>\tfrac12$ then $a_i\to1-\rho^{\,i}$, strictly less than $1$The fair walk is recurrent but the fortune is not bounded away from $0$, so a fixed bankroll is eventually wiped out however large it is; an edge buys you a positive survival probability but never a guarantee. At $p=0.55$, $i=10$: $\rho=9/11$ and $1-\rho^{10}=\tfrac{22450640200}{25937424601}=0.865569$ (F_rw_p55_i10_a_limit; the finite-$m$ formula at $m=5000$ already returns the same six decimals, F_rw_p55_i10_a_m5000).G5 §4; G5 §2 (recurrence vs transience)
H. Limit theorems
Markov inequality$\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)\le\dfrac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}$ for $a>0$Needs $X\ge0$ and the mean; nothing else. A guarantee for every distribution meeting the hypothesis — and usually very loose.G6 §1; B&T §5.1
Chebyshev inequality$\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge c)\le\dfrac{\sigma^2}{c^2}$; with $c=k\sigma$, $\le1/k^2$Needs mean and variance; $X$ may take either sign. Two-sided. Tight over the class it covers, but degrades badly in the far tail.G6 §1; B&T §5.1
Weak law of large numbers$\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}\to0$ for every fixed $\epsilon>0$, where $M_n=S_n/n$I.i.d. terms with finite mean and variance. It is Chebyshev applied to $M_n$ using $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n$ — there is no second idea. Gives an explicit $n$ meeting a stated tolerance.G6 §1; B&T §5.2–§5.3
Central limit theorem$Z_n=\dfrac{S_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}$ satisfies $\mathbf{P}(Z_n\le c)\to\Phi(c)$; in practice $\mathbf{P}(S_n\le s)\approx\Phi\!\Big(\dfrac{s-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\Big)$I.i.d., finite variance, $n$ large. Only $\mu$ and $\sigma$ of the summands matter. Returns a number, not a bound — no error guarantee is attached.G6 §2; B&T §5.4
The $\tfrac12$ correction (de Moivre–Laplace)$\mathbf{P}(S_n\le k)\approx\Phi\!\Big(\dfrac{k+\tfrac12-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\Big)$,   $\mathbf{P}(S_n\ge k)\approx1-\Phi\!\Big(\dfrac{k-\tfrac12-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\Big)$Integer-valued $S_n$ only. The bar at $k$ occupies $[k-\tfrac12,k+\tfrac12]$, so an uncorrected cut at $k$ discards half of it. On Binomial$(36,\tfrac12)$ at $k=21$: exact $0.878508$, uncorrected $0.841345$ (error $0.037163$), corrected $0.878327$ (error $0.000180$) — a 200-fold improvement for one added constant.G6 §2; B&T §5.4
I. Inference — Bayesian (G6) and classical (G7)
Posterior$f_{\Theta|X}(\theta\mid x)=\dfrac{f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X|\Theta}(x\mid\theta)}{f_X(x)}$, with $f_X(x)=\int f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X|\Theta}(x\mid\theta)\,d\theta$ (plus the three discrete/mixed variants)Bayesian: $\Theta$ is a random variable with a prior. The posterior is the complete answer; every estimator below is a summary of it.G6 §3; G3 §3 (the four Bayes variants); B&T §8.1
MAP estimate$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\max_\theta f_{\Theta|X}(\theta\mid x)=\arg\max_\theta f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X|\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$Needs the posterior only up to a constant — no normalizing integral. Minimizes $\mathbf{P}(\hat\Theta\ne\Theta)$ when $\Theta$ is discrete.G6 §3; B&T §8.2
LMS estimate$\hat\Theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$, with resulting MSE $=\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)\big]$Minimizes $\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-g(X))^2\big]$ over all functions $g$. Needs the full posterior and one integration against it.G6 §4; B&T §8.3
Linear LMS$\hat\Theta_L=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+\dfrac{\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)}{\operatorname{var}(X)}\big(X-\mathbb{E}[X]\big)$, with MSE $=(1-\rho^2)\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$Five numbers, no posterior, no integral: $\mathbb{E}[\Theta],\mathbb{E}[X],\operatorname{var}(X),\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)$ for the estimator, plus $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$ for its error. $\rho^2$ is literally the fraction of the prior variance a straight line removes.G6 §4; B&T §8.4
Estimator qualitybias $=\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]-\theta$;   MSE $=\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_n)+(\text{bias})^2$;   consistent iff $\hat\Theta_n\to\theta$ in probabilityClassical: $\theta$ is an unknown constant, so the model is written $p_X(x;\theta)$ with a semicolon, never a conditioning bar. Each criterion must hold for all $\theta$.G7 §1; B&T §9.1
Confidence interval$\Big[\hat\Theta_n-\dfrac{z\hat\sigma}{\sqrt n},\ \hat\Theta_n+\dfrac{z\hat\sigma}{\sqrt n}\Big]$ with $\Phi(z)=1-\alpha/2$$z=1.644854$ ($90\%$), $1.959964$ ($95\%$), $2.575829$ ($99\%$). Needs the CLT, so $n$ large, plus a $\sigma$ — bounded ($\sigma\le\frac12$ for Bernoulli), plugged in, or estimated by $\hat S_n^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i(X_i-\hat\Theta_n)^2$. The statement is about the interval, which is random, not about $\theta$, which is not.G7 §1; B&T §9.1
Maximum likelihood$\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=\arg\max_\theta p_X(x;\theta)$; in practice maximize $\log p_X(x;\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\log p_{X_i}(x_i;\theta)$ under independenceNo prior, no integral, no normalizing constant. Consistent and asymptotically normal under regularity, but not generally unbiased at finite $n$.G7 §2; B&T §9.1
Linear regression$\hat\theta_1=\dfrac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}$,   $\hat\theta_0=\bar y-\hat\theta_1\bar x$Minimizes $\sum_i(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^2$; closed form, no iteration. Recipe — using this sheet under time pressure.
  1. Name the object before the formula. Is the thing you want a number, a random variable, a distribution, or a bound? Block C's first row exists because that question is answered wrongly more often than any other.
  2. Check the hypothesis column before the statement column. Independence is never implied here: every row that needs it says so in its own Conditions cell, and there are more of them than people remember — products of expectations, convolution, both random-sum rows, merging, splitting (plus the bonus independence between the two split Poisson streams), competing exponentials, the independent-trials hypotheses of the Bernoulli and Poisson process rows and of the waiting times in block F, and the i.i.d. hypotheses of the limit theorems in block H. One row is easy to misfile: variance of a sum is unconditional as printed — it is only the vanishing of its cross terms that needs independence. And linearity of expectation and total expectation never need it at all, which is exactly why the coupon-collector row of block F and the indicator rows of block B work.
  3. Match units. $p$ is dimensionless, $\lambda$ is $1/\text{time}$, a variance carries squared units, $\rho$ carries none. A dimensional mismatch is a modeling error you can catch without doing the problem.
  4. Prefer the row that avoids the distribution. Linearity beats convolution; the indicator method beats a direct sum; linear LMS beats a posterior integral you cannot do. The cheapest row that answers the question is the right one.
  5. Sanity-check against §1.1–§1.2. If your derived mean or variance does not reduce to the table entry in the special case ($k=1$ for Pascal or Erlang, $n=1$ for binomial), the derivation is wrong, not the table.
Practice 1.3 (which row, and does it need independence?). You have $X$ and $Y$ with $\mathbb{E}[X]=3$, $\mathbb{E}[Y]=5$, $\operatorname{var}(X)=4$, $\operatorname{var}(Y)=9$, $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=-3$. Find (i) $\mathbb{E}[2X-Y+1]$, (ii) $\operatorname{var}(2X-Y)$, (iii) $\rho$, (iv) $\mathbb{E}[XY]$, and (v) the MSE of the best linear estimator of $Y$ from $X$. State for each whether independence was needed.
Solution

(i) Linearity (block B, no independence): $2(3)-5+1=2$.

(ii) Variance of a sum with the covariance term, on $2X$ and $-Y$. Using $\operatorname{cov}(aX,bY)=ab\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)$ and the affine rule, $$\operatorname{var}(2X-Y)=4\operatorname{var}(X)+\operatorname{var}(Y)+2(2)(-1)\operatorname{cov}(X,Y) =16+9-4(-3)=37 .$$ No independence — and note that assuming it would have given $25$, a $32.4\%$ understatement.

(iii) $\rho=\operatorname{cov}/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)=-3/(2\times3)=-0.5$, comfortably inside $[-1,1]$ as the bound requires.

(iv) $\mathbb{E}[XY]=\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)+\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]=-3+15=12$. This uses the covariance identity, which is unconditional; the product rule $\mathbb{E}[XY]=\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]=15$ would need independence and is wrong here — the covariance is nonzero, so they are not independent.

(v) Linear LMS with the roles $\Theta=Y$, observation $X$: $\text{MSE}=(1-\rho^2)\operatorname{var}(Y)=(1-0.25)(9)=6.75$. The straight line removed a quarter of $Y$'s variance and no more. No independence — indeed if they were independent, $\rho=0$ and the MSE would be the full $9$: independence is exactly the case where the data are useless.

  • Seven discrete families (§1.1): Bernoulli, discrete uniform, binomial, geometric, Pascal, Poisson, hypergeometric. Distinguish them by what is fixed and what is random — binomial fixes the trials and randomizes the count; Pascal fixes the count and randomizes the trials; hypergeometric removes independence altogether. Plus one multivariate row, the multinomial: $r$ categories instead of $2$, marginals Binomial$(n,\theta_k)$, and covariances $-n\theta_i\theta_j$ that are negative because the counts must add to $n$.
  • Five continuous families (§1.2): uniform, exponential, Erlang/Gamma, normal, beta, plus $\chi^2$ and $t$ flagged as outside 6.041 — reached only if you replace the CLT-based interval of G7 §1 with a small-sample one.
  • Two moments per family, one pattern: summing $k$ independent copies multiplies both mean and variance by $k$ (geometric $\to$ Pascal, exponential $\to$ Erlang), which is why those four rows share two formulas between them.
  • The convention trap (§1.3): our geometric counts trials, mean $1/p$; the Chen–Blitzstein cheatsheet and many texts count failures, mean $(1-p)/p$. Means differ by exactly $1$, variances agree. scipy follows ours.
