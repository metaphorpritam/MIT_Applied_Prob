The first two sections of this page consolidated what 6.041 teaches. This one steps outside it, twice. Part A adds the tool the course deliberately leaves out — the moment generating function — because it turns the hardest routine computation in G3 (adding independent random variables) into multiplication, and because every neighboring course (Harvard's Stat 110, most graduate sequences, all of mathematical statistics) assumes you have it. Part B adds the single best cautionary tale about conditioning, Simpson's paradox: a reversal in which one treatment is worse for every patient group and yet better for the whole population. Nothing in G1–G7 is wrong without it, but a reader who leaves 6.041 able to compute $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B)$ flawlessly and unable to spot this trap is not yet safe with data.

3.1 The moment generating function

Definition (moment generating function; B&T §4.4). The moment generating function (MGF) of a random variable $X$ is the function of a real variable $t$ $$M_X(t)=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{tX}\big]= \begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_x e^{tx}p_X(x), & X \text{ discrete},\\[6pt] \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{tx}f_X(x)\,dx, & X\text{ continuous},\end{cases}$$ defined for every $t$ for which the sum or integral is finite. Here $t$ is not a random quantity and has no probabilistic meaning at all: it is a bookkeeping variable, a dial you turn to extract information about $X$. The expectation is of the random variable $e^{tX}$, computed by the expected value rule of G2 §2 (discrete) or G3 §1 (continuous) — no new machinery is involved.

Three immediate observations, each one line. First, $M_X(0)=\mathbb{E}[e^0]=\mathbb{E}[1]=1$ for every random variable, which is the cheapest sanity check there is on an MGF you have just computed (all four MGFs differentiated in computes/rf_s3.py return $M(0)=1.000000$). Second, $M_X(t)>0$ wherever it is defined, since $e^{tX}>0$. Third, $M_X$ depends on $X$ only through its distribution — two random variables with the same PMF or PDF have the same MGF, whatever experiment they live on.

Gotcha (existence). Unlike the mean and the variance, an MGF can fail to exist for every $t\ne0$. The sum or integral defining it must converge, and $e^{tx}$ grows fast. Two of the entries in the table below are only defined on part of the line: the exponential MGF needs $t<\lambda$ and the geometric MGF needs $e^t(1-p)<1$, i.e. $t<-\ln(1-p)$ — for $p=0.3$ that is $t<0.356675$ (mgf_geometric_radius). Heavy-tailed distributions such as the lognormal have $M_X(t)=\infty$ for every $t>0$. All the theory below needs $M_X$ finite only on some open interval containing $t=0$, which is why the definition is stated pointwise. (The characteristic function $\mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]$, which is $M_X$ evaluated on the imaginary axis, exists for every random variable and is what a rigorous treatment uses instead; that is a complex analysis course, not this one.)

Why it is called moment generating

Expand $e^{tX}$ in its power series $e^u=\sum_{n\ge0}u^n/n!$ with $u=tX$, and take expectations term by term (legitimate whenever $M_X$ is finite on an interval around $0$; the justification is a convergence theorem outside this course): $$M_X(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^nX^n}{n!}\right] =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^n}{n!}\,\mathbb{E}[X^n],$$ the second equality using linearity of expectation on each term. So $M_X$ is a power series in $t$ whose $n$th coefficient carries $\mathbb{E}[X^n]$, the $n$th moment of $X$: the MGF packages the whole sequence of moments into one function. Differentiating $n$ times and setting $t=0$ kills every term but one.

Property (moments by differentiation; B&T §4.4). Wherever $M_X$ is finite on an interval around $0$, $$\frac{d}{dt}M_X(t)\Big|_{t=0}=\mathbb{E}[X],\qquad \frac{d^2}{dt^2}M_X(t)\Big|_{t=0}=\mathbb{E}[X^2],\qquad \frac{d^n}{dt^n}M_X(t)\Big|_{t=0}=\mathbb{E}[X^n],$$ and therefore $\operatorname{var}(X)=M_X''(0)-\big(M_X'(0)\big)^2$ by the shortcut formula of G2 §3.

Direct proof of the first one, without the power series: differentiate under the expectation, $$\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{tX}\big]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{d}{dt}e^{tX}\right] =\mathbb{E}\big[Xe^{tX}\big],$$ and set $t=0$ to get $\mathbb{E}[Xe^{0}]=\mathbb{E}[X]$. Differentiating once more gives $\mathbb{E}[X^2e^{tX}]$ and hence $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ at $t=0$, and so on.

Example 3.1 (moments read off three MGFs). Each of the following is differentiated symbolically in computes/rf_s3.py and checked against the mean and variance the notes already derived.
  • Poisson($\lambda=1.5$), $M(t)=e^{1.5(e^t-1)}$: $M'(t)=1.5e^tM(t)$, so $M'(0)=1.5$; $M''(t)=1.5e^tM(t)+\big(1.5e^t\big)^2M(t)$, so $M''(0)=1.5+2.25=3.75$ and $\operatorname{var}=3.75-1.5^2=1.5$ (mom_pois_mean $=1.500000$, mom_pois_EX2 $=3.750000$, mom_pois_var $=1.500000$). Mean $=$ variance $=\lambda$, exactly as G4 §3 has it.
  • Exponential($\lambda=1.5$), $M(t)=\lambda/(\lambda-t)$: $M'(t)=\lambda/(\lambda-t)^2$ gives $M'(0)=1/\lambda=0.666667$, and $M''(t)=2\lambda/(\lambda-t)^3$ gives $M''(0)=2/\lambda^2=0.888889$, so $\operatorname{var}=0.888889-0.666667^2=0.444444=1/\lambda^2$ (mom_expo_mean, mom_expo_EX2, mom_expo_var) — the G3 §1 values.
  • Normal($\mu=1,\sigma^2=4$), $M(t)=e^{t+2t^2}$: $M'(0)=1.000000$, $M''(0)=5.000000$, $\operatorname{var}=5-1=4.000000$ (mom_norm_*).
Notice what did not happen: no integral was evaluated. Once the MGF is known, every moment is calculus on a formula rather than an integration against a density.

3.2 The two properties that make transforms powerful

Moment extraction is a convenience. The following two properties are the reason MGFs exist.

Property 1 (the MGF of a sum is the product of the MGFs; B&T §4.4). If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then for every $t$ at which both are finite $$M_{X+Y}(t)=M_X(t)\,M_Y(t).$$ Proof, one line. $M_{X+Y}(t)=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{t(X+Y)}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{tX}e^{tY}\big] =\mathbb{E}\big[e^{tX}\big]\mathbb{E}\big[e^{tY}\big]=M_X(t)M_Y(t)$, the first equality by $e^{a+b}=e^ae^b$ and the third by the rule of G3 §2 that expectation is multiplicative for functions of independent random variables — $e^{tX}$ is a function of $X$ alone and $e^{tY}$ a function of $Y$ alone, so if $X\perp Y$ then $e^{tX}\perp e^{tY}$. By induction, $M_{X_1+\cdots+X_n}=\prod_iM_{X_i}$, and for i.i.d. summands $M_{S_n}(t)=\big(M_X(t)\big)^n$.
Property 2 (uniqueness — the MGF determines the distribution). If $M_X(t)$ and $M_Y(t)$ are finite and equal for all $t$ in some open interval containing $0$, then $X$ and $Y$ have the same distribution: the same PMF if discrete, the same PDF if continuous. (The proof is an inversion theorem, well beyond 6.041 and beyond B&T, which states the result and uses it.)
Interpretation. Property 1 converts addition of independent random variables — the operation the whole second half of the course is built on: binomial as a sum of Bernoullis, Erlang as a sum of exponentials, the sample mean of the CLT, random sums in G4 §2 — into multiplication of functions. Property 2 says nothing is lost in the conversion, so you may work entirely in transform space and read the answer off at the end by recognizing the product. That is exactly the role logarithms play for multiplication, or Laplace transforms for differential equations: a hard operation on one side of a dictionary, an easy one on the other.
Gotcha. Property 1 needs independence, not merely uncorrelatedness, and it runs in one direction only. $M_{X+Y}=M_XM_Y$ can hold for dependent $X,Y$ by accident, so observing the factorization does not prove independence — exactly the asymmetry G3 §4 records for $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=0$.

3.4 The payoff: two theorems the notes prove the long way

G3 §4 establishes that a sum of independent normals is normal by a five-step convolution — expand the exponent, complete the square, do the Gaussian integral, simplify the exponent, simplify the constant. Here is the same theorem in one line, and a second theorem for free.

Fig. 3.1 — The two roads to the distribution of a sum of independent random variables. The upper road is the one G3 §4 takes, because 6.041 never introduces the lower one.
Theorem (sum of independent Poissons is Poisson). If $X\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_1)$ and $Y\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_2)$ are independent, then $X+Y\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$.

Proof. By Property 1 and the table, $$M_{X+Y}(t)=M_X(t)M_Y(t)=e^{\lambda_1(e^t-1)}\cdot e^{\lambda_2(e^t-1)} =e^{(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)(e^t-1)},$$ the last step because $e^ae^b=e^{a+b}$. That is the MGF of a Poisson with parameter $\lambda_1+\lambda_2$, so by Property 2 the sum is that Poisson. $\blacksquare$

The symbolic identity $e^{1.5(e^t-1)}e^{2.5(e^t-1)}=e^{4(e^t-1)}$ is confirmed in computes/rf_s3.py (pois_sum_symbolic_ok $=$ True), and the distributional claim is confirmed independently by brute force: the discrete convolution $\sum_{i=0}^{w}p_X(i)p_Y(w-i)$ for $\lambda_1=1.5,\lambda_2=2.5$ returns $0.018316,\,0.073263,\,0.146525,\,0.195367,\,0.195367,\,0.156293,\ldots$ for $w=0,1,\dots$, matching the Poisson($4$) PMF to $2.8\times10^{-17}$ (pois_conv_pmf, pois_closed_pmf, pois_conv_maxerr). The convolution proof of the same fact requires recognizing $\sum_i\binom{w}{i}\lambda_1^i\lambda_2^{w-i}=(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^w$ after multiplying and dividing by $w!$ — the binomial theorem, hidden inside a factorial manipulation. The transform proof needs no identity at all.

Theorem (sum of independent normals is normal; the G3 §4 result). If $X\sim N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)$ and $Y\sim N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)$ are independent, then $X+Y\sim N(\mu_1+\mu_2,\ \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$.

Proof. $$M_{X+Y}(t)=e^{\mu_1t+\sigma_1^2t^2/2}\cdot e^{\mu_2t+\sigma_2^2t^2/2} =e^{(\mu_1+\mu_2)t+(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)t^2/2},$$ collecting the coefficients of $t$ and of $t^2/2$ in the exponent. That is the MGF of $N(\mu_1+\mu_2,\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$; apply Property 2. $\blacksquare$

With G3 §4's numbers, $\sigma_1=1$ and $\sigma_2=2$: the product $e^{t^2/2}e^{4t^2/2}=e^{5t^2/2}$ (norm_sum_symbolic_ok), so $X+Y\sim N(0,5)$ with standard deviation $2.236068$, and the $N(0,5)$ density at $w=0,1,3$ equals $0.178412,\,0.161434,\,0.072537$ (norm_sum_density_0_1_3) — the very three numbers G3 §4 obtains by evaluating the convolution integral numerically. The general-parameter check $N(\mu,\sigma^2)+N(2\mu,9\sigma^2)=N(3\mu,10\sigma^2)$ also passes symbolically (norm_sum_general_ok).

Which is less work? The transform route, by a wide margin — and it is not close. The convolution derivation in G3 §4 runs to five labeled steps, needs the completed-square trick and the Gaussian integral $\int e^{-a(x-m)^2}dx=\sqrt{\pi/a}$, and produces two intermediate constants ($a$ and $\gamma$) that exist only to be simplified away. The transform derivation is two applications of $e^ae^b=e^{a+b}$ and a glance at the table. The same asymmetry holds for the Poisson, for the binomial (G3 §4 needs Vandermonde's identity; the MGF needs $(1-p+pe^t)^{n_1}(1-p+pe^t)^{n_2}=(1-p+pe^t)^{n_1+n_2}$), and for the Erlang. What convolution gives you that transforms do not is the shape of the answer when the answer is not a named distribution — the sliding-overlap picture of the triangular density in G3 §4 has no transform analogue. Use convolution to see; use transforms to prove.
Gotcha. Recognition is the whole method, so it only works when the product is in your table. $M_{X+Y}(t)$ for $X,Y$ independent uniforms on $[0,1]$ is $\big((e^t-1)/t\big)^2$, which is a perfectly good MGF and tells you nothing you can name; the triangular density of G3 §4 has to be obtained by convolution (or by inverting the transform, which is harder than the convolution). Transforms shine on closed families, not on arbitrary sums.
Why 6.041 leaves this out. The course states its own boundary: L12's random sums slide reads "mean and variance only; no transforms", which is why G4 §2 quotes the transform identity $M_Y(t)=M_N\big(\log M_X(t)\big)$ for a random sum $Y=X_1+\cdots+X_N$ only in a parenthesis and derives the mean and variance by the law of iterated expectations instead. B&T develops transforms properly in §4.4; the lectures skip that section, so G3 §4 routes every sum through convolution and G6 §2 states the central limit theorem without proving it. (The standard proof is a transform argument: the MGF of the standardized sum is $\big(M_{(X-\mu)/\sigma}(t/\sqrt n)\big)^n$ by Property 1, its logarithm expands to $t^2/2+O(n^{-1/2})$, so the MGF converges to $e^{t^2/2}$ — the $N(0,1)$ MGF — and a continuity theorem upgrades that to the CDF convergence G6 §2 states. Every ingredient except the last is on this page.) Knowing that this is the missing tool explains several of the notes' longer derivations at a stroke.
Practice 3.1. $X\sim\text{Exponential}(\lambda)$. Use only the MGF to obtain $\mathbb{E}[X]$, $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)$, and evaluate them at $\lambda=1.5$.
Solution

From the table, $M_X(t)=\lambda(\lambda-t)^{-1}$ for $t<\lambda$. Differentiate with the power rule and the chain rule (the inner derivative of $\lambda-t$ is $-1$): $$M_X'(t)=\lambda(\lambda-t)^{-2},\qquad M_X''(t)=2\lambda(\lambda-t)^{-3}.$$ At $t=0$: $\mathbb{E}[X]=\lambda/\lambda^2=1/\lambda$ and $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=2\lambda/\lambda^3 =2/\lambda^2$. Hence $$\operatorname{var}(X)=\frac{2}{\lambda^2}-\frac{1}{\lambda^2}=\frac{1}{\lambda^2}.$$ At $\lambda=1.5$: $\mathbb{E}[X]=0.666667$, $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=0.888889$, $\operatorname{var}(X)=0.444444$ (mom_expo_mean, mom_expo_EX2, mom_expo_var) — the values G3 §1 obtains by integrating by parts twice.

Practice 3.2. A random variable $W$ has $M_W(t)=\big(0.7+0.3e^t\big)^5$ for all $t$. Name its distribution, and give $\mathbf{P}(W=0)$.
Solution

The expression matches the table's binomial row $\big(1-p+pe^t\big)^n$ with $p=0.3$ and $n=5$. By Property 2 (uniqueness) no other distribution has this MGF, so $W\sim\text{Bin}(5,0.3)$ and $\mathbf{P}(W=0)=(0.7)^5=0.168070$ (prac32_p0). Check: $M_W(0.4)=(0.7+0.3e^{0.4})^5=1.990000$, and summing $\sum_k\binom5k(0.3)^k(0.7)^{5-k}e^{0.4k}$ directly also gives $1.990000$ (mgf_binom_num_closed, mgf_binom_num_direct). Equivalently, read the MGF as $\big(0.7+0.3e^t\big)^5=\prod_{i=1}^5M_{X_i}(t)$ with each $X_i$ Bernoulli($0.3$): Property 1 says $W$ is a sum of five independent Bernoulli($0.3$) trials, which is the definition of Bin($5,0.3$).

Practice 3.3. $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are i.i.d. Exponential($\lambda$). Find $M_{S_n}(t)$ for $S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n$, and use it to get $\mathbb{E}[S_n]$ and $\operatorname{var}(S_n)$. Evaluate $M_{S_n}(0.5)$ for $n=3,\lambda=2$.
Solution

By Property 1 applied $n-1$ times, and because the summands are identically distributed, $$M_{S_n}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^n M_{X_i}(t)=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-t}\right)^{\!n}, \qquad t<\lambda .$$ This is the MGF of the Erlang of order $n$, whose density $f_{S_n}(s)=\lambda^ns^{n-1}e^{-\lambda s}/(n-1)!$ appears in G4 §3 as the time of the $n$th Poisson arrival; by uniqueness, the two agree. For the moments write $M(t)=\lambda^n(\lambda-t)^{-n}$: $$M'(t)=n\lambda^n(\lambda-t)^{-n-1},\quad M''(t)=n(n+1)\lambda^n(\lambda-t)^{-n-2},$$ so $\mathbb{E}[S_n]=n/\lambda$ and $\mathbb{E}[S_n^2]=n(n+1)/\lambda^2$, giving $$\operatorname{var}(S_n)=\frac{n(n+1)}{\lambda^2}-\frac{n^2}{\lambda^2}=\frac{n}{\lambda^2},$$ which is $n$ times the single-exponential mean and variance, as linearity and independence require. Check at $n=3,\lambda=2,t=0.5$: the closed form gives $(2/1.5)^3=2.370370$, and integrating $\int_0^\infty e^{0.5s}f_{S_3}(s)ds$ numerically gives $2.370370$, difference $4.4\times10^{-16}$ (erlang_mgf_at_0.5, erlang_mgf_numeric, erlang_mgf_abserr).

3.5 Simpson's paradox: when conditioning reverses everything

Now the cautionary tale. G1 §2 taught you to condition and to reassemble conditional answers with the total probability theorem. Simpson's paradox is what happens when someone reassembles without thinking about the weights — and it is not a curiosity, it is the standard failure mode of real-world data analysis.

The paradox (Simpson, 1951). There exist events $A$, $B$, $C$ with all the relevant conditioning events of positive probability such that $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C)<\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C^c)<\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C^c),$$ and yet $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid B)>\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c).$$ In words: $B$ is worse than $B^c$ within the group $C$, worse than $B^c$ within the group $C^c$ — and better than $B^c$ overall. The two strata do not tell you the aggregate, and the aggregate does not tell you the strata.
Example 3.2 (kidney stones — Charig et al., BMJ 1986; a real instance). Two treatments for kidney stones were compared on $700$ patients, $350$ per treatment: open surgery (the older, more invasive operation) and percutaneous nephrolithotomy ("PN", the newer, less invasive one). Every patient's stone was classified as small ($<2$ cm) or large. Take

  • $B=\{\text{patient got PN}\}$, so $B^c=\{\text{patient got open surgery}\}$,
  • $C=\{\text{small stone}\}$, so $C^c=\{\text{large stone}\}$ — the lurking variable.

Why it happens: the weights, not the rates

Nothing paradoxical is going on; the arithmetic is the total probability theorem of G1 §2, applied inside each treatment group. Condition everything on the treatment and partition by stone size: $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid B)=\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C)\,\mathbf{P}(C\mid B) +\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C^c)\,\mathbf{P}(C^c\mid B),$$ which is exactly G1 §2's theorem with every probability conditioned additionally on $B$ (conditional probabilities are probabilities, so all the rules survive — that is the point of G1 §2.1). The aggregate rate for each treatment is therefore a weighted average of its two stratum rates, with weights given by that treatment's case mix. Substituting the numbers: $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid B)=0.866667\times0.771429+0.687500\times0.228571=0.825714,$$ $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c)=0.931034\times0.248571+0.730038\times0.751429=0.780000,$$ and both reproduce the marginal rates exactly as fractions (ks_totprob_PN, ks_totprob_OS, with exactness flags ks_totprob_PN_exact_ok and ks_totprob_OS_exact_ok both True).

Now look at the weights, which is where the whole effect lives. Of the PN patients, $\mathbf{P}(C\mid B)=270/350=0.771429$ had small stones; of the open-surgery patients, only $\mathbf{P}(C\mid B^c)=87/350=0.248571$ did (ks_wsmall_PN, ks_wsmall_OS) — a ratio of $3.103448$ (ks_assoc_ratio). Small stones are the easy cases: both treatments succeed about $0.87$–$0.93$ of the time on them versus $0.69$–$0.73$ on large ones. So PN's aggregate is mostly an average over easy cases and open surgery's is mostly an average over hard ones. PN wins the aggregate not by treating patients better but by treating easier patients — in the real study, surgeons sent the severe cases to open surgery, exactly the sort of association that makes the lurking variable dangerous.

Interpretation (the right-hand panel of Fig. 3.2). Write the aggregate as a function of the small-stone share $w=\mathbf{P}(C\mid\text{treatment})$: $$r(w)=r_{\text{large}}+w\big(r_{\text{small}}-r_{\text{large}}\big),$$ a straight line from the large-stone rate at $w=0$ to the small-stone rate at $w=1$. Open surgery's line lies above PN's at every $w$ — that is precisely the two stratum inequalities. The aggregate compares the two lines at different values of $w$: open surgery is read at $w=0.248571$, PN at $w=0.771429$. Comparing points on two curves at different arguments tells you nothing about which curve is higher. That is the entire paradox, in one sentence.

The repair: hold the weights fixed

Recipe (comparing two groups when a lurking variable is present).
  1. Compare within strata first. Report $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C)$ against $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C)$ and $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C^c)$ against $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C^c)$. If both comparisons point the same way, that is the answer, whatever the aggregate says.
  2. Check the case mix. Compute $\mathbf{P}(C\mid B)$ and $\mathbf{P}(C\mid B^c)$. If they differ materially, the aggregate comparison is uninterpretable as it stands.
  3. Standardize. Recombine each group's stratum rates using one common set of weights — for instance the pooled shares $\mathbf{P}(C)$ and $\mathbf{P}(C^c)$ — so that the comparison holds the lurking variable fixed.

For the kidney stones, the pooled shares are $\mathbf{P}(C)=357/700=0.510000$ and $\mathbf{P}(C^c)=343/700=0.490000$ (ks_pool_wsmall, ks_pool_wlarge). Standardizing both treatments to that common mix: $$\text{open surgery: }0.931034\times0.510000+0.730038\times0.490000=0.832546,$$ $$\text{PN: }0.866667\times0.510000+0.687500\times0.490000=0.778875,$$ so open surgery leads by $0.053671$ (ks_std_OS, ks_std_PN, ks_std_gap) — the sign now agrees with both strata, as it must, because with equal weights the difference of the two weighted averages is the weighted average of the two (negative) stratum differences.

Property (no reversal under a common case mix). If $\mathbf{P}(C\mid B)=\mathbf{P}(C\mid B^c)=w$, then $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid B)-\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c) =w\underbrace{\big[\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C)-\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C)\big]}_{\le\,0\text{ by hypothesis}} +(1-w)\underbrace{\big[\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C^c)-\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C^c)\big]}_{\le\,0},$$ a convex combination of two nonpositive numbers, hence nonpositive: no reversal is possible. The paradox needs unequal weights, i.e. it needs $C$ to be associated with $B$. (Check on the kidney-stone rates with the equal weights $w=1-w=\tfrac12$: the aggregate difference PN minus open surgery becomes $-0.053453$ — negative, as the strata demand; noreversal_demo.)
Gotcha (the practical rule). Never aggregate over a variable that is associated with both the treatment and the outcome. Both conditions matter. Stone size is associated with the outcome (easy cases succeed more often) and with the treatment (severe cases were steered to surgery); a variable associated with only one of the two cannot produce a reversal. And notice the direction of the fix: the disaggregated table is the trustworthy one here because stone size is a pre-existing feature of the patient. If the third variable is instead a consequence of the treatment — say, a side effect the treatment causes — then conditioning on it is the mistake and the aggregate is right. Which table to believe is a question about the causal story, not about the arithmetic; the arithmetic is consistent with both.
Where else this bites. The same reversal shows up whenever rates are pooled across unbalanced groups: department-by-department university admissions (each department admits men and women at similar rates, but women apply disproportionately to the competitive departments), hospital mortality rates (the best hospitals receive the sickest patients), and any comparison of "success rates" between two organizations with different intake. The tell is always the same — a striking aggregate difference and an unexamined case mix.
Practice 3.4 (batting averages, Jeter vs Justice, 1995–96). Derek Jeter batted $12$ for $48$ in 1995 and $183$ for $582$ in 1996. David Justice batted $104$ for $411$ in 1995 and $45$ for $140$ in 1996. Compute the four season averages and the two two-year averages. Who is better each year? Who is better overall? Identify the lurking variable and the weights.
Solution

Season averages (hits divided by at-bats): Jeter $12/48=0.250000$ in 1995 and $183/582=0.314433$ in 1996; Justice $104/411=0.253041$ in 1995 and $45/140=0.321429$ in 1996 (bat_Jeter_1995, bat_Jeter_1996, bat_Justice_1995, bat_Justice_1996). Justice is better in both seasons, by $0.003041$ and $0.006996$ (bat_gap_1995, bat_gap_1996).

Two-year averages. Add hits and at-bats separately: Jeter $195/630=0.309524$, Justice $149/551=0.270417$ (bat_Jeter_combined, bat_Justice_combined). Jeter is better overall, by $0.039106$ (bat_gap_combined) — a reversal, and a much larger margin than either season's (bat_reversal_ok $=$ True).

The lurking variable is the season ($C=\{1996\}$), which is associated with the outcome — both players hit far better in 1996 — and with the "treatment", i.e. which player we are looking at, because the two had wildly different at-bat mixes: $582/630=0.923810$ of Jeter's at-bats fell in the good year against only $140/551=0.254083$ of Justice's (bat_Jeter_w1996, bat_Justice_w1996). Jeter's combined average is essentially his 1996 average; Justice's is essentially his 1995 average. Once again the aggregate compares the two players at different points of the weight axis.

Practice 3.5. A colleague reports: "Across our two clinics combined, drug D cured $60\%$ of patients and drug E cured $70\%$; use E." What two follow-up questions settle whether this conclusion is safe, and what would each answer rule out?
Solution

Question 1: what are the cure rates clinic by clinic? If E beats D in each clinic, the aggregate agrees with the strata and nothing is hidden. If D beats E in each clinic, the aggregate is reversed and the recommendation is backwards.

Question 2: what fraction of each drug's patients came from each clinic? By the no-reversal property above, if the two drugs have the same clinic mix, $\mathbf{P}(\text{clinic 1}\mid D)=\mathbf{P}(\text{clinic 1}\mid E)$, then no reversal is possible and the aggregate is trustworthy. A reversal requires the clinic to be associated with the drug — for instance if the specialist clinic with the harder cases prescribes D more often.

If the mixes differ, ask for the standardized rates: recombine each drug's per-clinic rates with one common set of clinic weights, as in the kidney-stone repair above. And before doing any of this, check the causal direction — the clinic is a pre-existing feature of the patient, so conditioning on it is legitimate; a post-treatment variable would not be.

§3 cheatsheet — transforms and Simpson's paradox
  • MGF. $M_X(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]$, defined where finite; always $M_X(0)=1$. Moments: $\mathbb{E}[X^n]=M_X^{(n)}(0)$; $\operatorname{var}(X)=M''(0)-\big(M'(0)\big)^2$.
  • The two properties. $X\perp Y\Rightarrow M_{X+Y}=M_XM_Y$ (addition becomes multiplication); and the MGF determines the distribution (so recognizing the product finishes the proof). i.i.d. sums: $M_{S_n}=(M_X)^n$.
  • Table. Bernoulli $1-p+pe^t$ · binomial $(1-p+pe^t)^n$ · geometric $pe^t/\big(1-(1-p)e^t\big)$ for $t<-\ln(1-p)$ · Poisson $e^{\lambda(e^t-1)}$ · uniform $(e^{tb}-e^{ta})/\big(t(b-a)\big)$ · exponential $\lambda/(\lambda-t)$ for $t<\lambda$ · normal $e^{\mu t+\sigma^2t^2/2}$.
  • Payoffs in one line each. Poisson$(\lambda_1)+$Poisson$(\lambda_2)=$ Poisson$(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$; $N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)+N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)= N(\mu_1+\mu_2,\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$; Bin$(n_1,p)+$Bin$(n_2,p)=$Bin$(n_1+n_2,p)$; $n$ i.i.d. exponentials $=$ Erlang, $\big(\lambda/(\lambda-t)\big)^n$. G3 §4 proves the first two by convolution, which is strictly more work; transforms are absent from 6.041 by explicit choice (L12: "no transforms"), and B&T §4.4 is the section to read.
  • Simpson's paradox. $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C)<\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C)$ and $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C^c)<\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C^c)$ are compatible with $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B)>\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c)$. Kidney stones: open surgery wins $0.931$–$0.867$ on small stones and $0.730$–$0.688$ on large, yet loses $0.780$–$0.826$ overall.
  • Why. Each aggregate is a total-probability weighted average (G1 §2) of that group's own stratum rates, with that group's own weights: $0.771429$ small-stone share for PN against $0.248571$ for open surgery. Equal weights $\Rightarrow$ no reversal possible.
  • Rule. Never aggregate over a variable associated with both the treatment and the outcome; compare within strata, or standardize to a common mix (open surgery $0.832546$ vs PN $0.778875$ at the pooled mix $w=0.510000$). Which table to trust is a causal question: condition on pre-existing covariates, never on post-treatment ones.
