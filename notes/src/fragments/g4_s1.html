§1   Conditional expectation as a random variable

Sources: L12 slides 1–6 (raster p01–p02) · rec12 problems 1–4 (+ official solutions) · Bertsekas–Tsitsiklis §4.2 (covariance and correlation, pp. 217–222), §4.3 (conditional expectation and variance revisited, Examples 4.16–4.21, pp. 222–228), §4.1 (derived distributions), B&T Problems 4.16 (p. 248) and 4.20 (p. 250). G2 §3 and G3 §2 taught you to condition and then average: fix the value $Y=y$, work inside the smaller universe where that is known, get a number $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$, then weight those numbers by $p_Y(y)$ or $f_Y(y)$ and add. That is the total expectation theorem, and it is a computational device. This section takes one small step that turns the device into a way of thinking: instead of treating $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$ as a table of numbers indexed by $y$, we treat the whole table as a single random variable $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ — the one whose value is $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$ on the outcomes where $Y$ happens to equal $y$.

The payoff is two formulas that will carry the rest of this note. The law of iterated expectations, $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]=\mathbb{E}[X]$, is the total expectation theorem rewritten so that it composes: you can condition on anything, in any order, and the averages agree. The law of total variance, $\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]+\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)$, has no counterpart at all in the earlier chapters: it splits randomness into within-group and between-group parts and is the tool that makes random sums (§2), Bernoulli and Poisson process quantities (§§2–4), and estimation errors (G6) computable. L12's stick-breaking and class-sections examples are done here in full, the lecture's unsolved section-quiz example is solved, and recitation 12 — which consolidates the correlation and derived-distribution machinery of G3 — is worked end to end.

Interpretation. Everything below is one sentence made precise: knowing $Y$ changes your best guess about $X$, and the amount by which it changes is itself random. The law of iterated expectations says the change averages out to nothing. The law of total variance says the change nevertheless accounts for a definite share of the total uncertainty in $X$ — the share you would remove by learning $Y$.

1.1 From a number to a random variable: $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$ versus $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$

Definition (conditional expectation given a value; L12 slide 2, B&T §4.3). Let $X$ and $Y$ be random variables on the same experiment, and let $y$ be a value with $p_Y(y)>0$ (discrete case) or $f_Y(y)>0$ (continuous case). The conditional expectation of $X$ given $Y=y$ is $$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]\;=\;\sum_x x\,p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)\quad\text{(discrete)},\qquad \mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]\;=\;\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)\,dx\quad\text{(continuous)} .$$ Here $p_{X\mid Y}(\cdot\mid y)$ and $f_{X\mid Y}(\cdot\mid y)$ are the conditional PMF and PDF of G2 §4 and G3 §2 — legitimate probability laws in their own right — and $x$ ranges over the values $X$ can take. For each fixed $y$ this is a number.

Now vary $y$. Every value of $y$ produces one number, so we have defined a function $$g(y)\;=\;\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y],$$ a perfectly ordinary real-valued function of a real variable. Feed it the random variable $Y$ instead of a number $y$ and you get a new random variable, by the same "function of a random variable is a random variable" rule from G2 §1.

Definition (conditional expectation as a random variable; L12 slide 2, B&T §4.3). $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ denotes the random variable $g(Y)$, where $g(y)=\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$. On any outcome of the experiment for which $Y$ takes the value $y$, the random variable $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ takes the value $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$. Its distribution is inherited from the distribution of $Y$.
Gotcha — the one piece of notation everything here depends on.
$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$a number. It depends on $y$, which is a bound symbol you supply, not on chance. You can plot it against $y$; you cannot ask for its variance.
$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$a random variable. It is a function of $Y$, so it has a distribution, an expectation, and a variance. It is not a function of $X$ — $X$ has been averaged away.
$\mathbb{E}[X]$a number, the unconditional mean.
Three symptoms of getting this wrong: writing "$\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y])$" (the variance of a number, always $0$); writing "$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=\sum_y \mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]p_Y(y)$" (that is $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]$, a number, not the random variable); and treating $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ as still depending on $X$ — for instance "cancelling" $X$ against it.

Example 1.1 (two class sections; L12 slide 4, B&T Example 4.18). A class of $30$ students is split into two sections: section $1$ has $10$ students whose quiz scores average $90$, section $2$ has $20$ students whose scores average $60$. Pick one student uniformly at random; let $X$ be that student's score and $Y\in\{1,2\}$ the student's section. Find $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$, the random variable $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ and its distribution, and $\mathbb{E}[X]$.

The probability model (the slide does not spell this out — [DERIVATION GAP] filled). "Pick a student uniformly at random" means each of the $30$ students has probability $1/30$. The event $\{Y=1\}$ contains the $10$ students of section 1, so $$\mathbf{P}(Y=1)=\frac{10}{30}=\frac13,\qquad \mathbf{P}(Y=2)=\frac{20}{30}=\frac23 .$$ That is where the $1/3$ and $2/3$ on the slide come from — they are section sizes, not anything about scores.

Conditional expectations. Given $\{Y=1\}$, the chosen student is uniform among the $10$ students of section 1, so each has conditional probability $1/10$, and $$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=1]=\sum_{i=1}^{10}x_i\cdot\frac1{10}=\frac1{10}\sum_{i=1}^{10}x_i=90,$$ which is precisely the section-1 average given on the slide. Identically $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=2]=\frac1{20}\sum_{i=11}^{30}x_i=60$. So $g(1)=90$ and $g(2)=60$.

The random variable. $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=g(Y)$ takes the value $90$ exactly when $Y=1$ and $60$ exactly when $Y=2$: $$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=\begin{cases}90,&\text{with probability }1/3,\\[2pt] 60,&\text{with probability }2/3.\end{cases}$$ It is a two-valued random variable — a coarse-grained version of $X$ in which every student has been replaced by his or her section average.

Its mean. By the expected value rule applied to this two-valued random variable, $$\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]=\tfrac13\cdot 90+\tfrac23\cdot 60=30+40=70 .$$ And directly, averaging all $30$ scores, $\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac1{30}\sum_{i=1}^{30}x_i=\frac{90\cdot10+60\cdot20}{30}=\frac{900+1200}{30}=\frac{2100}{30}=70$ (sec_EX). The two agree — that is the law of iterated expectations, proved in §1.2.

Example 1.2 (stick breaking; L12 slide 2, B&T Example 4.17). A stick of length $\ell$ is broken at a point chosen uniformly over its length; keep the left piece, of length $Y$. Break that piece again at a point chosen uniformly over its length; keep the left piece, of length $X$. Find $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$, then $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$.

Solution — filling the [DERIVATION GAP] on the slide. The slide simply asserts $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]=y/2$. Here is why. Condition on $\{Y=y\}$ for some $0<y\le\ell$. In that universe the second break point is, by the statement of the experiment, uniformly distributed over the interval $[0,y]$, and $X$ is that break point. So the conditional density is the uniform density on $[0,y]$ (G3 §1): $$f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)=\frac1y,\qquad 0\le x\le y,$$ and $0$ otherwise. Apply the definition: $$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)\,dx=\int_0^{y}x\cdot\frac1y\,dx =\frac1y\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^{y}=\frac1y\cdot\frac{y^2}{2}=\frac{y}{2},$$ using only the antiderivative of $x$ and the fact that $1/y$ is constant in $x$. (You could also quote the mean of a $U(0,y)$ variable from G3 §1, which is the midpoint $(0+y)/2$.) Hence $g(y)=y/2$.

The random variable. Replace the dummy $y$ by the random $Y$: $$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=\frac{Y}{2}.$$ Since $Y\sim U(0,\ell)$, the random variable $Y/2$ is uniform on $(0,\ell/2)$ — the right panel of Fig. 1.1 is exactly this statement: the horizontal uniform density is squeezed by the factor $1/2$ into the vertical one.

Fig. 1.2 — The two-break experiment and the resulting density of $X$, $f_X(x)=\ln(\ell/x)/\ell$ (drawn with $\ell=1$). The mean $\ell/4$ and variance $7\ell^2/144\approx0.048611\,\ell^2$ are derived in §§1.2 and 1.4 (L12 slide 2; B&T Example 4.17).
Example 1.3 (a coin whose bias is random; B&T Example 4.16). Because of manufacturing defects the probability of heads of a coin is itself a random variable $Y$, uniform on $[0,1]$. The coin is tossed $n$ times independently; $X$ is the number of heads. Find $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$.

Solution. Condition on $\{Y=y\}$. In that universe the tosses are $n$ independent Bernoulli trials with common success probability $y$, so $X$ is binomial with parameters $n$ and $y$ (G2 §1), whose mean is $ny$ (G2 §2). Thus $g(y)=\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]=ny$ and $$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=nY .$$ Note the shape of the answer: a random variable built out of $Y$ alone, with $X$ gone. With $n=10$ this is $10Y$, uniform on $[0,10]$.

Property (two rules you will use constantly; B&T §4.3).
  1. Pulling out what is known. For any function $h$, $\;\mathbb{E}\big[X\,h(Y)\,\big|\,Y\big]=h(Y)\,\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$. Reason: inside the universe $\{Y=y\}$ the quantity $h(y)$ is a constant, and constants come out of expectations (G2 §2).
  2. Independence collapses it. If $X$ and $Y$ are independent then $p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)=p_X(x)$ for every $y$ (G2 §4), so $g(y)=\mathbb{E}[X]$ for every $y$ and therefore $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]$ — a constant random variable. Conditioning on something irrelevant changes nothing.
  3. Linearity survives. $\mathbb{E}[aX_1+bX_2\mid Y]=a\,\mathbb{E}[X_1\mid Y]+b\,\mathbb{E}[X_2\mid Y]$, because linearity holds inside each conditional universe and the identity therefore holds value by value of $y$.
Gotcha — $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ is a function of $Y$, and only of $Y$. Whatever $X$ was, it has been integrated out. Two consequences that trip people up: (i) $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\,\big|\,Y\big]=\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$, since conditioning a function of $Y$ on $Y$ does nothing; (ii) $\mathbb{E}[Y\mid Y]=Y$, not $\mathbb{E}[Y]$. If your answer for $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ still contains the symbol $X$, you have not finished the computation.
Practice 1.1. Roll a fair six-sided die; let $X$ be the outcome and let $Y=1$ if $X$ is even and $Y=0$ if $X$ is odd. Write down $g(y)=\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$, the distribution of $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$, and verify $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]=\mathbb{E}[X]$.
Solution

Given $\{Y=0\}$ the outcome is uniform on $\{1,3,5\}$, each with conditional probability $1/3$, so $$g(0)=\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=0]=\tfrac13(1+3+5)=\tfrac13\cdot9=3 .$$ Given $\{Y=1\}$ the outcome is uniform on $\{2,4,6\}$, so $g(1)=\tfrac13(2+4+6)=\tfrac13\cdot12=4$ (pr1_EX_odd, pr1_EX_even).

Since $\mathbf{P}(Y=0)=\mathbf{P}(Y=1)=3/6=1/2$, $$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=\begin{cases}3,&\text{w.p. }1/2,\\ 4,&\text{w.p. }1/2,\end{cases}\qquad \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]=\tfrac12\cdot3+\tfrac12\cdot4=\tfrac72=3.5,$$ which equals $\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6}=\frac{21}{6}=3.5$ ✓ (pr1_EX).

Practice 1.2. Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent with $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu$. Simplify (a) $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$, (b) $\mathbb{E}[XY\mid Y]$, (c) $\mathbb{E}[XY]$, and (d) $\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)$.
Solution

(a) By rule 2 above, independence gives $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu$, a constant.

(b) Take $h(Y)=Y$ in rule 1: $\mathbb{E}[XY\mid Y]=Y\,\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=Y\mu=\mu Y$.

(c) Average (b): $\mathbb{E}[XY]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[XY\mid Y]\big]=\mathbb{E}[\mu Y]=\mu\,\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$ — the familiar product rule for independent variables (G2 §4), now obtained by conditioning.

(d) A constant random variable has variance $0$, so $\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)=0$. Anticipating §1.3: when $Y$ says nothing about $X$, the "between-group" part of the variance vanishes and $\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)]$.

1.2 The law of iterated expectations

Theorem (law of iterated expectations; L12 slide 2, B&T §4.3). For any random variables $X$ and $Y$ on the same experiment, provided $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is well defined and finite, $$\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]\;=\;\mathbb{E}[X].$$ The inner expectation is taken over $X$ with $Y$ held fixed; the outer one is taken over $Y$.

Proof, discrete case, every step. Write $g(y)=\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$, so that $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=g(Y)$. Apply the expected value rule (G2 §2) to the function $g$ of the random variable $Y$: $$\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]=\mathbb{E}[g(Y)]=\sum_y g(y)\,p_Y(y) =\sum_y \Big(\sum_x x\,p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)\Big)p_Y(y),$$ where the last equality substitutes the definition of $g$. Move $p_Y(y)$ inside the inner sum (it does not depend on $x$) and use the multiplication rule $p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)\,p_Y(y)=p_{X,Y}(x,y)$ from G2 §4: $$=\sum_y\sum_x x\,p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)p_Y(y)=\sum_y\sum_x x\,p_{X,Y}(x,y).$$ Both sums are over countably many terms and (under the stated finiteness) absolutely convergent, so they may be exchanged: $$=\sum_x x\sum_y p_{X,Y}(x,y)=\sum_x x\,p_X(x)=\mathbb{E}[X],$$ the middle step being the marginalization identity $\sum_y p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_X(x)$ (G2 §4) and the last the definition of $\mathbb{E}[X]$. $\blacksquare$

Proof, continuous case. Identical with sums replaced by integrals and PMFs by PDFs: $$\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(y)f_Y(y)\,dy =\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)f_Y(y)\,dx\,dy =\int\!\!\int x\,f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\,dy=\int x f_X(x)\,dx=\mathbb{E}[X],$$ using $f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)f_Y(y)=f_{X,Y}(x,y)$ (G3 §2), Fubini's theorem to exchange the order of integration, and $\int f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dy=f_X(x)$. The mixed case ($Y$ continuous, $X$ discrete, or vice versa) goes the same way with the corresponding Bayes-variation formulas of G3 §3. $\blacksquare$

Interpretation — three readings of the same equation.
  1. Divide and conquer. To find $\mathbb{E}[X]$, split the experiment into cases according to $Y$, compute the mean in each case, and average the case means with the case probabilities as weights. This is the total expectation theorem of G2 §3, and the theorem says the packaging as a random variable loses nothing.
  2. Weighted averaging of subgroup averages. In Example 1.1, the class average is the section averages weighted by section sizes — exactly $70=\frac13\cdot90+\frac23\cdot60$. The weights must be the sizes; the unweighted average $\frac{90+60}{2}=75$ is a different (and wrong) number.
  3. Forecast revisions have mean zero (B&T Example 4.19). If $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is a January forecast of annual sales $X$ and $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ is the revised forecast made in July after first-semester sales $Y$ are known, then the revision is $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]-\mathbb{E}[X]$, and $$\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]-\mathbb{E}[X]\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]-\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X]-\mathbb{E}[X]=0 .$$ You cannot forecast your own future forecast revision. If you expected the revision to be positive, your January forecast was too low to begin with.
Example 1.4 (the stick's mean, completed; L12 slide 2 — [DERIVATION GAP] filled). Continue Example 1.2 and compute $\mathbb{E}[X]$.

Solution. The slide writes $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[Y/2]=\ell/4$ without showing $\mathbb{E}[Y]$. Since the first break point is uniform on $[0,\ell]$, $Y\sim U(0,\ell)$ and (G3 §1, or by direct integration) $$\mathbb{E}[Y]=\int_0^{\ell}y\cdot\frac1\ell\,dy=\frac1\ell\left[\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_0^{\ell}=\frac1\ell\cdot\frac{\ell^2}{2}=\frac{\ell}{2}.$$ Then by the law of iterated expectations and linearity, $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{Y}{2}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y]}{2}=\frac{\ell/2}{2}=\frac{\ell}{4}.$$ Check without conditioning. Example 1.5 (the coin with random bias, mean; B&T Example 4.16). Continue Example 1.3: $Y\sim U(0,1)$, $X\mid\{Y=y\}\sim\text{Bin}(n,y)$.

Solution. $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=nY$, and $\mathbb{E}[Y]=\frac{0+1}{2}=\frac12$, so $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[nY]=n\,\mathbb{E}[Y]=\frac{n}{2}.$$ With $n=10$ that is $\mathbb{E}[X]=5$ (coin_EX); two million simulated experiments give $4.999161$ (coin_EX_mc). Sensible: a coin of unknown bias, symmetric about $1/2$, behaves on average like a fair one.

Gotcha — average the right thing. $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]=\mathbb{E}[X]$ requires the outer average to be over the distribution of $Y$. Two frequent errors: (i) averaging the values $g(y)$ with equal weights instead of weights $p_Y(y)$ — in Example 1.1 that gives $75$ instead of $70$; (ii) using $\mathbb{E}[g(Y)]=g(\mathbb{E}[Y])$. That last one is legal only when $g$ is affine. It is fine for the stick ($g(y)=y/2$, affine), which is why $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[Y]/2$ works; it is not fine in general, and §1.3 is essentially the study of the gap $\mathbb{E}[g(Y)]\ne g(\mathbb{E}[Y])$ for $g(y)=y^2$.
Practice 1.3. Let $N$ be uniform on $\{1,2,3,4\}$, and given $\{N=n\}$ let $X$ be uniform on $\{1,2,\dots,n\}$. Find $\mathbb{E}[X]$.
Solution

Inside the universe $\{N=n\}$, $X$ is uniform on $n$ consecutive integers starting at $1$, whose mean is the midpoint $\frac{1+n}{2}$. So $g(n)=\frac{n+1}{2}$ and $$\mathbb{E}[X\mid N]=\frac{N+1}{2}.$$ Now $\mathbb{E}[N]=\frac{1+2+3+4}{4}=\frac{10}{4}=2.5$ (pr2_EN), so by the law of iterated expectations and linearity $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{N+1}{2}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[N]+1}{2}=\frac{2.5+1}{2}=\frac{3.5}{2}=1.75 .$$ Check by brute force. Building the joint PMF $p_{N,X}(n,x)=\frac14\cdot\frac1n$ for $1\le x\le n$ and summing gives total probability $1.000000$ and $\mathbb{E}[X]=1.750000$ ✓ (pr2_EX_brute).

1.3 Conditional variance and the law of total variance

The same "make it a random variable" move applies to the variance. Recall from G2 §3 that inside the conditional universe $\{Y=y\}$ every notion of probability has a conditional counterpart; in particular the variance of $X$ in that universe is the expected squared deviation from that universe's own mean.

Definition (conditional variance; L12 slide 3, B&T §4.3). $$\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)\;=\;\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(X-\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]\big)^2\;\Big|\;Y=y\Big],$$ a number for each $y$. Writing $h(y)=\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)$, the random variable $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y):=h(Y)$ takes the value $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)$ whenever $Y=y$. Note the deviation is measured from the conditional mean $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$, not from $\mathbb{E}[X]$.
Theorem (law of total variance; L12 slide 3, B&T §4.3). $$\operatorname{var}(X)\;=\;\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]}_{\text{average variability }\textbf{within}\text{ groups}}\;+\;\underbrace{\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)}_{\text{variability }\textbf{between}\text{ groups}} .$$

Proof, following L12 slide 3 with the two missing steps supplied ([DERIVATION GAP] filled). Four lines, labelled as on the slide.

(a) The variance identity from G2 §3, valid for any random variable $W$ with finite second moment: $\operatorname{var}(W)=\mathbb{E}[W^2]-(\mathbb{E}[W])^2$.

(b) Apply (a) inside the universe $\{Y=y\}$ — everything there is an honest probability law, so the identity holds verbatim: $$\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)=\mathbb{E}[X^2\mid Y=y]-\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]\big)^2 .$$ This is an identity between numbers, true for every $y$; replacing $y$ by $Y$ makes it an identity between random variables, $$\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)=\mathbb{E}[X^2\mid Y]-\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)^2 .$$

(c) Take the expectation of (b) and use linearity of expectation: $$\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}[X^2\mid Y]\Big]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)^2\Big].$$ The first term is where the slide skips a step: apply the law of iterated expectations of §1.2 to the random variable $X^2$ in place of $X$, which is legitimate because that law holds for any random variable with a finite mean. Hence $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X^2\mid Y]\big]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ and $$\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)^2\Big].$$

(d) Now apply (a) to the random variable $W=\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ — legitimate precisely because §1.1 established that it is a random variable: $$\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)^2\Big]-\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]\Big)^2 =\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)^2\Big]-\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2,$$ the second equality being the law of iterated expectations again (this is the second step the slide leaves implicit).

Add (c) and (d). The term $\mathbb{E}\big[(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y])^2\big]$ appears once with a minus sign and once with a plus sign, so it cancels: $$\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]+\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2=\operatorname{var}(X)$$ by (a) applied to $X$ itself. $\blacksquare$

Interpretation — decomposing uncertainty (L12 slide 5). Think of $Y$ as sorting the population into groups.
  • $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)$ measures how spread out $X$ is inside group $y$. Averaging over groups, $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)]$ is the within-group (or unexplained, or residual) variance — the uncertainty that would remain even after you were told which group you are in.
  • $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$ is the level of group $y$. Its variance $\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y])$ is the between-group (or explained) variance — the uncertainty that comes purely from not knowing which group you are in.
  • Total uncertainty is exactly their sum. Both pieces are variances, hence $\ge 0$, so $\operatorname{var}(X)\ge\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)\ge\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y])$: conditioning can never increase the average variance. Observing $Y$ removes the between-group share and nothing more.
This is the probabilistic form of the analysis of variance; the ratio $\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y])/\operatorname{var}(X)$ is the fraction of the variance "explained" by $Y$.
Example 1.6 (two class sections, variance; L12 slides 4–5). Continue Example 1.1. Suppose in addition that the within-section mean squared deviations are $$\frac1{10}\sum_{i=1}^{10}(x_i-90)^2=10,\qquad \frac1{20}\sum_{i=11}^{30}(x_i-60)^2=20 .$$ Find $\operatorname{var}(X)$.

Step 1 — the conditional variances. Given $\{Y=1\}$ the student is uniform among the $10$ students of section 1 whose mean is $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=1]=90$, so by definition $$\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=1)=\sum_{i=1}^{10}\frac1{10}(x_i-90)^2=10,$$ and likewise $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=2)=20$. The displayed sums are the conditional variances; that is why the data were given in that form.

Fig. 1.3 — The law of total variance for Example 1.6: within-group variance $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)]=50/3\approx16.67$ plus between-group variance $\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y])=200$ equals $\operatorname{var}(X)=650/3\approx216.67$ (L12 slides 4–5).
Gotcha — four ways to garble the law.
  1. The two terms are not interchangeable. $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)]$ takes the expectation of a variance; $\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y])$ takes the variance of an expectation. In Example 1.6 they are $16.67$ and $200$ — swapping them is not a small error.
  2. Variances of the conditional distributions do not simply average to $\operatorname{var}(X)$. Writing $\operatorname{var}(X)=\sum_y \operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)p_Y(y)$ drops the between-group term entirely; it is correct only when all the group means coincide.
  3. There is no "law of total standard deviation". The decomposition is additive in variances, never in standard deviations: $\sqrt{216.67}=14.72\ne\sqrt{16.67}+\sqrt{200}=4.08+14.14=18.22$.
  4. $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)$ is measured about $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$, not about $\mathbb{E}[X]$. Using the grand mean inside the groups double-counts the between-group part and inflates the answer.
Recipe (computing means and variances by conditioning; L12 slides 2–3, B&T §4.3).
  1. Choose the conditioning variable $Y$. Pick the quantity whose value makes the description of $X$ simple — the stage-one outcome, the group label, the number of trials, the unknown parameter.
  2. Work inside the universe $\{Y=y\}$. Identify the conditional distribution of $X$ there, then read off $g(y)=\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$ and $h(y)=\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)$ as formulas in $y$. Usually you recognize a named distribution and quote its mean and variance.
  3. Promote $y$ to $Y$: $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=g(Y)$ and $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)=h(Y)$. Both are functions of $Y$ alone; if $X$ still appears, redo step 2.
  4. Mean: $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[g(Y)]$, computed with the expected value rule over the distribution of $Y$.
  5. Variance: $\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]+\operatorname{var}(g(Y))$. Both terms need only moments of $Y$; note $\operatorname{var}(g(Y))$ typically requires $\mathbb{E}[Y^2]$, so compute $\mathbb{E}[Y]$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y^2]$ once and reuse them.
  6. Check: both terms are nonnegative, so $\operatorname{var}(X)$ must exceed each of them; and if $g$ is constant the between-group term must vanish.

Step 0 — is it a density? Total area $=\frac13\cdot1+\frac23\cdot1=\frac13+\frac23=1$ ✓ (q_area_check).

Step 2 — the conditional densities. Step 3 — conditional means and variances. For $U(a,b)$ the mean is the midpoint $(a+b)/2$ and the variance is $(b-a)^2/12$ (G3 §1): $$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=1]=\frac{0+1}{2}=\frac12,\qquad \mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=2]=\frac{1+2}{2}=\frac32,$$ $$\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=1)=\frac{(1-0)^2}{12}=\frac1{12},\qquad \operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=2)=\frac{(2-1)^2}{12}=\frac1{12}.$$ (q_EX_Y1, q_EX_Y2, q_var_Y1, q_var_Y2.)

Gotcha — two traps in Step 2 and Step 6 of Example 1.7.
  1. Conditioning on a positive-probability event rescales the density; it does not reshape it. The rule is $f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid 1)=f_X(x)/\mathbf{P}(Y=1)$ on the event, and $0$ off it — divide by the probability of the event, nothing else. The frequent error is to renormalize by the interval length, or to keep the original height $1/3$ and call it a conditional density (it integrates to $1/3$, not $1$), or to divide by $\mathbf{P}(Y=2)$ on the piece belonging to $Y=1$. Here the two rescalings, $\frac{1/3}{1/3}$ and $\frac{2/3}{2/3}$, both give height $1$ — a coincidence of this particular density, not a rule.
  2. Equal conditional variances are a coincidence of equal interval widths. In Example 1.7 both pieces have width $1$, so $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=1)=\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=2)=1/12$ and the weighted average $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)]$ comes out to $1/12$ whatever $\mathbf{P}(Y=1)$ is. Do not read that as "the weights don't matter": Example 1.8, immediately below, is the same problem with widths $1$ and $2$, where the conditional variances are $1/12$ and $1/3$ and the within term $\frac5{24}$ genuinely depends on the weights. Only $\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y])$ ever depends on the conditional means; the within term depends on the conditional spreads, and both are weighted by $p_Y$.
Example 1.8 (the textbook's variant; B&T Example 4.21). The book poses the same question with a different picture (its Figure 4.13): $f_X(x)=\frac12$ on $[0,1]$ and $f_X(x)=\frac14$ on $(1,3]$, with $Y=1$ and $Y=2$ labelling the two pieces.

Then $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cdot2=\tfrac14+1=\frac54 ,$$ $$\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)=\tfrac12\Big(\tfrac12-\tfrac54\Big)^2+\tfrac12\Big(2-\tfrac54\Big)^2=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac9{16}+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac9{16}=\frac9{16},$$ $$\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac1{12}+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac4{12}=\frac{1}{24}+\frac{4}{24}=\frac5{24},$$ $$\operatorname{var}(X)=\frac5{24}+\frac9{16}=\frac{10}{48}+\frac{27}{48}=\frac{37}{48}\approx0.770833 ,$$ matching the book (bt_EX, bt_varE, bt_Evar, bt_varX). Example 1.10 (the coin with random bias, variance; B&T Example 4.16 continued). $Y\sim U(0,1)$, $X\mid\{Y=y\}\sim\text{Bin}(n,y)$. Find $\operatorname{var}(X)$.

Within. Between. $\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)=\operatorname{var}(nY)=n^2\operatorname{var}(Y)=\frac{n^2}{12}$.

Example 1.11 (rec12 P1 — the correlation coefficient is unchanged by an affine change of units with positive scale). Show that $\rho(aX+b,\,Y)=\rho(X,Y)$.
Solution

Definitions (B&T §4.2, G3 §4). $\operatorname{cov}(U,V)=\mathbb{E}\big[(U-\mathbb{E}[U])(V-\mathbb{E}[V])\big]$ and, when both variances are positive, $$\rho(U,V)=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(U,V)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(U)\operatorname{var}(V)}} .$$ Here $a\ne0$ and $b$ are constants: $a$ is a change of scale (points out of $105$ instead of out of $100$), $b$ a shift of origin (a curve added to everyone's mark).

Preliminary — the [SOLUTION GAP] the official solution uses without proof. $\mathbb{E}[aX+b]=a\mathbb{E}[X]+b$ by linearity, so the deviation of $aX+b$ from its own mean is $$(aX+b)-\mathbb{E}[aX+b]=aX+b-a\mathbb{E}[X]-b=a\big(X-\mathbb{E}[X]\big),$$ the $b$'s cancelling exactly. Squaring and taking expectations, $$\operatorname{var}(aX+b)=\mathbb{E}\Big[a^2\big(X-\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2\Big]=a^2\operatorname{var}(X).$$

Main computation. Substituting $U=aX+b$, $V=Y$: $$\rho(aX+b,Y)=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(aX+b,Y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(aX+b)\operatorname{var}(Y)}} =\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[a(X-\mathbb{E}[X])(Y-\mathbb{E}[Y])\big]}{\sqrt{a^2\operatorname{var}(X)\operatorname{var}(Y)}} =\frac{a\,\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)}{|a|\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(X)\operatorname{var}(Y)}} =\operatorname{sign}(a)\,\rho(X,Y).$$ Each step: the numerator used the deviation identity above and pulled the constant $a$ out of the expectation; the denominator used $\operatorname{var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{var}(X)$ and $\sqrt{a^2}=|a|$.

[SOURCE TYPO?] The official solution writes $\sqrt{a^2\operatorname{var}(X)\operatorname{var}(Y)}=a\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(X)\operatorname{var}(Y)}$, i.e. $\sqrt{a^2}=a$. That holds only for $a>0$. For $a>0$ the claim $\rho(aX+b,Y)=\rho(X,Y)$ is exactly right; for $a<0$ the correct statement is $\rho(aX+b,Y)=-\rho(X,Y)$, since reversing the direction of a scale reverses the sign of every association. (The first line of the official solution also writes $\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(aX+b)(\operatorname{var}(Y))}$ with a redundant pair of parentheses; harmless.)

Numerical illustration. Five students with homework scores $(70,85,90,60,95)$ and exam scores $(65,80,88,55,92)$ have $\operatorname{cov}=182$, $\operatorname{var}(X)=170$, $\operatorname{var}(Y)=195.6$ and $\rho=0.998073$. Rescaling the homework to a $105$-point scale and subtracting a $3$-point handicap, $a=1.05$, $b=-3$, leaves $\rho=0.998073$ unchanged; using $a=-1.05$ instead gives $-0.998073$ (corr_rho, corr_rho_affine, corr_rho_negative_a).

Why this matters. $\rho$ is dimensionless: it is unchanged by the units in which either variable is recorded. Covariance is not — measuring in points instead of percentage points multiplies it by $a$. That is why $\rho$, not $\operatorname{cov}$, is the number quoted when comparing associations measured in different units.

Example 1.12 (rec12 P2 — Romeo and Juliet: the PDF of a difference of exponentials). Romeo and Juliet are each late by independent amounts $X$ and $Y$, both exponential with parameter $\lambda$. Let $Z=X-Y$ be the amount by which Romeo is later than Juliet. (a) Find $f_Z$ via the CDF. (b) Find $f_Z$ by the total probability theorem.
Solution

Setup. $f_X(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x\ge0$ and $0$ for $x<0$; same for $Y$; independence gives $f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\lambda e^{-\lambda y}$ on the first quadrant. $Z$ can be any real number.

(a) CDF method, case $z\ge0$. The event is $$\{Z\le z\}=\{X-Y\le z\}=\{X\le Y+z\},$$ which in the $(x,y)$ plane is the part of the first quadrant lying above the line $y=x-z$ (Fig. 1.6a). Integrate $y$ from $0$ to $\infty$ and, for each $y$, $x$ from $0$ to $y+z$ (positive because $y\ge0$ and $z\ge0$): $$F_Z(z)=\int_0^{\infty}\lambda e^{-\lambda y}\left(\int_0^{y+z}\lambda e^{-\lambda x}dx\right)dy =\int_0^{\infty}\lambda e^{-\lambda y}\Big(1-e^{-\lambda(y+z)}\Big)dy,$$ where the inner integral is the exponential CDF, $\int_0^{t}\lambda e^{-\lambda x}dx=1-e^{-\lambda t}$ with $t=y+z$.

Filling the [SOLUTION GAP] — the source jumps straight to an antiderivative. Example 1.13 (rec12 P3 — polar coordinates of a standard normal pair; B&T Problem 4.16, p. 248). Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent standard normal random variables and write $X=R\cos\Theta$, $Y=R\sin\Theta$ with $R\ge0$ and $\Theta\in[0,2\pi]$. Find (a) $f_R$, (b) $f_\Theta$, (c) $f_{R,\Theta}$, and show $R$ and $\Theta$ are independent.
Solution

The joint density in polar form — [SOLUTION GAP] filled. By independence, $$f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-y^2/2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}.$$ Substituting $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ and using $\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$ gives $x^2+y^2=r^2$, so $$f_{X,Y}(r\cos\theta,\;r\sin\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-r^2/2},$$ which depends on $r$ only — the density is circularly symmetric (Fig. 1.7a). The area element in polar coordinates is $dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta$; the extra factor $r$ is the Jacobian and is the whole content of the computation.

(a) The radius. The event $\{R\le r\}$ is the disc of radius $r$: $$F_R(r)=\int_0^{r}\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(r')^2/2}\,d\theta\;r'\,dr' =\left(\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\right)\int_0^{r}r'e^{-(r')^2/2}\,dr' =1\cdot\int_0^{r}r'e^{-(r')^2/2}\,dr' .$$ Substitute $u=(r')^2/2$, so $du=r'\,dr'$ and the limits $r'=0,r$ become $u=0,r^2/2$: $$F_R(r)=\int_0^{r^2/2}e^{-u}du=\big[-e^{-u}\big]_0^{r^2/2}=-e^{-r^2/2}+1=1-e^{-r^2/2},\qquad r\ge0$$ (and $F_R(r)=0$ for $r<0$) — the evaluation the source skips. Differentiating by the chain rule, $$f_R(r)=\frac{d}{dr}\Big(1-e^{-r^2/2}\Big)=-e^{-r^2/2}\cdot\Big(-\frac{2r}{2}\Big)=r\,e^{-r^2/2},\qquad r\ge0 .$$ This is the Rayleigh density. [SOURCE TYPO?] the official solution writes this derivative as $(-1/2)(2r)(-e^{-r^2/2})$, which is numerically right but grouped as though one were differentiating $-e^{-r^2/2}$.

(b) The angle. Slicing the plane by angle instead of radius, $$F_\Theta(\theta)=\int_0^{\theta}\!\!\int_0^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-r^2/2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta' =\left(\int_0^{\theta}\frac{d\theta'}{2\pi}\right)\int_0^{\infty}re^{-r^2/2}dr=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\int_0^{\infty}e^{-u}du=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\cdot 1=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}$$ for $0\le\theta\le2\pi$ (same substitution $u=r^2/2$), with $F_\Theta=0$ below $0$ and $1$ above $2\pi$. Differentiating, $f_\Theta(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}$ on $[0,2\pi]$: the angle is uniform.

(c) The joint density. $$F_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)=\int_0^{\theta}\!\!\int_0^{r}\frac{1}{2\pi}r'e^{-(r')^2/2}dr'\,d\theta' =\int_0^{\theta}\frac{1}{2\pi}\Big(1-e^{-r^2/2}\Big)d\theta'=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\Big(1-e^{-r^2/2}\Big)$$ for $r\ge0$ and $0\le\theta\le2\pi$. [SOURCE TYPO?] the source labels this branch "$r\ge0,\ \theta>2\pi$"; the case list immediately below it (and the derivation) show the branch is the one for $0\le\theta\le2\pi$. Differentiating once in each variable, $$f_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)=\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r\,\partial\theta}F_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,r\,e^{-r^2/2}.$$

Conclusion — the thing the problem actually asked, which the official solution never states ([SOLUTION GAP]). Comparing with (a) and (b), $$f_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}re^{-r^2/2}=\Big(re^{-r^2/2}\Big)\cdot\Big(\frac{1}{2\pi}\Big)=f_R(r)\,f_\Theta(\theta)\quad\text{for all }r\ge0,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi,$$ so by the definition of independence for continuous random variables (G3 §2), $R$ and $\Theta$ are independent. A grid check of the two sides agrees to machine precision (ray_factor_maxerr $=0.000000$).

Numbers. $\int_0^\infty re^{-r^2/2}dr=1.000000$ ✓; $\mathbb{E}[R]=\sqrt{\pi/2}\approx1.253314$; $\mathbb{E}[R^2]=2$ exactly, so $\operatorname{var}(R)=2-\frac{\pi}{2}\approx0.429204$; the median radius is $\sqrt{2\ln2}\approx1.177410$ and $\mathbf{P}(R\le1)=1-e^{-1/2}\approx0.393469$. Two million simulated normal pairs give $\mathbb{E}[R]=1.253245$ and $\mathbb{E}[R^2]=1.999536$ ✓ (ray_ER, ray_varR, ray_ER_mc).

Why anyone cares (the recitation's closing note). $R^2$ has CDF $\mathbf{P}(R^2\le t)=\mathbf{P}(R\le\sqrt t)=1-e^{-t/2}$, i.e. $R^2$ is exponential with parameter $\lambda=1/2$ — mean $2$, matching $\mathbb{E}[R^2]=2$ above. So to generate a standard normal pair: draw $T$ exponential$(1/2)$ and $\Theta$ uniform on $[0,2\pi]$ independently, and set $X=\sqrt T\cos\Theta$, $Y=\sqrt T\sin\Theta$. This is the Box–Muller method, and it exists because the normal CDF has no closed-form inverse, so the usual inverse-CDF trick of G3 §4 is unavailable. Example 1.14 (rec12 P4 — the Schwarz inequality; B&T Problem 4.20, p. 250). Show that $\big(\mathbb{E}[XY]\big)^2\le\mathbb{E}[X^2]\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2]$ for any random variables $X,Y$.
Solution

Idea. Try to approximate $X$ by a multiple $\alpha Y$ of $Y$ and see how good the best approximation can be. Define, for a real parameter $\alpha$, $$J(\alpha)=\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\alpha Y)^2\big].$$ Being the expectation of a square, $J(\alpha)\ge0$ for every $\alpha$ — that single fact is the whole inequality.

Expand ([SOLUTION GAP]: the source uses this expansion silently). $(X-\alpha Y)^2=X^2-2\alpha XY+\alpha^2Y^2$, so by linearity of expectation $$J(\alpha)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-2\alpha\,\mathbb{E}[XY]+\alpha^2\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2],$$ a quadratic in $\alpha$ with leading coefficient $\mathbb{E}[Y^2]$.

Minimize ([SOLUTION GAP]: the differentiation is skipped in the source). Assume $\mathbb{E}[Y^2]>0$. Then $$\frac{dJ}{d\alpha}=-2\,\mathbb{E}[XY]+2\alpha\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2]=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \alpha^{*}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[XY]}{\mathbb{E}[Y^2]},$$ and $\frac{d^2J}{d\alpha^2}=2\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2]>0$, so this stationary point is the unique minimum (the parabola of the recitation's figure).

Evaluate at the minimum. $$J(\alpha^{*})=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-2\frac{\big(\mathbb{E}[XY]\big)^2}{\mathbb{E}[Y^2]}+\frac{\big(\mathbb{E}[XY]\big)^2}{\big(\mathbb{E}[Y^2]\big)^2}\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2] =\mathbb{E}[X^2]-2\frac{\big(\mathbb{E}[XY]\big)^2}{\mathbb{E}[Y^2]}+\frac{\big(\mathbb{E}[XY]\big)^2}{\mathbb{E}[Y^2]} =\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\frac{\big(\mathbb{E}[XY]\big)^2}{\mathbb{E}[Y^2]} .$$ Since $J(\alpha^{*})\ge0$, multiply through by $\mathbb{E}[Y^2]>0$: $$\mathbb{E}[X^2]\mathbb{E}[Y^2]-\big(\mathbb{E}[XY]\big)^2\ge0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \big(\mathbb{E}[XY]\big)^2\le\mathbb{E}[X^2]\mathbb{E}[Y^2].$$

The degenerate case. If $\mathbb{E}[Y^2]=0$ then $Y=0$ with probability $1$, so $\mathbb{E}[XY]=0$ and both sides are $0$: the inequality holds with equality. [SOURCE TYPO?] the solution phrases the condition as "$Y\ne0$" / "$Y=0$" about the random variable itself; the relevant condition is $\mathbb{E}[Y^2]\ne0$, i.e. $Y$ is not almost surely zero.

Equality. $J(\alpha^*)=0$ means $\mathbb{E}[(X-\alpha^*Y)^2]=0$, i.e. $X=\alpha^*Y$ with probability $1$: equality holds exactly when $X$ is a deterministic multiple of $Y$.

Numerical illustration. Take $X$ uniform on $\{-1,0,1,2\}$ and $Y=X^2-1$. Then $\mathbb{E}[XY]=1.5$, $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=1.5$, $\mathbb{E}[Y^2]=2.5$, so $\big(\mathbb{E}[XY]\big)^2=2.25\le 3.75=\mathbb{E}[X^2]\mathbb{E}[Y^2]$ ✓, with $\alpha^*=1.5/2.5=0.6$ and $J(\alpha^*)=1.5-2.25/2.5=0.6>0$ (strict, since $Y$ is not a multiple of $X$). Replacing $Y$ by $3X$ makes both sides $20.25$ — equality, as predicted (sch_lhs, sch_rhs, sch_Jmin, sch_eq_lhs).

Why it belongs here. Apply the inequality to the centered variables $X-\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $Y-\mathbb{E}[Y]$: $$\big(\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)\big)^2\le\operatorname{var}(X)\operatorname{var}(Y)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |\rho(X,Y)|\le1,$$ which is the bound quoted without proof in G3 §4 and used in Example 1.11. The minimizing $\alpha^*$ is also the first appearance of linear least-squares estimation: $\alpha^*Y$ is the best multiple of $Y$ for predicting $X$, the same optimization that $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ solves among all functions of $Y$ (G6).

Practice 1.8. For the Rayleigh radius $R$ of Example 1.13, use the law of total variance with $Y=\Theta$ to predict $\operatorname{var}(R)$ without any new integration, then state what the calculation illustrates.
Solution

$R$ and $\Theta$ are independent, so inside every universe $\{\Theta=\theta\}$ the distribution of $R$ is its unconditional one: $\mathbb{E}[R\mid\Theta]=\mathbb{E}[R]=\sqrt{\pi/2}$, a constant, and $\operatorname{var}(R\mid\Theta)=\operatorname{var}(R)$, also a constant. Therefore $$\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[R\mid\Theta]\big)=0,\qquad \mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(R\mid\Theta)\big]=\operatorname{var}(R),$$ and the law returns $\operatorname{var}(R)=\operatorname{var}(R)+0$ — true but empty. Numerically $\operatorname{var}(R)=2-\pi/2\approx0.429204$ (ray_varR), obtained from $\mathbb{E}[R^2]=2$ and $\mathbb{E}[R]=\sqrt{\pi/2}$.

What it illustrates. Conditioning only helps when the conditioning variable actually carries information: an independent $Y$ puts all of $\operatorname{var}(X)$ into the within-group term and none into the between-group term. Choosing $Y$ well — the stage that makes $X$ simple — is the whole skill in the Recipe of §1.3.

§1 cheatsheet.
  • Number vs. random variable. $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]=g(y)$ is a number; $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=g(Y)$ is a random variable, a function of $Y$ alone. Same for $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)=h(y)$ and $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)=h(Y)$.
  • Law of iterated expectations. $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]=\mathbb{E}[X]$. Proof: expected value rule, multiplication rule, exchange of sums/integrals, marginalization.
  • Law of total variance. $\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]+\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)$ = within-group + between-group. Both terms $\ge0$, so conditioning never raises the average variance.
  • Useful rules. $\mathbb{E}[Xh(Y)\mid Y]=h(Y)\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$; independence $\Rightarrow\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]$ and the between term vanishes; $\operatorname{var}(aY+b)=a^2\operatorname{var}(Y)$.
  • Worked totals. Two sections: $\mathbb{E}[X]=70$, $\operatorname{var}(X)=\frac{50}{3}+200=\frac{650}{3}\approx216.67$.
