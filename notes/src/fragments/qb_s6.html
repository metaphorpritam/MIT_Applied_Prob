Q115. ★ · Two bounds from mean and variance · G6 §1.1–1.2

A server's weekly downtime $X$, in hours, is nonnegative with $\mathbb{E}[X]=6$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)=9$. Nothing else about its distribution is known. (a) Bound $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 15)$ using Markov. (b) Bound the same probability using Chebyshev. (c) How many standard deviations above the mean is $15$, and does your Chebyshev answer agree with the $k$-sigma form $1/k^2$?

Solution

Setup. $\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]=6$, $\sigma^2=\operatorname{var}(X)=9$, so $\sigma=\sqrt{9}=3$. The threshold is $a=15$.

(a) Markov. $X\ge 0$ and $a=15>0$, so the hypothesis of Markov's inequality is met and $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge 15)\;\le\;\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{15}=\frac{6}{15}=0.4 .$$

(b) Chebyshev. Chebyshev bounds a two-sided event, so first embed the one-sided one. If $X\ge 15$ then $X-6\ge 9$, hence $|X-6|\ge 9$, so $\{X\ge15\}\subseteq\{|X-6|\ge 9\}$ and monotonicity of probability gives $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge 15)\;\le\;\mathbf{P}(|X-6|\ge 9)\;\le\;\frac{\sigma^2}{9^2} =\frac{9}{81}=0.111111 .$$ Chebyshev is tighter than Markov by a factor $0.4/0.111111=3.6$, and the extra hypothesis paying for that is one number, the variance.

(c) In sigma units. $(15-6)/3=3$, so $15$ is exactly $k=3$ standard deviations above the mean, and the $k$-sigma form gives $1/k^2=1/9=0.111111$ — the same number, as it must be, since $c=k\sigma$ is what was substituted in (b).

Answer. $\;\mathbf{P}(X\ge15)\le 0.4$ (Markov), $\;\le 0.111111$ (Chebyshev); $15$ is $3\sigma$ above the mean.

The tempting wrong answer. Writing $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 15)\le \sigma^2/15^2=9/225=0.04$. That is a correct bound on $\mathbf{P}(|X-6|\ge 15)$ — a strictly smaller event, requiring a deviation of $15$ rather than $9$ — so it does not bound the event asked for. Chebyshev's $c$ is a distance from the mean, never a raw threshold.

Q116. ★ · When Chebyshev is worse than Markov · G6 §1.2

True or false, with reasons: "For a nonnegative random variable, the Chebyshev bound on $\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)$ is always at least as tight as the Markov bound, since it uses more information." Use the $X$ of Q115 ($\mu=6$, $\sigma^2=9$) and find the threshold $a$ above which Chebyshev actually is the better of the two.

Solution

The claim is false. More information does not automatically mean a tighter number: the two inequalities bound different quantities and their bounds cross.

A counterexample. Take $a=7$, barely above the mean. Markov gives $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge 7)\le \frac{6}{7}=0.857143 ,$$ while Chebyshev, via $\{X\ge7\}\subseteq\{|X-6|\ge1\}$, gives $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge 7)\le\frac{9}{1^2}=9 ,$$ which is vacuous — it says a probability is at most $9$, i.e. nothing at all. Markov wins outright here.

Where they cross. The two bounds are $\mu/a=6/a$ and $\sigma^2/(a-\mu)^2=9/(a-6)^2$. Setting them equal, $6(a-6)^2=9a$, i.e. $$6a^2-72a+216=9a\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 6a^2-81a+216=0 .$$ Its roots are $a=3.657671$ and $a=9.842329$. Only the second exceeds the mean and is therefore relevant; at that point both bounds equal $0.609612$. For $a>9.842329$ Chebyshev is the tighter, for $\mu<a<9.842329$ Markov is. Check just past the crossing, at $a=10$: Markov gives $6/10=0.6$, Chebyshev $9/16=0.5625$ — Chebyshev now leads, and by Q115 the lead grows to $3.6\times$ at $a=15$.

Answer. $\;$False; for this $X$ Chebyshev only overtakes Markov for $a>9.842329$ (both bounds equal $0.609612$ there).

What the reasoning behind the false claim gets wrong. Chebyshev spends its variance on a symmetric event, half of which ($X\le \mu-c$) is irrelevant to a one-sided question; near the mean that waste swamps the extra information. The right summary is "Chebyshev dominates in the tail", not "always".

Q117. ★★ · Bounds versus the true tail · G6 §1.1–1.2

The number of hardware faults a machine suffers in a year is $X\sim\text{Poisson}(4)$, so $\mathbb{E}[X]=\operatorname{var}(X)=4$. Bound $\mathbf{P}(X\ge10)$ by Markov and by Chebyshev, compute the exact value, and report each bound as a multiple of the truth. Repeat at the threshold $20$ and say what the comparison shows about where bounds are useful.

Solution

Threshold $10$ — the two bounds. $X\ge0$, so Markov applies: $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge10)\le\frac{4}{10}=0.4 .$$ For Chebyshev, $X\ge 10$ forces $X-4\ge 6$, so $\{X\ge10\}\subseteq\{|X-4|\ge6\}$ and $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge10)\le\frac{4}{6^2}=\frac{4}{36}=0.111111 .$$

The truth. Summing the Poisson PMF, $\mathbf{P}(X\ge10)=1-\sum_{k=0}^{9}e^{-4}4^k/k!=0.008132$ (four million simulated years give $0.008145$ — agreement to three decimals).

Looseness. $0.4/0.008132=49.19$ and $0.111111/0.008132=13.66$: Markov overstates the truth by a factor of $49$, Chebyshev by $14$.

Threshold $20$. Markov: $4/20=0.2$. Chebyshev: $\{X\ge20\}\subseteq\{|X-4|\ge16\}$, so $4/16^2=4/256=0.015625$. Exact: $\mathbf{P}(X\ge20)=1.020052\times10^{-8}$. Now Chebyshev overstates by $0.015625/1.020052\times10^{-8}=1.53\times10^{6}$.

What it shows. Both bounds decay polynomially in the threshold ($1/a$ and $1/(a-\mu)^2$) while a Poisson tail decays faster than exponentially. So the further into the tail you go, the worse the ratio — the bounds are least informative exactly where tail probabilities matter most. They remain the only legal statements when all you know is a mean and a variance — but neither displayed number is the best such statement. Over the class of all nonnegative $X$ with mean $4$ and variance $4$, the sharp value of $\mathbf{P}(X\ge10)$ is the one-sided (Cantelli) bound of Q118, $$\sup_X\ \mathbf{P}(X\ge10)=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+(10-\mu)^2}=\frac{4}{4+36}=0.1 ,$$ attained by the two-point law $\mathbf{P}(X=10)=0.1$, $\mathbf{P}(X=10/3)=0.9$ (mean $0.1(10)+0.9(3.333333)=4$ ✓, variance $0.1(36)+0.9(4/9)=4$ ✓). So both bounds above are strictly conservative for this event: no admissible distribution reaches either $0.4$ or $0.111111$.

Answer. $\;$Markov $0.4$, Chebyshev $0.111111$, exact $0.008132$ (factors $49.19$ and $13.66$); at $20$ the factors become $1.96\times10^{7}$ and $1.53\times10^{6}$.

The tempting wrong answer. Concluding "the bounds are wrong / useless" — or, at the other extreme, "they are tight, so some distribution in the class attains them". Both are wrong. They are valid but not sharp here. Chebyshev's $0.111111$ is attained only by the two-sided event $|X-4|\ge6$, which would need mass at $X=-2$ — impossible for a count. Markov's $0.4$ is attained only by $\mathbf{P}(X=10)=0.4$, $\mathbf{P}(X=0)=0.6$, whose variance is $0.4(36)+0.6(16)=24$, not $4$, so pinning the variance rules it out too. The sharp value over the class is $0.1$, and the Poisson's $0.008132$ is far below even that.

Q118. ★★★ · Derivation: the one-sided Chebyshev · G6 §1.1–1.2

Let $X$ have mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, both finite, and let $c>0$. Prove the one-sided Chebyshev inequality $$\mathbf{P}(X-\mu\ge c)\;\le\;\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+c^2}$$ by applying Markov's inequality to $(X-\mu+t)^2$ for a well-chosen $t>0$. Then evaluate it for the Poisson$(4)$ of Q117 at $c=6$ and say how much it improved matters — and how much it did not.

Solution

Step 1 — build a nonnegative random variable. Markov needs a nonnegative quantity, and $X-\mu$ is not one; but $Y=(X-\mu+t)^2$ is nonnegative for every constant $t$. Fix any $t>0$.

Step 2 — relate the events. If $X-\mu\ge c$ then, adding $t>0$ to both sides, $X-\mu+t\ge c+t>0$; squaring a positive inequality preserves it, so $(X-\mu+t)^2\ge(c+t)^2$. Hence $\{X-\mu\ge c\}\subseteq\{Y\ge (c+t)^2\}$, and by monotonicity of probability $$\mathbf{P}(X-\mu\ge c)\le\mathbf{P}\big(Y\ge (c+t)^2\big).$$

Step 3 — Markov on $Y$. Since $Y\ge0$ and $(c+t)^2>0$, $$\mathbf{P}\big(Y\ge(c+t)^2\big)\le\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y]}{(c+t)^2},\qquad \mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mu)^2\big]+2t\,\mathbb{E}[X-\mu]+t^2=\sigma^2+0+t^2 ,$$ using linearity of expectation and $\mathbb{E}[X-\mu]=0$. So for every $t>0$, $$\mathbf{P}(X-\mu\ge c)\le\frac{\sigma^2+t^2}{(c+t)^2}=:g(t).$$

Step 4 — choose the best $t$. Every $t$ gives a valid bound, so take the smallest. Differentiating, $$g'(t)=\frac{2t(c+t)^2-2(c+t)(\sigma^2+t^2)}{(c+t)^4} =\frac{2\big(t(c+t)-(\sigma^2+t^2)\big)}{(c+t)^3}=\frac{2(tc-\sigma^2)}{(c+t)^3},$$ which is negative for $t<\sigma^2/c$ and positive for $t>\sigma^2/c$: the minimum is at $t^\ast=\sigma^2/c$. Substituting, $$g(t^\ast)=\frac{\sigma^2+\sigma^4/c^2}{(c+\sigma^2/c)^2} =\frac{\sigma^2(c^2+\sigma^2)/c^2}{(c^2+\sigma^2)^2/c^2} =\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+c^2}.\qquad\blacksquare$$

Step 5 — the numbers. With $\sigma^2=4$ and $c=6$: $t^\ast=4/6=0.666667$ and $$\mathbf{P}(X-4\ge6)\le\frac{4}{4+36}=\frac{4}{40}=0.1 .$$ A numerical sweep of $g(t)$ over $t\in(0,20]$ bottoms out at $0.100000$ at $t=0.666672$ — confirming both the minimizer and the value. The two-sided bound of Q117 was $0.111111$, so the improvement is a factor $1.111111$, and the exact probability is still $0.008132$: the one-sided bound remains $12.30$ times the truth.

Answer. $\;\mathbf{P}(X-\mu\ge c)\le\sigma^2/(\sigma^2+c^2)$, attained by $t=\sigma^2/c$; here $0.1$ against the two-sided $0.111111$ and the exact $0.008132$.

The tempting wrong answer. Expecting the one-sided version to be roughly twice as tight, "because it drops half the event". It is not: as $c$ grows, $\sigma^2/(\sigma^2+c^2)\to\sigma^2/c^2$, so the two bounds merge. The symmetric half is cheap; what makes both loose is that they use only two moments.

Q119. ★★ · Weak law & sample size · G6 §1.4

You measure a battery's voltage $n$ times. The measurements $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are i.i.d. with unknown mean $\mu$ and known standard deviation $\sigma=0.3$ V, and $M_n$ is their average. You want $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge 0.05)\le 0.02$. (a) How large must $n$ be by Chebyshev / the weak law? (b) How large by the CLT? (c) What does Chebyshev say about the CLT's $n$, and what does the CLT say about Chebyshev's $n$?

Solution

Setup. $\mathbb{E}[M_n]=\mu$ and $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n=0.09/n$, since independent variances add and dividing by $n$ divides the variance by $n^2$. Write $\epsilon=0.05$ and $\delta=0.02$.

(a) Chebyshev. Applying Chebyshev to $M_n$, $$\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge0.05)\le\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}=\frac{0.09}{n(0.0025)}=\frac{36}{n},$$ and $36/n\le0.02$ requires $n\ge 36/0.02=1800$. So $n=1800$.

(b) CLT. Standardize: $(M_n-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)$ is approximately standard normal, so $$\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\approx 2\Big(1-\Phi\big(\tfrac{\epsilon\sqrt n}{\sigma}\big)\Big),$$ by symmetry of the normal. Setting this to $0.02$ needs $\epsilon\sqrt n/\sigma=z_{0.99}=2.326348$, so $$\sqrt n=\frac{2.326348\times 0.3}{0.05}=13.958087,\qquad n=194.828200\ \Rightarrow\ n=195 .$$

(c) Each about the other. At $n=195$ Chebyshev's guarantee is $36/195=0.184615$ — more than nine times the tolerance, so Chebyshev cannot certify the CLT's sample size. At $n=1800$ the CLT's forecast is $2\big(1-\Phi(0.05\sqrt{1800}/0.3)\big)=2\big(1-\Phi(7.071068)\big)=1.54\times10^{-12}$: the Chebyshev-sized experiment is astronomically better than its own promise. The ratio of sample sizes is $1800/195=9.23$.

Answer. $\;n=1800$ by Chebyshev, $n=195$ by the CLT (a factor $9.23$); Chebyshev's bound at $n=195$ is $0.184615$ and the CLT's probability at $n=1800$ is $1.54\times10^{-12}$.

The tempting wrong answer. Using $z_{0.98}=2.053749$ — that is the $98$th percentile, appropriate to a one-sided requirement. The event $|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon$ has two tails of $0.01$ each, so the percentile needed is $z_{1-\delta/2}=z_{0.99}$. Getting this wrong understates $n$ by about $22\%$.

Q120. ★★ · Counts spread, frequencies concentrate · G6 §1.5

A student writes: "The weak law says the fair-coin count settles down, so the gap $|S_n-n/2|$ between heads and half the tosses shrinks as $n$ grows." Decide whether that is right by computing $\mathbb{E}\big[|S_n-n/2|\big]$ for $n=100$ and $n=10{,}000$, and by computing $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\tfrac12|<0.01)$ at $n=10{,}000$, where $S_n$ is the number of heads and $M_n=S_n/n$.

Solution

Setup. $S_n\sim\text{Binomial}(n,\tfrac12)$, $\mathbb{E}[S_n]=n/2$, $\operatorname{var}(S_n)=n/4$, $\sigma_{S_n}=\sqrt n/2$. The weak law is a statement about $M_n=S_n/n$, not about $S_n$.

The absolute gap grows. Summing $\sum_k |k-n/2|\,p_{S_n}(k)$ exactly: $$\mathbb{E}\big[|S_{100}-50|\big]=3.979462,\qquad \mathbb{E}\big[|S_{10000}-5000|\big]=39.893231 .$$ Two million simulated runs at $n=10{,}000$ give $39.880865$  ✓. The normal approximation explains the pattern: for a nearly normal variable $\mathbb{E}|S_n-n/2|\approx\sigma_{S_n}\sqrt{2/\pi}=0.797885\,\sigma_{S_n}$, which is $3.989423$ at $n=100$ and $39.894228$ at $n=10{,}000$. Multiplying $n$ by $100$ multiplied the typical gap by $\sqrt{100}=10$. At $n=10^6$ it would be $398.942280$.

The relative gap shrinks. Dividing by $n$ is what the weak law watches. Exactly, $$\mathbf{P}\big(|M_{10000}-\tfrac12|\ge0.01\big)=\mathbf{P}(|S_{10000}-5000|\ge100)=0.046586,$$ so $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\tfrac12|<0.01)=0.953414$. The CLT approximation, $2\big(1-\Phi(100/50)\big)=2(1-\Phi(2))=0.045500$, tracks it closely.

Reading the two. The gap in counts grows like $\sqrt n$; the gap in frequency shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$, because it is the count gap divided by $n$. The weak law says the second goes to zero for every fixed tolerance, and says nothing whatever about the first. As a coda, the single most likely count has probability $\mathbf{P}(S_{10000}=5000)=0.007979$ — under one percent — so "settles down to exactly $n/2$" is false in the strongest possible way.

Answer. $\;$The student is wrong: $\mathbb{E}|S_n-n/2|$ grows from $3.979462$ to $39.893231$, while $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\tfrac12|<0.01)=0.953414$ at $n=10{,}000$.

Why the misreading is tempting. "Law of averages" language invites you to attach the convergence to $S_n$. Always write the weak law with the $1/n$ visible: $\mathbf{P}(|S_n/n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\to0$. It is a statement about a ratio, for a fixed $\epsilon$, and about probabilities, not about any particular sequence of outcomes.

Q121. ★ · Which tool can be signed · G6 §1.4

An engineer must sign a statement about $n=30$ i.i.d. measurements whose common variance is known to be at most $0.25$ and whose mean $\mu$ is unknown: "the sample mean $M_{30}$ misses $\mu$ by $0.2$ or more with probability at most $p$." Which of Markov, Chebyshev, the weak law, and the CLT can supply a defensible $p$, and what is it? If the engineer needs $p=0.05$ guaranteed, how many measurements are required?

Solution

Markov: not applicable. Markov requires a nonnegative random variable. $M_{30}-\mu$ takes both signs, and $|M_{30}-\mu|$ is nonnegative but its mean is not among the given data.

The weak law: not by itself. The weak law is a limit statement, "the probability tends to $0$ as $n\to\infty$". It certifies nothing at a fixed $n=30$. Its finite-$n$ content is exactly the Chebyshev computation below — that is how it is proved.

Chebyshev: yes, and here is the number. $\operatorname{var}(M_{30})=\sigma^2/30\le 0.25/30=0.008333$, so $$\mathbf{P}(|M_{30}-\mu|\ge0.2)\le\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2} =\frac{0.25}{30\times(0.2)^2}=\frac{0.25}{1.2}=0.208333 .$$ This is a theorem, valid for every distribution with variance at most $0.25$, so it can be signed.

CLT: a number, but not a guarantee. With $z=0.2\sqrt{30}/0.5=2.190890$ the CLT suggests $2\big(1-\Phi(2.190890)\big)=0.028460$ — seven times smaller. But it is an approximation with no error bound, and at $n=30$ with an unknown summand distribution it may be poor. It is the right number for planning, the wrong number for a signature.

The sample size for a guaranteed $0.05$. Solve $\sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2)\le0.05$: $$n\ge\frac{0.25}{(0.2)^2\times0.05}=\frac{0.25}{0.002}=125 .$$

Answer. $\;$Chebyshev, giving $p=0.208333$; a guaranteed $p=0.05$ needs $n\ge125$.

The tempting wrong answer. Quoting the CLT's $0.028460$ as the guarantee. The word in the statement is "at most", which is a contract; only an inequality valid for every admissible distribution can back it.

Q122. ★★★ · Convergence in probability · G6 §1.5

Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be i.i.d. uniform on $[0,2]$, so $\mu=1$ and $\sigma^2=1/3$, and let $M_n$ be the sample mean. (a) Prove directly — without quoting any continuous-mapping theorem — that $M_n^2$ converges in probability to $1$, by converting a tolerance on $M_n^2$ into a tolerance on $M_n$. (b) Evaluate your bound at $\epsilon=0.1$ for $n=100$ and $n=10{,}000$ and compare with the true probabilities.

Solution

(a) Step 1 — a deterministic implication. Suppose $|M_n-1|\le\delta$ for some $\delta>0$. Then, factoring the difference of squares, $$|M_n^2-1|=|M_n-1|\cdot|M_n+1|\le \delta\,(2+\delta),$$ using $|M_n+1|\le |M_n-1|+2\le\delta+2$ by the triangle inequality. So if we choose $\delta$ with $\delta(2+\delta)\le\epsilon$, the event $\{|M_n-1|\le\delta\}$ is contained in $\{|M_n^2-1|\le\epsilon\}$; equivalently, taking complements, $$\{|M_n^2-1|>\epsilon\}\subseteq\{|M_n-1|>\delta\}.$$

Step 2 — solve for $\delta$. $\delta(2+\delta)=\epsilon$ is $\delta^2+2\delta-\epsilon=0$, whose positive root is $\delta=\sqrt{1+\epsilon}-1$.

Step 3 — Chebyshev. Applying the weak-law bound to $M_n$, $$\mathbf{P}\big(|M_n^2-1|>\epsilon\big)\le\mathbf{P}\big(|M_n-1|>\delta\big) \le\frac{\sigma^2}{n\delta^2}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0$$ for every fixed $\epsilon>0$, since $\delta>0$ does not depend on $n$. That is convergence in probability of $M_n^2$ to $1$. $\blacksquare$

(b) The numbers. With $\epsilon=0.1$: $\delta=\sqrt{1.1}-1=0.048809$, and a check, $\delta(2+\delta)=0.100000$ ✓. The bound is $$\frac{\sigma^2}{n\delta^2}=\frac{1/3}{n(0.048809)^2}=\frac{139.920590}{n}.$$ At $n=100$ this is $1.399206$ — vacuous. The truth there, from $200{,}000$ simulated sample means, is $0.387150$: large, so the bound at least is not lying, but it certifies nothing. At $n=10{,}000$ the bound is $0.013992$. The truth is far smaller: $\operatorname{sd}(M_n)= \sqrt{(1/3)/10^4}=0.005774$, and the event needs $M_n\ge\sqrt{1.1}=1.048809$ (that is $z=8.453940$ standard deviations up) or $M_n\le\sqrt{0.9}=0.948683$ ($z=8.888314$ down), for a total probability of about $1.44\times10^{-17}$; $4{,}000$ simulated runs at $n=10{,}000$ produced $0$ exceedances, as expected.

Answer. $\;\mathbf{P}(|M_n^2-1|>0.1)\le 139.920590/n$, i.e. $1.399206$ (vacuous) at $n=100$ and $0.013992$ at $n=10{,}000$, against a true value near $1.4\times10^{-17}$ — the bound exceeds the truth by some $10^{15}$.

The tempting wrong answer. "Apply Chebyshev to $M_n^2$ directly with variance $\operatorname{var}(M_n^2)$." That is legal but requires fourth moments of $X$ and a computation of $\operatorname{var}(M_n^2)$; the $\delta$-conversion needs only what the weak law already gives. Worse is to let $\delta$ depend on $n$ — then $\sigma^2/(n\delta^2)$ need not vanish and the proof collapses.

Q123. ★ · Standardizing a sum · G6 §2.3

A call center handles $200$ calls in a shift. Handling times are i.i.d. with mean $3$ minutes and standard deviation $2$ minutes. Approximate the probability that the total handling time exceeds $620$ minutes.

Solution

Step 1 — the sum's mean and standard deviation. With $S=X_1+\cdots+X_{200}$, $$\mathbb{E}[S]=200\times3=600,\qquad \operatorname{var}(S)=200\times2^2=800,\qquad \sigma_S=2\sqrt{200}=28.284271,$$ variances adding because the calls are independent.

Step 2 — standardize. $$\mathbf{P}(S>620)=\mathbf{P}\!\left(\frac{S-600}{28.284271}>\frac{620-600}{28.284271}\right) =\mathbf{P}(Z_n>0.707107).$$

Step 3 — read the normal table. By the CLT, $Z_n$ is approximately standard normal, so $$\mathbf{P}(S>620)\approx 1-\Phi(0.707107)=1-0.760250=0.239750 .$$ Sanity check: $\mathbf{P}(S>600)\approx 1-\Phi(0)=0.5$, as it should be, since $600$ is the mean.

Answer. $\;\mathbf{P}(S>620)\approx 0.239750$.

The tempting wrong answer. Standardizing with $\sigma/\sqrt n=2/\sqrt{200}=0.141421$ instead of $\sigma\sqrt n=28.284271$. The first is the standard deviation of the average, the second of the sum; using it would give $z=141.42$ and a probability of $0$. Decide first which of $S_n$ and $M_n$ the question is about, then pick the matching scale.

Q124. ★★ · De Moivre–Laplace · G6 §2.4

A basketball player takes $n=100$ independent free throws, each successful with probability $p=0.4$; let $S$ be the number made. (a) Approximate $\mathbf{P}(S=45)$ with the $\tfrac12$ correction and compare with the exact binomial value. (b) Approximate $\mathbf{P}(35\le S\le 45)$ both with and without the correction, and compare both with the exact value. Quantify what the correction bought.

Solution

Setup. $\mathbb{E}[S]=np=100(0.4)=40$ and $\sigma_S=\sqrt{np(1-p)}=\sqrt{100(0.4)(0.6)}=\sqrt{24}=4.898979$.

(a) A point probability. A normal density assigns probability $0$ to any single point, so first turn the point into the unit interval it "owns": since $S$ is integer valued, $\mathbf{P}(S=45)=\mathbf{P}(44.5\le S\le 45.5)$ exactly. Then $$\mathbf{P}(S=45)\approx\Phi\!\left(\frac{45.5-40}{4.898979}\right)- \Phi\!\left(\frac{44.5-40}{4.898979}\right)=\Phi(1.122683)-\Phi(0.918559)=0.048377 .$$ Exactly, $\binom{100}{45}(0.4)^{45}(0.6)^{55}=0.047811$. The relative error is $0.011837$, i.e. $1.18\%$.

(b) An interval. The event $\{35\le S\le45\}$ owns the interval $[34.5,45.5]$, so with the correction $$\mathbf{P}(35\le S\le45)\approx\Phi\!\left(\frac{45.5-40}{4.898979}\right)- \Phi\!\left(\frac{34.5-40}{4.898979}\right)=\Phi(1.122683)-\Phi(-1.122683)=0.738428 .$$ Without it, cutting at the integers themselves, $$\Phi\!\left(\frac{45-40}{4.898979}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{35-40}{4.898979}\right) =\Phi(1.020621)-\Phi(-1.020621)=0.692566 .$$ The exact binomial value is $0.738573$.

What the correction bought. Absolute errors: $0.000145$ with the correction, $0.046007$ without — the corrected answer is $317$ times more accurate, and the uncorrected one is wrong in the third percentage point. The uncorrected version omits half the bar at $35$ and half the bar at $45$, worth $\tfrac12\big(p_S(35)+p_S(45)\big)=0.048472$ — within $5\%$ of the observed gap $0.046007$, the remainder being the curvature of the density across those two bars.

Answer. $\;\mathbf{P}(S=45)\approx0.048377$ (exact $0.047811$); $\mathbf{P}(35\le S\le45)\approx0.738428$ corrected and $0.692566$ uncorrected, against the exact $0.738573$.

An equally good alternative for (a). The local form of De Moivre–Laplace approximates the bar by the density at its center, $p_S(k)\approx\phi(z_k)/\sigma_S$ with $z_k=(k-np)/\sigma_S$. Here $z_{45}=1.020621$, $\phi(1.020621)=0.236982$ and $$\mathbf{P}(S=45)\approx\frac{0.236982}{4.898979}=0.048374 ,$$ a relative error of $1.18\%$ — the same accuracy as the $\Phi(1.122683)-\Phi(0.918559)$ route above, of which it is the midpoint-rule approximation (one bar is one unit wide, so the integral of $\phi$ across it is about its center value). Either is a correct answer to (a).

The tempting wrong answer. Answering $0$, on the grounds that a normal density gives any single point probability $0$ — the point is precisely that a discrete $S$ must first be given its unit bar. Also common: shifting both endpoints the same way in (b), e.g. using $[34.5,44.5]$. The rule is that the interval must expand by $\tfrac12$ at each end — every included integer keeps its whole unit bar; $[34.5,44.5]$ silently drops the bar at $45$ and would give $\Phi(0.918559)-\Phi(-1.122683)=0.690051$.

Q125. ★★ · When the CLT is the wrong limit · G6 §2.2

Each of $n=200$ components fails during a mission independently with probability $p=0.01$; let $S$ be the number of failures. Estimate $\mathbf{P}(S\ge6)$ with the normal approximation (using the $\tfrac12$ correction), then with a Poisson approximation, and compare both with the exact binomial value. Diagnose which approximation is appropriate and why.

Solution

Setup. $\mathbb{E}[S]=np=200(0.01)=2$ and $\sigma_S=\sqrt{np(1-p)}=\sqrt{200(0.01)(0.99)}=\sqrt{1.98}=1.407125$.

Normal approximation. With the correction, $\{S\ge6\}$ starts at $5.5$: $$\mathbf{P}(S\ge6)\approx 1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{5.5-2}{1.407125}\right)=1-\Phi(2.487342)=0.006435 .$$ (Without the correction it would be $1-\Phi(2.842676)=0.002237$, worse still.)

Poisson approximation. $n$ is large and $p$ small with $np=2$ moderate, so $S\approx\text{Poisson}(2)$ and $$\mathbf{P}(S\ge6)\approx 1-\sum_{k=0}^{5}e^{-2}\frac{2^k}{k!}=0.016564 .$$

Exact. $\mathbf{P}(S\ge6)=1-\sum_{k=0}^{5}\binom{200}{k}(0.01)^k(0.99)^{200-k}=0.016023$; four million simulated missions give $0.015986$ ✓.

Diagnosis. The normal answer is off by a factor $0.016023/0.006435=2.49$ — it understates the risk by more than half. The Poisson answer is off by $3.37\%$. The reason is that the CLT's quality depends on $n$ only through how many summands actually vary: here the binomial's own skewness is $(1-2p)/\sqrt{np(1-p)}=0.98/1.407125=0.696456$, far from the normal's $0$, and the common rule of thumb $np\ge5$ fails badly ($np=2$). To reach $np=5$ at this $p$ you would need $n\ge 5/0.01=500$. When $p$ is small and $np$ is fixed, the binomial's natural limit is Poisson, not normal.

Answer. $\;$normal $0.006435$, Poisson $0.016564$, exact $0.016023$ — the normal approximation understates by a factor $2.49$ and should not be used here.

The tempting wrong answer. Reasoning "$n=200$ is large, so the CLT applies". The CLT is a statement about $n\to\infty$ with the summand distribution held fixed; when the interesting events are rare, $n=200$ is not large in the relevant sense. Check $np$ and $n(1-p)$ before trusting a normal approximation to a binomial.

Q126. ★★ · Spot the error: sum scale vs. mean scale · G6 §2.3

Spot the error. Fifty jobs have i.i.d. exponential service times with mean $1$ hour, and $S$ is the total. A student writes:

"$\mathbb{E}[S]=50$ and $\sigma=1$, so $\mathbf{P}(S\ge65)\approx1-\Phi\big(\frac{65-50}{1/\sqrt{50}}\big)=1-\Phi(106.066)\approx 0$."

Identify the mistake, give the correct CLT value, and compare it with the exact value (the sum of $50$ i.i.d. Exponential$(1)$ variables is Erlang/Gamma with shape $50$ and scale $1$).

Solution

The mistake. The student used $\sigma/\sqrt n$, the standard deviation of the sample mean $M_n$, to standardize the sum $S_n$. Since $\operatorname{var}(S_n)=n\sigma^2$ for independent terms, the correct scale is $$\sigma_{S}=\sigma\sqrt n=1\times\sqrt{50}=7.071068,$$ not $1/\sqrt{50}=0.141421$ — a factor of $50$ too small, which is why the reported $z$-score $106.066$ is absurd. (A $z$-score above about $6$ should always prompt a re-read of the scaling.)

The correct CLT value. $$\mathbf{P}(S\ge65)\approx1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{65-50}{7.071068}\right)=1-\Phi(2.121320) =0.016947 .$$ No continuity correction: $S$ is a continuous random variable, and the $\tfrac12$ shift applies only to integer-valued sums.

The exact value. $S\sim\text{Gamma}(50,1)$, and $\mathbf{P}(S\ge65)=0.023512$; four million simulated totals give $0.023488$ ✓.

Reading the comparison. The CLT value is $0.016947/0.023512=0.72$ of the truth — it understates the right tail by $28\%$, even at $n=50$. The exponential is right-skewed (skewness $2$), so the sum's skewness is $2/\sqrt{50}=0.282843$, still positive, and a symmetric normal cannot reproduce a fatter right tail. The approximation is fine near the mean and optimistic out at $2\sigma$.

Answer. $\;$The scale should be $\sigma\sqrt n=7.071068$; the CLT gives $\mathbf{P}(S\ge65)\approx0.016947$ against the exact $0.023512$.

The tempting wrong answer. Besides the scale slip, adding a $\tfrac12$ correction "for safety": $1-\Phi((64.5-50)/7.071068)=0.020152$ happens to land closer to the truth here, but only by accident — the correction is a statement about probability bars of a discrete variable, and using it on a continuous sum is unjustified.

Q127. ★★ · Sizing a buffer, and what the CLT does not guarantee · G6 §2.3

A data center receives $50$ independent daily upload batches, each of size exponential with mean $5$ GB. You must size a buffer $C$ so that the total of the $50$ batches exceeds $C$ with probability at most $0.01$. (a) Give $C$ from the CLT. (b) The exact distribution of the total is Gamma with shape $50$ and scale $5$; what overflow probability does your $C$ actually deliver, and what $C$ would have been correct?

Solution

(a) The CLT sizing. Each batch has mean $5$ and standard deviation $5$ (for an exponential, $\sigma=$ mean). So $$\mathbb{E}[S]=50\times5=250,\qquad \sigma_S=5\sqrt{50}=35.355339 .$$ We need $\mathbf{P}(S>C)\le0.01$, i.e. $1-\Phi\big((C-250)/35.355339\big)=0.01$, i.e. $(C-250)/35.355339=z_{0.99}=2.326348$. Hence $$C=250+2.326348\times35.355339=250+82.248818=332.248818\ \text{GB}.$$

(b) What it actually delivers. Under the exact Gamma$(50,\text{scale }5)$ law, $$\mathbf{P}(S>332.248818)=0.015495 ,$$ confirmed by four million simulated totals at $0.015419$. That is $1.55$ times the $0.01$ you promised. The correct $99$th percentile is $$C_{\text{exact}}=339.516808\ \text{GB},$$ so the CLT undersized the buffer by $339.516808-332.248818=7.267990$ GB, about $2.2\%$.

Why the error goes this way. Exponential summands are right-skewed (skewness $2$ each, $2/\sqrt{50}=0.282843$ for the sum), so the true distribution has a heavier right tail than the fitted normal. A symmetric approximation therefore always underestimates a far-right tail for right-skewed data — and underestimating an overflow probability is the dangerous direction.

Answer. $\;C_{\text{CLT}}=332.248818$ GB, which really overflows with probability $0.015495$; the correct value is $C=339.516808$ GB.

The tempting wrong answer. Reporting "the overflow risk is $1\%$" as a fact. The CLT returns a number, not a bound (G6 §5.1): with $n=50$ skewed summands the number is off by half again. If a guarantee is needed, either use the exact law when you have it, or add margin.

Q128. ★ · Discrete prior, discrete observation · G6 §3.1–3.2

A monitoring system's alarm rate $\Theta$, in alarms per hour, is one of $1$, $2$ or $3$, with prior probabilities $0.5$, $0.3$, $0.2$. Given $\Theta=\theta$, the number of alarms in one hour is Poisson$(\theta)$. You observe $X=4$ alarms in an hour. Compute the posterior PMF of $\Theta$, the MAP estimate, and $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=4]$.

Solution

Step 1 — the likelihoods. $p_{X|\Theta}(4\mid\theta)=e^{-\theta}\theta^4/4!$, so $$p_{X|\Theta}(4\mid1)=0.015328,\quad p_{X|\Theta}(4\mid2)=0.090224,\quad p_{X|\Theta}(4\mid3)=0.168031 .$$

Step 2 — multiply by the prior. The Bayes numerator $p_\Theta(\theta)p_{X|\Theta}(4\mid\theta)$ takes the values $$0.5\times0.015328=0.007664,\qquad 0.3\times0.090224=0.027067,\qquad 0.2\times0.168031=0.033606 .$$

Step 3 — normalize. The denominator is the total probability of the data, $$p_X(4)=0.007664+0.027067+0.033606=0.068337 ,$$ so the posterior PMF is $$p_{\Theta|X}(1\mid4)=\frac{0.007664}{0.068337}=0.112152,\quad p_{\Theta|X}(2\mid4)=0.396079,\quad p_{\Theta|X}(3\mid4)=0.491769 ,$$ which sum to $1$ ✓.

Step 4 — the two summaries. The largest posterior probability is at $\theta=3$, so $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=3$, and it is wrong with probability $1-0.491769=0.508231$. The conditional expectation is $$\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=4]=1(0.112152)+2(0.396079)+3(0.491769) =0.112152+0.792159+1.475308=2.379618 ,$$ against a prior mean of $1(0.5)+2(0.3)+3(0.2)=1.7$: the data pulled the estimate up.

Answer. $\;$posterior $(0.112152,\;0.396079,\;0.491769)$; $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=3$; $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=4]=2.379618$.

The tempting wrong answer. Reporting $2.379618$ as "the most likely rate". It is not a possible value of $\Theta$ at all. The conditional mean is the best squared-error summary; if you must name one of the three rates, MAP is the rule that minimizes the probability of naming the wrong one.

Q129. ★★ · Conjugate Beta coin bias · G6 §3.2

A coin's bias $\Theta$ has prior density $f_\Theta(\theta)=6\theta(1-\theta)$ on $[0,1]$ — a Beta$(2,2)$, peaked at $\tfrac12$. You toss the coin $10$ times, independently given $\Theta$, and see $7$ heads. (a) Find the posterior density, including its normalizing constant. (b) Give its mean, mode and standard deviation, and compare the mean with the sample frequency $7/10$. (c) Show that the posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and the sample frequency, and give the weights. (d) Compute $\mathbf{P}(\Theta>\tfrac12\mid \text{data})$.

Solution

(a) The posterior. Given $\Theta=\theta$, the count $K$ of heads is binomial: $\mathbf{P}(K=7\mid\Theta=\theta)=\binom{10}{7}\theta^7(1-\theta)^3$. Bayes for a continuous unknown with discrete data gives $$f_{\Theta|K}(\theta\mid7)=\frac{f_\Theta(\theta)\mathbf{P}(K=7\mid\Theta=\theta)} {\int_0^1 f_\Theta(t)\mathbf{P}(K=7\mid\Theta=t)\,dt} .$$ The constants $6$ and $\binom{10}{7}=120$ appear in numerator and denominator alike and cancel, so $$f_{\Theta|K}(\theta\mid7)\propto \theta(1-\theta)\cdot\theta^7(1-\theta)^3=\theta^{8}(1-\theta)^{4},$$ which is the Beta$(9,5)$ shape. Its normalizing constant is $1/B(9,5)=13!/(8!\,4!)=6435$, so $$f_{\Theta|K}(\theta\mid 7)=6435\,\theta^{8}(1-\theta)^{4},\qquad 0\le\theta\le1 .$$ This is conjugacy at work: a Beta prior with $a$ heads-worth and $b$ tails-worth of prior belief, plus $k$ heads in $n$ tosses, gives Beta$(a+k,\,b+n-k)=\text{Beta}(2+7,\,2+3)$.

(b) Summaries. For Beta$(a,b)$ the mean is $a/(a+b)$, the mode (for $a,b>1$) is $(a-1)/(a+b-2)$, and the variance is $ab/\big((a+b)^2(a+b+1)\big)$. With $a=9$, $b=5$: $$\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid K=7]=\frac{9}{14}=0.642857,\qquad \hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\frac{8}{12}=0.666667,$$ $$\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid K=7)=\frac{9\times5}{14^2\times15}=\frac{45}{2940}=0.015306, \qquad \text{sd}=0.123718 .$$ Four million draws from Beta$(9,5)$ average $0.642802$ ✓. Both summaries sit below the sample frequency $0.7$, pulled toward the prior mean $0.5$.

(c) The weights. Write the posterior mean with $a_0=b_0=2$, $n=10$, $k=7$: $$\frac{a_0+k}{a_0+b_0+n}=\frac{a_0+b_0}{a_0+b_0+n}\cdot\frac{a_0}{a_0+b_0} +\frac{n}{a_0+b_0+n}\cdot\frac{k}{n},$$ which is an identity (expand the right side and the numerators add to $a_0+k$). Numerically the weights are $4/14=0.285714$ on the prior mean $0.5$ and $10/14=0.714286$ on the frequency $0.7$: $$0.285714(0.5)+0.714286(0.7)=0.142857+0.5=0.642857\ \checkmark$$ Ten tosses are worth $10$ "pseudo-observations" against the prior's $a_0+b_0=4$; as $n$ grows the prior's weight $\to0$ and the posterior mean $\to$ the sample frequency.

(d) The posterior probability of a head-biased coin. We want $\mathbf{P}(\Theta>\tfrac12\mid K=7)=\int_{1/2}^1 6435\,\theta^8(1-\theta)^4\,d\theta$. Rather than integrate by parts nine times, use the Beta-to-binomial identity: for integer $a,b\ge1$ and any $x\in[0,1]$, a Beta$(a,b)$ variable satisfies $$\mathbf{P}\big(\text{Beta}(a,b)>x\big)=\mathbf{P}\big(\text{Binomial}(a+b-1,\,x)\le a-1\big).$$ (Both sides are the probability that, among $a+b-1$ i.i.d. uniforms on $[0,1]$, at most $a-1$ land below $x$ — the left side because the $a$th smallest of them is a Beta$(a,b)$ variable, and "the $a$th smallest exceeds $x$" is exactly "fewer than $a$ fall below $x$".) With $a=9$, $b=5$, $x=\tfrac12$, so $a+b-1=13$: $$\mathbf{P}\big(\Theta>\tfrac12\mid K=7\big)=\sum_{j=0}^{8}\binom{13}{j}\Big(\tfrac12\Big)^{13} =\frac{1+13+78+286+715+1287+1716+1716+1287}{8192}=\frac{7099}{8192}=0.866577 .$$ So the data make "head-biased" about $6.5$ times as likely as not, but far from certain.

Answer. $\;f_{\Theta|K}(\theta\mid7)=6435\,\theta^8(1-\theta)^4$; mean $9/14=0.642857$, mode $0.666667$, sd $0.123718$; weights $0.285714$ and $0.714286$; $\mathbf{P}(\Theta>\tfrac12\mid\text{data})=0.866577$.

The tempting wrong answer. Reporting $0.7$ — the value maximizing the likelihood $\theta^7(1-\theta)^3$ — as "the Bayesian estimate". That ignores the prior entirely; it is the maximum-likelihood estimate of G7 §2. The MAP estimate maximizes prior $\times$ likelihood and comes out at $0.666667$, and the LMS estimate is $0.642857$. Also do not drop the prior's $\theta(1-\theta)$ factor as "just a constant": it depends on $\theta$, and it is what shifts $0.7$ down to $0.642857$.

Q130. ★★ · MAP vs conditional mean · G6 §3.3

A component's failure rate $\Theta$ has prior density $f_\Theta(\theta)=e^{-\theta}$ for $\theta\ge0$ (exponential with parameter $1$). Given $\Theta=\theta$, the component's lifetime $T$ is exponential with parameter $\theta$, so $f_{T|\Theta}(t\mid\theta)=\theta e^{-\theta t}$. One component fails at $t=1$. (a) Find the posterior density of $\Theta$. (b) Give the MAP estimate and the conditional mean. (c) Compute the conditional mean squared error of each, and say which you would report.

Solution

(a) The posterior. By Bayes for two continuous variables, $$f_{\Theta|T}(\theta\mid1)=\frac{f_\Theta(\theta)f_{T|\Theta}(1\mid\theta)} {\int_0^\infty f_\Theta(s)f_{T|\Theta}(1\mid s)\,ds} =\frac{e^{-\theta}\cdot\theta e^{-\theta}}{\int_0^\infty s e^{-2s}\,ds} =\frac{\theta e^{-2\theta}}{1/4}=4\theta e^{-2\theta},\quad\theta\ge0,$$ using $\int_0^\infty se^{-2s}ds=1/2^2=0.25$. This is the Gamma density with shape $2$ and rate $2$.

(b) The two point estimates. For the MAP, maximize $\theta e^{-2\theta}$: $$\frac{d}{d\theta}\big(\theta e^{-2\theta}\big)=(1-2\theta)e^{-2\theta}=0 \;\Longrightarrow\;\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=0.5 ,$$ and the derivative is positive before and negative after, so this is the maximum. For the conditional mean, a Gamma$(2,\text{rate }2)$ has mean $2/2$: $$\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid T=1]=\int_0^\infty\theta\cdot4\theta e^{-2\theta}d\theta =4\cdot\frac{2!}{2^3}=1 .$$ (Four million posterior draws average $1.000260$ ✓.) The posterior variance is $2/2^2=0.5$ and, for reference, the posterior median is $0.839173$ — mode $<$ median $<$ mean, the signature of a right-skewed posterior.

(c) The mean squared errors. For any constant report $c$, $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-c)^2\mid T=1\big]=\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid T=1) +\big(\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid T=1]-c\big)^2 = 0.5+(1-c)^2 .$$ At $c=1$ (the mean) this is $0.5$ — the minimum. At $c=0.5$ (the MAP) it is $0.5+0.25=0.75$, a factor $1.5$ worse; simulation gives $0.749709$ ✓. At the median it is $0.525865$.

Which to report. If the loss is squared error, report $1$; the MAP costs $50\%$ more mean squared error. If the loss is "how often am I exactly right", neither matters, since $\mathbf{P}(\hat\Theta=\Theta)=0$ for any estimator of a continuous $\Theta$. Best of all, report the posterior $4\theta e^{-2\theta}$, which shows the skew that no single number conveys.

Answer. $\;f_{\Theta|T}(\theta\mid1)=4\theta e^{-2\theta}$; $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=0.5$, $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid T=1]=1$, with conditional MSEs $0.75$ and $0.5$.

The tempting wrong answer. "MAP minimizes the probability of error, so it is the better estimate." That optimality is a theorem about discrete $\Theta$; with a continuous posterior the probability of being exactly right is $0$ for every rule, so the property is vacuous and the mode is simply the wrong summary for a squared-error question.

Q131. ★★★ · MAP under reparametrization · G6 §3.3

Continue Q130: the posterior for the failure rate is $f_{\Theta}(\theta)=4\theta e^{-2\theta}$ on $\theta\ge0$ (conditioning on the data is suppressed in the notation). A colleague prefers to work with the mean lifetime $\Phi=1/\Theta$. (a) Derive the posterior density of $\Phi$. (b) Find its MAP estimate and compare with $1/\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}$. (c) Compare $\mathbb{E}[\Phi]$ with $1/\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$. What general lesson does each comparison teach?

Solution

(a) Change of variables. $\phi=g(\theta)=1/\theta$ is a decreasing bijection of $(0,\infty)$ onto itself with inverse $\theta=1/\phi$ and $\big|\tfrac{d\theta}{d\phi}\big|=1/\phi^2$. The density transformation rule (G3 §4) gives $$f_\Phi(\phi)=f_\Theta\!\left(\frac1\phi\right)\cdot\frac{1}{\phi^2} =4\cdot\frac1\phi e^{-2/\phi}\cdot\frac{1}{\phi^2} =\frac{4}{\phi^{3}}e^{-2/\phi},\qquad \phi>0 .$$ (Numerically integrating this from $10^{-4}$ to $20$ gives $0.995321$, and the tail beyond $20$ is about $4/(2\cdot20^2)=0.005$ — it integrates to $1$ ✓.)

(b) The MAP in the new variable. Maximizing $f_\Phi$ is easier through its logarithm, $\ln f_\Phi(\phi)=\ln4-3\ln\phi-2/\phi$: $$\frac{d}{d\phi}\ln f_\Phi(\phi)=-\frac{3}{\phi}+\frac{2}{\phi^{2}} =\frac{2-3\phi}{\phi^{2}}=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\hat\phi_{\text{MAP}}=\frac{2}{3}=0.666667,$$ positive before and negative after, so a maximum (a grid search over $(0,20]$ agrees, returning $0.666667$). But transforming the old answer gives $$\frac{1}{\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}}=\frac{1}{0.5}=2 .$$ The two disagree by a factor of $3$. MAP is not invariant under reparametrization: a density picks up the Jacobian $|d\theta/d\phi|$, here $1/\phi^2$, which is not constant, so it moves the peak. Both answers are "the MAP" — of different questions.

(c) The means. $$\mathbb{E}[\Phi]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac1\Theta\right] =\int_0^\infty\frac1\theta\,4\theta e^{-2\theta}\,d\theta =4\int_0^\infty e^{-2\theta}d\theta=\frac{4}{2}=2 ,$$ (four million draws give $1.996161$ ✓), whereas $1/\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=1/1=1$. Also not equal — but for a different reason: $x\mapsto1/x$ is strictly convex on $(0,\infty)$, so Jensen's inequality forces $\mathbb{E}[1/\Theta]>1/\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$, and indeed $2>1$.

The two lessons. (i) The MAP failure is a defect of the estimator: the mode of a density is not a property of the underlying random variable, so "the most likely value" depends on the coordinates you chose. (ii) The mean's failure is not a defect at all: $\mathbb{E}[\Phi]$ is still the correct LMS estimate of $\Phi$, and it is simply a fact that the LMS estimate of $1/\Theta$ is not $1/(\text{LMS estimate of }\Theta)$. Decide which quantity you are being scored on before you estimate.

Answer. $\;f_\Phi(\phi)=4\phi^{-3}e^{-2/\phi}$; $\hat\phi_{\text{MAP}}=2/3=0.666667$ against $1/\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=2$; and $\mathbb{E}[\Phi]=2$ against $1/\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=1$.

The tempting wrong answer. "The MAP estimate is invariant, like the maximum-likelihood estimate." The ML estimate is invariant, because a likelihood is a function of the parameter with no Jacobian attached — you are reindexing the same numbers. A posterior is a density in the parameter, and densities transform with a Jacobian. The distinction is exactly the one in G6 §3.2's Gotcha that a likelihood is not a density in $\theta$.

Q132. ★ · The no-data baseline · G6 §4.1–4.2

An unknown quantity $\Theta$ has $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=8$ and $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=5$, and you observe nothing. (a) What constant $c$ minimizes $\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-c)^2]$, and what is the minimum? (b) What is the mean squared error if you report $9$ instead? (c) A colleague proposes estimating $\Theta$ from an observation $X$ that is independent of $\Theta$. Can that beat your answer to (a)?

Solution

(a) The identity. Expand around the mean: $$\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-c)^2]=\mathbb{E}\big[\big((\Theta-8)+(8-c)\big)^2\big] =\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-8)^2]+2(8-c)\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\Theta-8]}_{=0}+(8-c)^2 =\operatorname{var}(\Theta)+(c-8)^2 .$$ The first term does not involve $c$ and the second is a square, so the minimum is at $c=8$ with value $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=5$.

(b) Reporting $9$. Substituting $c=9$: $5+(9-8)^2=5+1=6$, a penalty factor $6/5=1.2$. A miss of one unit in the estimate costs one unit of mean squared error — errors in the estimate add in quadrature with the irreducible spread.

(c) An independent observation. No. The LMS estimator is $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$, and independence gives $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=8$ for every value of $X$, with mean squared error $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)]=\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=5$. Since no estimator can beat the LMS estimator, $5$ stands. Data that carry no information about $\Theta$ cannot reduce the error — and using them can only add noise.

Answer. $\;c=8$ with MSE $5$; reporting $9$ gives MSE $6$; an independent $X$ cannot improve on $5$.

The tempting wrong answer. Reporting the mode of $\Theta$. With no distribution given, the mode is not even determined by the data in the problem — and even when it is known, it minimizes the probability of error, not the mean squared error. Under squared-error loss the no-data answer is always the mean.

Q133. ★★ · Linear LMS · G6 §4.5

A part's true mass $\Theta$ (grams) has $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=70$ and $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=8$. A gauge reports $X=2\Theta+W$, where the factor $2$ is a known gain and the noise $W$ is independent of $\Theta$ with $\mathbb{E}[W]=0$ and $\operatorname{var}(W)=2$. No distributions are given. (a) Find the linear LMS estimator $\hat\Theta_L=aX+b$. (b) Give $\rho^2$ and the resulting mean squared error, and say what fraction of the prior variance the gauge removed. (c) Evaluate the estimate at $x=150$.

Solution

Step 1 — the five numbers. By linearity of expectation, $\mathbb{E}[X]=2\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+\mathbb{E}[W]=2(70)+0=140$. By independence (variances add, and a constant factor squares), $$\operatorname{var}(X)=2^2\operatorname{var}(\Theta)+\operatorname{var}(W)=4(8)+2=34 .$$ For the covariance, bilinearity plus $\operatorname{cov}(W,\Theta)=0$ under independence: $$\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)=\operatorname{cov}(2\Theta+W,\Theta)=2\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=2(8)=16 .$$ The fifth number is $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=8$, needed only for the error.

Step 2 — the estimator. $$a=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)}{\operatorname{var}(X)}=\frac{16}{34}=0.470588,\qquad b=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-a\,\mathbb{E}[X]=70-0.470588(140)=70-65.882353=4.117647 ,$$ so $\hat\Theta_L=0.470588\,X+4.117647$. A least-squares fit to four million simulated pairs returns $a=0.470561$, $b=4.121381$ ✓.

Step 3 — the error. $$\rho^2=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)^2}{\operatorname{var}(X)\operatorname{var}(\Theta)} =\frac{16^2}{34\times8}=\frac{256}{272}=0.941176,\qquad \rho=0.970143,$$ $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\hat\Theta_L)^2\big]=(1-\rho^2)\operatorname{var}(\Theta) =0.058824\times8=0.470588 .$$ Simulation gives $0.470601$ ✓, and the closed form for this model agrees: $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)\operatorname{var}(W)/\big(4\operatorname{var}(\Theta)+\operatorname{var}(W)\big) =8(2)/34=0.470588$. Against the no-data baseline $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=8$, the gauge removed $94.117647\%$ of the variance.

Step 4 — at $x=150$. $\hat\theta_L=0.470588(150)+4.117647=70.588235+4.117647=74.705882$ grams. Sensible: a reading of $150$ suggests $\Theta\approx75$, and the estimate is shrunk slightly toward the prior mean $70$.

Answer. $\;\hat\Theta_L=0.470588X+4.117647$; $\rho^2=0.941176$ and MSE $=0.470588$ ($94.117647\%$ of the prior variance removed); at $x=150$ the estimate is $74.705882$ g.

The tempting wrong answer. Taking $\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)=\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=8$ — correct only when $X=\Theta+W$ with unit gain. It gives $a=8/34=0.235294$ and, at $x=150$, an estimate of $72.352941$ g, badly biased toward the prior. Whenever the observation is scaled, the gain appears once in the covariance and twice in $\operatorname{var}(X)$.

Q134. ★★★ · Orthogonality is not independence · G6 §4.4

True or false, with a proof or a counterexample: "Because the LMS estimation error $\tilde\Theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]-\Theta$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta\,h(X)]=0$ for every function $h$, the error is independent of the observation $X$." Test the claim on the model in which $X$ is uniform on $[-1,1]$ and, given $X=x$, $\Theta$ is uniform on $[-|x|,\,|x|]$.

Solution

The claim is false. Orthogonality is a statement about correlations — expectations of products that are linear in $\tilde\Theta$ — and uncorrelatedness never implies independence.

Step 1 — the estimator in this model. Given $X=x$, $\Theta$ is uniform on a symmetric interval, so $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]=0$ for every $x$. Hence $\hat\Theta\equiv0$ and $\tilde\Theta=\hat\Theta-\Theta=-\Theta$.

Step 2 — the error variance. A uniform on $[-|x|,|x|]$ has variance $(2|x|)^2/12=x^2/3$, so $\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2\mid X=x]=x^2/3$. By the law of iterated expectations, with $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=\int_{-1}^{1}\tfrac12 x^2dx=1/3$, $$\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{X^2}{3}\right] =\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac13=\frac19=0.111111,$$ which is also the mean squared error, since $\hat\Theta\equiv0$. Four million simulated pairs give $0.111195$ ✓. The variance decomposition checks out: $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta)+\operatorname{var}(\tilde\Theta) =0+1/9$ ✓.

Step 3 — orthogonality does hold. For any $h$, $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta h(X)]=\mathbb{E}\big[h(X)\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta\mid X]\big]=0$ since the inner conditional expectation is $0$. Simulation with $h(x)=x^2$ returns $0.000113$, and $\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)=0.000123$ — both zero to Monte-Carlo accuracy ✓.

Step 4 — but the error is not independent of $X$. Its spread is driven by $X$: the conditional error variance is $x^2/3$, which equals $0.27$ at $x=0.9$ and $0.003333$ at $x=0.1$ — a factor of $81$. Formally, if $\tilde\Theta$ and $X$ were independent then $\tilde\Theta^2$ and $X^2$ would be too, forcing $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta^2X^2]=\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta^2]\mathbb{E}[X^2]$. But $$\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta^2X^2]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{X^4}{3}\right]=\frac{1/5}{3}=0.066667, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta^2]\mathbb{E}[X^2]=\frac19\cdot\frac13=0.037037,$$ using $\mathbb{E}[X^4]=\int_{-1}^1\tfrac12x^4dx=1/5$. The gap is $0.029630$ (simulation gives $0.066751$ for the left side ✓), so independence fails.

Answer. $\;$False. Here orthogonality holds exactly while $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta^2X^2]=0.066667\neq0.037037=\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta^2]\mathbb{E}[X^2]$, so the error depends on $X$ through its variance.

Why this matters, and the tempting wrong answer. "The residual is uncorrelated with everything the data could tell you" is the correct reading of orthogonality; "the residual knows nothing about $X$" is not. Concretely, an engineer who reports the single number $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta^2]=0.111111$ as the accuracy of the estimate is misleading the reader: at $x=0.1$ the estimate is nearly exact and at $x=0.9$ it is $81$ times worse. Always quote the conditional spread $\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=x)$ alongside the estimate. (The one case where independence does follow is joint normality, where zero correlation is independence.)
