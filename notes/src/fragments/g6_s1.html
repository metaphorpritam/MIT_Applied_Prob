Through G1–G5 the questions were always "given the model, compute the answer exactly." We built PMFs, PDFs, joint laws, transforms, and Markov chains, and in every case the target was a number obtained by summing or integrating something we knew completely. This section changes the question in two ways at once.

First, we ask what can be said when the distribution is not known — when all we have is a mean, or a mean and a variance. The answers are the Markov and Chebyshev inequalities: crude, always-valid bounds that trade accuracy for the ability to say anything at all. Second, we ask what happens to the sample mean $M_n=(X_1+\cdots+X_n)/n$ of many i.i.d. terms as $n\to\infty$ (L19 slide 1). The answer is the weak law of large numbers, and the proof is one line of Chebyshev applied to a variance we compute in three steps. Making "$M_n\to\mu$" precise forces a new notion of limit for random variables — convergence in probability — whose definition is deceptively easy and whose content is easy to overstate.

The plan: §1.1 derives Markov, §1.2 derives Chebyshev from it (and independently, the way L19 slide 2 does it) and confronts how loose these bounds really are, §1.3 computes $\mathbb{E}[M_n]$ and $\operatorname{var}(M_n)$ and proves the weak law, §1.4 turns the weak law into a usable sample size through the pollster's problem of L19 slide 6, §1.5 makes convergence in probability precise and marks off exactly what it does not claim, and §1.6 works recitation 20 in full. The next section (§2) picks up the question L19 slide 7 leaves hanging — the shape of the limit.

1.1 The Markov inequality

Suppose the only thing you know about a random variable is that it is nonnegative and that its average is small. Intuition says it cannot often be huge: a variable that took a huge value with substantial probability would drag the average up. The Markov inequality is that sentence made exact.

Theorem (Markov inequality; B&T §5.1). Let $X$ be a random variable that takes only nonnegative values, i.e. $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 0)=1$, and let $a>0$ be any positive number (the "level" whose exceedance we want to bound). Then $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)\;\le\;\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}.$$ Here $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is the (finite) mean of $X$ and $\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)$ is the probability of the upper tail event $\{X\ge a\}$.

Derivation 1 — the truncation picture (B&T §5.1, its Figure 5.1). Fix $a>0$ and build a brand-new random variable $Y_a$ out of $X$ by flattening it: $$Y_a=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }X<a,\\[2pt] a,&\text{if }X\ge a.\end{cases}$$ In words: every outcome for which $X$ lands below $a$ is pushed down to $0$; every outcome for which $X$ reaches $a$ or beyond is pushed down to exactly $a$. Each step of the argument now goes as follows.

  1. $Y_a\le X$ always. Check the two cases separately. If $X<a$ then $Y_a=0\le X$, using $X\ge 0$ — this is the only place nonnegativity is used, and it is essential. If $X\ge a$ then $Y_a=a\le X$ by the very definition of the case. So the inequality $Y_a\le X$ holds outcome by outcome, for every point of the sample space.
  2. Therefore $\mathbb{E}[Y_a]\le \mathbb{E}[X]$. Expectation is monotone: if one random variable is never larger than another, its average cannot be larger either (G2 §2). Equivalently, $X-Y_a\ge 0$ pointwise, so $\mathbb{E}[X-Y_a]\ge 0$, and linearity turns this into $\mathbb{E}[X]-\mathbb{E}[Y_a]\ge 0$.
  3. Compute $\mathbb{E}[Y_a]$ exactly. $Y_a$ is a discrete random variable with exactly two possible values, $0$ and $a$, and $\mathbf{P}(Y_a=a)=\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)$ by construction. Hence $$\mathbb{E}[Y_a]\;=\;0\cdot\mathbf{P}(Y_a=0)\;+\;a\cdot\mathbf{P}(Y_a=a)\;=\;a\,\mathbf{P}(X\ge a).$$
  4. Combine. Steps 2 and 3 give $a\,\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)\le\mathbb{E}[X]$. Divide both sides by the strictly positive number $a$ (dividing by a positive number preserves the inequality) to get $\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)\le\mathbb{E}[X]/a$. $\blacksquare$

Derivation 2 — the indicator trick, in one line. The same argument written with an indicator random variable, which is the form worth memorizing. Let $\mathbf{1}_{\{X\ge a\}}$ be the indicator of the event $\{X\ge a\}$: it equals $1$ on outcomes where $X\ge a$ and $0$ elsewhere. Then

$$X\;\ge\;a\,\mathbf{1}_{\{X\ge a\}}\qquad\text{pointwise},$$

because on $\{X\ge a\}$ the right side is $a$ and the left side is at least $a$, while off $\{X\ge a\}$ the right side is $0$ and the left side is $\ge 0$ (nonnegativity again). Taking expectations of both sides and using monotonicity, then linearity, then the fundamental fact $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_A]=\mathbf{P}(A)$ (G2 §2):

$$\mathbb{E}[X]\;\ge\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[a\,\mathbf{1}_{\{X\ge a\}}\right]\;=\;a\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{X\ge a\}}\right]\;=\;a\,\mathbf{P}(X\ge a),$$

and dividing by $a>0$ finishes it. The two derivations are the same argument; $Y_a$ is literally $a\,\mathbf{1}_{\{X\ge a\}}$.

Fig. 1.1 — The Markov derivation for $X\sim\text{exponential}(1)$ at level $a=1.5$ (B&T §5.1, its Figure 5.1). All mass of $X$ below $a$ is moved to $0$ and all mass above $a$ is moved down to $a$; mass only ever moves left, so the mean can only decrease, giving $a\,\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)=\mathbb{E}[Y_a]\le\mathbb{E}[X]$. Here $\mathbb{E}[Y_a]=1.5\times 0.223=0.335\le 1=\mathbb{E}[X]$, so Markov returns the bound $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 1.5)\le 1/1.5=0.667$ against a true value of $0.223$.
Example 1.1 (Markov on a uniform; B&T Example 5.1). Let $X$ be uniformly distributed on $[0,4]$. Compare the Markov bounds on $\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)$ with the exact values, at $a=2,3,4$.

Solution. First the mean, from the uniform-density formula of G3 §1: for $X\sim\text{uniform}[\alpha,\beta]$, $\mathbb{E}[X]=(\alpha+\beta)/2$, so here $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{0+4}{2}=2 .$$ $X$ is nonnegative (its density is supported on $[0,4]$), so Markov applies. Its bound is $\mathbb{E}[X]/a=2/a$: $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge 2)\le \tfrac{2}{2}=1,\qquad \mathbf{P}(X\ge 3)\le \tfrac{2}{3}=0.6667,\qquad \mathbf{P}(X\ge 4)\le \tfrac{2}{4}=0.5 .$$ The exact values come from the uniform CDF, $\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)=(4-a)/4$ for $0\le a\le 4$: $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge 2)=\tfrac{2}{4}=0.5,\qquad \mathbf{P}(X\ge 3)=\tfrac{1}{4}=0.25,\qquad \mathbf{P}(X\ge 4)=0 .$$ So the bounds are off by factors of $2$, $2.667$, and infinitely much: the bound at $a=2$ is the completely vacuous statement "a probability is at most $1$", and at $a=4$ the bound $0.5$ misses a true value of $0$ entirely. Values recomputed as A_markov_a2/3/4 and A_exact_a2/3/4.

A second data point. For $X\sim\text{exponential}(1)$, $\mathbb{E}[X]=1$, so Markov gives $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 4)\le 1/4=0.25$, whereas the true value is $e^{-4}=0.01832$ (A_exp_markov_c4, A_exp_exact_c4) — a factor of $13.7$.

Interpretation — what Markov actually buys you. The bound uses one number about $X$: its mean. It cannot possibly distinguish between two nonnegative random variables with the same mean, however differently they are spread out. That is simultaneously its weakness (the bounds above are terrible) and its power (you can apply it when you know nothing else). Markov's real importance in this course is not as a numerical tool but as the engine: applied to $(X-\mu)^2$ it produces Chebyshev, applied to $|X_n-c|^2$ it proves that mean-square convergence implies convergence in probability (rec20 P2(e), §1.6), and applied to $e^{sX}$ it produces the Chernoff bounds of later courses.
Gotcha — three ways to misuse Markov.
  1. Nonnegativity is not optional. Let $X$ be $+1$ or $-1$ with probability $1/2$ each. Then $\mathbb{E}[X]=0$ and "Markov" would claim $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 1)\le 0/1=0$, while the truth is $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 1)=0.5$. Step 1 of the derivation breaks: on the outcome $X=-1$ we have $Y_1=0>-1=X$. If $X$ can be negative, apply Markov to $|X|$ or to $(X-\mu)^2$ instead — never to $X$ itself.
  2. The bound is useless whenever $a\le\mathbb{E}[X]$, since then $\mathbb{E}[X]/a\ge 1$ and the statement is "a probability is at most something $\ge 1$." Markov only says something when you ask about a level well above the mean.
  3. It bounds the upper tail only. $\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)$, not $\mathbf{P}(X\le a)$ and not $\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge a)$. For two-sided statements you need Chebyshev.
Interpretation — Markov is the best possible bound of its kind. The bound cannot be improved without extra assumptions, because it is attained. Fix $a>0$ and $0<m\le a$, and let $X$ take the value $a$ with probability $m/a$ and the value $0$ otherwise. Then $\mathbb{E}[X]=a\cdot(m/a)+0=m$ and $\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)=m/a=\mathbb{E}[X]/a$ exactly. The uniform of Example 1.1 is far from this shape, which is why its bound is loose; the two-point variable is precisely the worst case Markov must protect against.
Practice 1.1. $X$ is a nonnegative random variable with $\mathbb{E}[X]=3$. (a) Bound $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 12)$. (b) Exhibit a distribution for which your bound is exact. (c) Can Markov ever prove $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 12)<0.25$ from $\mathbb{E}[X]=3$ alone?
Solution

(a) $X\ge 0$ and $a=12>0$, so Markov applies: $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 12)\le \mathbb{E}[X]/12=3/12=0.25$ (I_markov_mean3_a12).

(b) Take $\mathbf{P}(X=12)=1/4$ and $\mathbf{P}(X=0)=3/4$. Then $\mathbb{E}[X]=12\cdot\tfrac14+0\cdot\tfrac34=3$ ✓ and $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 12)=1/4=0.25$, matching the bound with equality.

(c) No. Part (b) exhibits a nonnegative random variable with mean $3$ whose tail probability equals exactly $0.25$, so no valid bound depending only on $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $a$ can be smaller than $0.25$. Any strictly better bound must use more information about $X$ — which is exactly what Chebyshev does with the variance.

Practice 1.2. A machine's daily output $X$ is nonnegative with mean $50$ units. Your manager claims "so the chance of a day above $200$ units is under 10%." Is the claim justified by Markov? What is the best Markov can certify?
Solution

Markov gives $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 200)\le 50/200=0.25$, i.e. at most 25%. The manager's 10% claim is not justified by the mean alone: the two-point variable with $\mathbf{P}(X=200)=0.25$, $\mathbf{P}(X=0)=0.75$ has mean $50$ and a 25% chance of a day at $200$. To certify 10% you would need extra information — for instance a variance. If additionally $\sigma=30$, Chebyshev (§1.2) gives $\mathbf{P}(|X-50|\ge 150)\le 30^2/150^2=900/22500=0.04$, so $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 200)\le 0.04$, and now the 10% claim is safe.

1.2 The Chebyshev inequality

Chebyshev upgrades Markov by using a second number, the variance. Because the variance measures spread about the mean, the resulting bound is two-sided and much more useful in practice.

Theorem (Chebyshev inequality; L19 slide 2, B&T §5.1). Let $X$ be any random variable — no nonnegativity assumed — with finite mean $\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]$ and finite variance $\sigma^2=\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]$. Then for every $c>0$, $$\mathbf{P}\big(|X-\mu|\ge c\big)\;\le\;\frac{\sigma^2}{c^2}.$$ Equivalently, writing the distance in units of standard deviations by setting $c=k\sigma$ with $k>0$ (assuming $\sigma>0$), $$\mathbf{P}\big(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma\big)\;\le\;\frac{1}{k^2}.$$ Here $c$ is an absolute tolerance in the units of $X$, while $k$ is a dimensionless number of standard deviations.

Derivation 1 — from Markov, in three lines (B&T §5.1). Consider the random variable $W=(X-\mu)^2$. It is nonnegative by construction (a square), so Markov applies to it. Apply Markov at level $a=c^2>0$:

$$\mathbf{P}\big((X-\mu)^2\ge c^2\big)\;\le\;\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mu)^2\big]}{c^2}\;=\;\frac{\sigma^2}{c^2},$$

where the last equality is just the definition of variance. Now identify the event on the left. Since $t\mapsto t^2$ is increasing on $[0,\infty)$ and both $|X-\mu|$ and $c$ are nonnegative,

$$(X-\mu)^2\ge c^2\iff |X-\mu|\ge c ,$$

so the two events are literally the same event, and their probabilities are equal. Substituting gives $\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge c)\le\sigma^2/c^2$. Finally, put $c=k\sigma$: the right side becomes $\sigma^2/(k\sigma)^2=\sigma^2/(k^2\sigma^2)=1/k^2$, which is the second form. $\blacksquare$

Derivation 2 — the integral argument of L19 slide 2, with its gaps filled. The lecture slide argues directly, assuming $X$ continuous with density $f_X$. Slide 2 writes the limits as $\int_{-\infty}^{-c}$ and $\int_c^{\infty}$, which is shorthand valid when $\mu=0$; the correct general limits are $\mu-c$ and $\mu+c$. Written out:

$$ \begin{aligned} \sigma^2 \;&=\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(x-\mu)^2 f_X(x)\,dx &&\text{definition of variance for a continuous r.v.}\\[4pt] &=\; \int_{-\infty}^{\mu-c}(x-\mu)^2 f_X(x)\,dx \;+\; \int_{\mu-c}^{\mu+c}(\cdots)\,dx \;+\; \int_{\mu+c}^{\infty}(x-\mu)^2 f_X(x)\,dx &&\text{split the line into three pieces}\\[4pt] &\ge\; \int_{-\infty}^{\mu-c}(x-\mu)^2 f_X(x)\,dx \;+\; \int_{\mu+c}^{\infty}(x-\mu)^2 f_X(x)\,dx &&\text{the dropped middle integrand }(x-\mu)^2f_X(x)\ge 0\\[4pt] &\ge\; \int_{-\infty}^{\mu-c} c^2 f_X(x)\,dx \;+\; \int_{\mu+c}^{\infty} c^2 f_X(x)\,dx &&\text{on both retained regions }|x-\mu|\ge c\text{, so }(x-\mu)^2\ge c^2\\[4pt] &=\; c^2\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\mu-c} f_X(x)\,dx + \int_{\mu+c}^{\infty} f_X(x)\,dx\right] &&\text{pull out the constant }c^2\\[4pt] &=\; c^2\,\mathbf{P}\big(|X-\mu|\ge c\big) &&\text{the bracket is the probability of }\{x:|x-\mu|\ge c\}. \end{aligned} $$

Dividing the resulting $\sigma^2\ge c^2\,\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge c)$ by $c^2>0$ gives the inequality. The slide's compressed version is exactly this with the three justifications suppressed. Equivalently and without any continuity assumption, define the step function $$g(x)=\begin{cases}0,&|x-\mu|<c,\\ c^2,&|x-\mu|\ge c,\end{cases}$$ note $g(x)\le (x-\mu)^2$ for every $x$ (if $|x-\mu|<c$ then $g=0\le(x-\mu)^2$; if $|x-\mu|\ge c$ then $g=c^2\le(x-\mu)^2$), and take expectations: $\sigma^2=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]\ge\mathbb{E}[g(X)]=c^2\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge c)$. This is panel (b) of the figure below.

Example 1.2 (Chebyshev on a uniform and on an exponential; B&T Example 5.2). (a) $X\sim\text{uniform}[0,4]$: bound $\mathbf{P}(|X-2|\ge 1)$. (b) $X\sim\text{exponential}(1)$: bound $\mathbf{P}(X\ge c)$ for $c>1$, and compare with the exact answer.

Solution (a). For $X\sim\text{uniform}[\alpha,\beta]$ we have $\operatorname{var}(X)=(\beta-\alpha)^2/12$ (G3 §1), so $$\mu=2,\qquad \sigma^2=\frac{(4-0)^2}{12}=\frac{16}{12}=\frac{4}{3}=1.3333 .$$ Chebyshev with $c=1$: $$\mathbf{P}(|X-2|\ge 1)\;\le\;\frac{\sigma^2}{c^2}=\frac{4/3}{1^2}=\frac{4}{3}=1.3333 .$$ This is uninformative — it is larger than $1$, so it says nothing at all. The exact value is easy here: $\{|X-2|\ge 1\}=[0,1]\cup[3,4]$, total length $2$ out of $4$, so $\mathbf{P}=0.5$ (B_cheb_c1, B_exact_c1). Chebyshev needs $c$ to be a decent multiple of $\sigma=\sqrt{4/3}=1.155$ before it says anything; $c=1$ is less than one standard deviation.

Solution (b). For $X\sim\text{exponential}(\lambda=1)$, $\mathbb{E}[X]=1/\lambda=1$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)=1/\lambda^2=1$ (G3 §1), so $\mu=\sigma^2=1$. For $c>1$, first rewrite the one-sided event as a piece of a two-sided one: $$\{X\ge c\}=\{X-1\ge c-1\}\subseteq\{|X-1|\ge c-1\},$$ so by monotonicity of probability and then Chebyshev with tolerance $c-1>0$, $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge c)\;\le\;\mathbf{P}\big(|X-1|\ge c-1\big)\;\le\;\frac{\sigma^2}{(c-1)^2}=\frac{1}{(c-1)^2}.$$ The exact value is $\mathbf{P}(X\ge c)=e^{-c}$. The bound decays like $1/c^2$ while the truth decays like $e^{-c}$: for far tails Chebyshev is not merely loose, it is loose by a growing factor. This is the structural reason the central limit theorem of §2 (and, beyond this course, the Chernoff bound) is worth having.

Example 1.3 (what to do when you do not know $\sigma^2$ — the range bound; B&T Example 5.3). Suppose all you know is that $X$ takes values in a bounded range $[a,b]$. Show $\operatorname{var}(X)\le (b-a)^2/4$, so that $$\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge c)\;\le\;\frac{(b-a)^2}{4c^2}\quad\text{for all }c>0 .$$

Solution. Step 1: the variance is the smallest mean squared deviation. For any constant $\gamma$, $$\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\gamma)^2\big]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-2\gamma\,\mathbb{E}[X]+\gamma^2,$$ by expanding the square and using linearity. As a function of $\gamma$ this is an upward parabola; setting its derivative $-2\mathbb{E}[X]+2\gamma$ to zero gives the minimizer $\gamma=\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu$, whose value is $\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]=\sigma^2$. Hence $$\sigma^2\;\le\;\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\gamma)^2\big]\qquad\text{for every constant }\gamma .$$ Step 2: choose the midpoint $\gamma=(a+b)/2$. Then a direct expansion gives the identity $$\left(x-\frac{a+b}{2}\right)^2=(x-a)(x-b)+\frac{(b-a)^2}{4},$$ which you can verify by multiplying out both sides: the left is $x^2-(a+b)x+\frac{(a+b)^2}{4}$, the right is $x^2-(a+b)x+ab+\frac{(b-a)^2}{4}$, and $ab+\frac{(b-a)^2}{4}=\frac{4ab+b^2-2ab+a^2}{4}=\frac{(a+b)^2}{4}$ ✓. Taking expectations, $$\sigma^2\;\le\;\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X-\frac{a+b}{2}\right)^2\right]=\mathbb{E}\big[(X-a)(X-b)\big]+\frac{(b-a)^2}{4}\;\le\;\frac{(b-a)^2}{4},$$ because for every $x\in[a,b]$ we have $x-a\ge 0$ and $x-b\le 0$, so $(x-a)(x-b)\le 0$ pointwise and its expectation is $\le 0$.

Tightness. Equality holds when $X$ is $a$ or $b$ with probability $1/2$ each: then $\mu=(a+b)/2$ and $\sigma^2=\big(\frac{b-a}{2}\big)^2=\frac{(b-a)^2}{4}$. So the bound cannot be improved without more information.

How loose in practice. For $X\sim\text{uniform}[0,4]$ the range bound gives $\sigma^2\le 16/4=4$ against a true $4/3$, a factor of $3$ (B_rangebound_unif, B_rangebound_ratio). For a Bernoulli variable, which lives in $[0,1]$, the bound reads $\operatorname{var}\le 1/4$ — and this one is exactly the maximum of $p(1-p)$, attained at $p=1/2$. That special case is the workhorse of §1.4.

Interpretation — the $k$-sigma table everybody quotes. The form $\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma)\le 1/k^2$ says: no matter what the distribution is, at most $1/4$ of the probability sits two or more standard deviations from the mean, at most $1/9$ sits three or more, at most $1/16$ sits four or more. This is a genuine distribution-free guarantee. Its price is that for any specific distribution it is enormously conservative — for a normal random variable the true two-sigma tail is $0.0455$ against the bound $0.25$ (a factor of $5.5$), the three-sigma tail is $0.0027$ against $0.1111$ (a factor of $41$), and the four-sigma tail is $6.33\times 10^{-5}$ against $0.0625$ (a factor of $987$). Values C_gap_k2_normal, C_gap_k3_normal, C_gap_k4_normal.
Gotcha — four traps in Chebyshev.
  1. $k\le 1$ gives nothing. $1/k^2\ge 1$ then, so the bound is vacuous. Any conclusion you "derive" at $k=1$ is empty.
  2. It bounds $\ge$, not $>$, and it is two-sided. If you want a one-sided statement $\mathbf{P}(X-\mu\ge c)$, Chebyshev only gives you the two-sided bound, which is generally twice as much as you need for a symmetric distribution and is simply the best available in general.
  3. Do not read the bound as an estimate. "Chebyshev says the probability is $0.25$" is wrong; it says at most $0.25$, and the truth may be $0$ (uniform at $k=2$, see the widget) or $0.0455$ (normal) or exactly $0.25$ (the two-point variable below).
  4. Chebyshev needs a finite variance. For heavy-tailed variables with $\sigma^2=\infty$ the bound is the trivial $\mathbf{P}\le\infty$. (The weak law itself survives without finite variance — B&T remark in §5.2 — but its Chebyshev proof does not.)
Interpretation — Chebyshev is also attained. Fix $k>1$ and let $X$ take the values $-k$, $0$, $+k$ with probabilities $\frac{1}{2k^2}$, $1-\frac{1}{k^2}$, $\frac{1}{2k^2}$. Then $\mu=0$ by symmetry and $\sigma^2=k^2\cdot\frac{1}{2k^2}+0+k^2\cdot\frac{1}{2k^2}=1$, and $\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma)=\mathbf{P}(|X|\ge k)=\frac{1}{2k^2}+\frac{1}{2k^2}=\frac{1}{k^2}$ — equality. At $k=2$ that is masses $0.125,\,0.75,\,0.125$ and tail exactly $0.25$ (C_tight_p, C_tight_check_var). So the $1/k^2$ curve is not slack, it is the exact envelope over all distributions; it is loose only for the nice ones.
Recipe (which bound or approximation to reach for).
  1. Is the distribution known and the sum/integral tractable? Then do not bound at all — compute exactly. Bounds are for ignorance, not for laziness.
  2. Is the quantity a sample mean $M_n$ with $n$ large, and do you want a number rather than a guarantee? Use the central limit theorem (§2). It is an approximation, not a bound, but it is usually accurate to a few percent where Chebyshev is off by an order of magnitude.
  3. Do you know $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ (or an upper bound on $\sigma^2$)? Use Chebyshev, $\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge c)\le\sigma^2/c^2$. If $\sigma^2$ is unknown but $X\in[a,b]$, substitute $\sigma^2\le(b-a)^2/4$ (Example 1.3). If $X=M_n$, substitute $\sigma^2_{M_n}=\sigma^2/n$ (§1.3).
  4. Do you know only $\mathbb{E}[X]$, with $X\ge 0$? Markov, $\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)\le\mathbb{E}[X]/a$ — and check that $a>\mathbb{E}[X]$ or the answer is vacuous.
  5. Sanity check. Any bound $\ge 1$ is worthless; say so rather than reporting it. Any "bound" below the exact value you can verify means an algebra error.

Practice 1.3. A lightbulb's lifetime $X$ (hours) has $\mathbb{E}[X]=1000$ and $\sigma=100$. (a) Bound $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 1250)$ with Markov. (b) Bound $\mathbf{P}(|X-1000|\ge 250)$ with Chebyshev, and deduce a bound on $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 1250)$. (c) Which is better, and why?
Solution

(a) Lifetimes are nonnegative, so Markov at $a=1250$: $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 1250)\le 1000/1250=0.8$ (I_bulb_markov).

(b) Chebyshev with $c=250$: $\mathbf{P}(|X-1000|\ge 250)\le \sigma^2/c^2=100^2/250^2=10000/62500=0.16$ (I_bulb_cheb). Since $\{X\ge 1250\}\subseteq\{|X-1000|\ge 250\}$, monotonicity gives $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 1250)\le 0.16$.

(c) Chebyshev is five times better ($0.16$ vs $0.8$) because it uses the variance, which is the information that says the lifetime rarely strays far from $1000$. Note the deviation is $c/\sigma=250/100=2.5$ standard deviations, and indeed $1/2.5^2=0.16$ — the same number from the $k$-sigma form.

Practice 1.4. For which $k$ does the $k$-sigma Chebyshev bound first become better than the trivial statement "$\mathbf{P}\le 1$"? And for a uniform random variable, for which $k$ does the bound stop describing anything real at all?
Solution

The bound $1/k^2$ is below $1$ exactly when $k>1$; at $k=1$ it equals $1$ and is vacuous. So Chebyshev only ever says something beyond one standard deviation.

For $X$ uniform on any interval $[\alpha,\beta]$, write $h=(\beta-\alpha)/2$ for the half-width, so $\mu$ is the midpoint and $\sigma=h/\sqrt{3}$. Then $|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma$ requires $|X-\mu|\ge kh/\sqrt3$, which is impossible once $kh/\sqrt3>h$, i.e. once $k>\sqrt3=1.7321$ (C_sqrt3). So for every $k>1.7321$ the exact probability is $0$ while Chebyshev still promises $1/k^2>0$: the bound guards against an event that cannot occur. Practice 1.5. A quantity $X$ is known to lie in $[1.4,\,2.0]$ (metres) but nothing else about it is known. Bound $\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge 0.3)$.
Solution

By the range bound of Example 1.3, $\sigma^2\le (b-a)^2/4=(2.0-1.4)^2/4=0.36/4=0.09$ (I_p1c_sigma2). Chebyshev with $c=0.3$ then gives $$\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge 0.3)\le \frac{0.09}{0.3^2}=\frac{0.09}{0.09}=1 .$$ Vacuous — and necessarily so: $c=0.3$ is exactly $(b-a)/2$, and the two-point worst case ($X=1.4$ or $2.0$ with probability $1/2$ each) has $\mu=1.7$ and $|X-\mu|=0.3$ with probability $1$. So no better bound is possible from the range alone. This is a healthy reminder that a vacuous bound is sometimes the correct answer, not a mistake.

1.3 The sample mean $M_n$ and the weak law of large numbers

Now the object of the whole chapter. Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with common finite mean $\mu=\mathbb{E}[X_i]$ and common finite variance $\sigma^2=\operatorname{var}(X_i)$, and define the sample mean (L19 slide 1)

$$S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n,\qquad M_n=\frac{S_n}{n}=\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n}.$$

Here $n$ is the sample size, $S_n$ is the running sum, and $M_n$ is a random variable, not a number: run the experiment again and you get a different $M_n$. L19 slide 5 leaves its mean and variance blank for the lecture to fill in; here they are, with every step.

Property (mean and variance of the sample mean; L19 slide 5, B&T §5.2). $$\mathbb{E}[M_n]=\mu,\qquad \operatorname{var}(M_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n},\qquad \sigma_{M_n}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}.$$ Derivation of the mean. $$\mathbb{E}[M_n]\;\overset{(1)}{=}\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n}\right]\;\overset{(2)}{=}\;\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}[X_1+\cdots+X_n]\;\overset{(3)}{=}\;\frac{1}{n}\big(\mathbb{E}[X_1]+\cdots+\mathbb{E}[X_n]\big)\;\overset{(4)}{=}\;\frac{1}{n}\cdot n\mu\;=\;\mu .$$ (1) is the definition of $M_n$; (2) pulls the constant $1/n$ out of the expectation ($\mathbb{E}[aX]=a\mathbb{E}[X]$, G2 §2); (3) is linearity of expectation over a sum, which needs no independence; (4) uses that all $n$ terms have the same mean $\mu$ ("identically distributed"). Note that $\mathbb{E}[M_n]=\mu$ for every $n$, including $n=1$: the sample mean is an unbiased estimator of $\mu$. Derivation of the variance. $$\operatorname{var}(M_n)\;\overset{(1)}{=}\;\operatorname{var}\!\left(\frac{S_n}{n}\right)\;\overset{(2)}{=}\;\frac{1}{n^2}\operatorname{var}(S_n)\;\overset{(3)}{=}\;\frac{1}{n^2}\big(\operatorname{var}(X_1)+\cdots+\operatorname{var}(X_n)\big)\;\overset{(4)}{=}\;\frac{1}{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2\;=\;\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.$$ (1) is the definition; (2) uses the scaling rule $\operatorname{var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{var}(X)$ with $a=1/n$ (G2 §3) — note the square, which is what makes the variance shrink like $1/n$ rather than $1/n$-times-nothing; (3) is the only step that uses independence: the variance of a sum of independent random variables is the sum of their variances (G3 §4), and it is false in general otherwise; (4) uses identical distribution again. Taking square roots, the standard deviation of $M_n$ is $\sigma/\sqrt n$: to halve the typical error you must quadruple the sample.
Theorem (weak law of large numbers, WLLN; L19 slide 5, B&T §5.2). Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be i.i.d. with finite mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$. Then for every fixed $\epsilon>0$, $$\mathbf{P}\big(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon\big)\;\le\;\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\;0 .$$ Here $\epsilon$ is the accuracy you demand, fixed once and for all before letting $n$ grow.

Proof, every step.

  1. $M_n$ has finite mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2/n$ by the Property above, so the Chebyshev inequality of §1.2 applies to it.
  2. Apply Chebyshev to the random variable $M_n$, with its mean $\mu$ and its variance $\sigma^2/n$, at tolerance $c=\epsilon$: $$\mathbf{P}\big(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon\big)\;\le\;\frac{\operatorname{var}(M_n)}{\epsilon^2}\;=\;\frac{\sigma^2/n}{\epsilon^2}\;=\;\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}.$$ This is exactly the display on L19 slide 5.
  3. Fix $\epsilon>0$ and let $n\to\infty$. In the expression $\sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2)$ the numerator $\sigma^2$ and the factor $\epsilon^2$ are constants; only $n$ grows. So $\sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2)\to 0$ — this is an ordinary limit of a sequence of numbers, of the form $\text{constant}/n$.
  4. Squeeze. For every $n$, $0\le \mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le \sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2)$; the left bound is $0$ and the right bound tends to $0$, so the middle sequence tends to $0$: $$\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\big(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon\big)=0 .$$
  5. Since $\epsilon>0$ was arbitrary, this holds for every $\epsilon>0$, which is precisely the definition of "$M_n$ converges to $\mu$ in probability" given in §1.5. $\blacksquare$

(The theorem is in fact true assuming only that $\mathbb{E}[X_i]$ exists, with no finite-variance assumption, but the proof then requires machinery outside 6.041; B&T §5.2 states this and omits it.)

Fig. 1.4 — The weak law in one picture: the PMF of $M_n$ for i.i.d. Bernoulli$(1/2)$ terms, $\mu=0.5$, $\sigma^2=0.25$ (L19 slide 5; B&T §5.2, Example 5.4). The green band is $[\mu-0.1,\mu+0.1]$; the mass outside it falls from $0.625$ to $0.00139$ as $n$ goes from $4$ to $256$, while the Chebyshev bound $0.25/(n\cdot 0.01)$ falls from $1$ (vacuous) to $0.0977$. Interpretation — three ways to say the same thing.
  1. Statistical. Averaging $n$ independent measurements of the same quantity divides the standard deviation of the error by $\sqrt n$. That is the entire reason experiments are repeated.
  2. Geometric. The PMF/PDF of $M_n$ is a fixed-shape cloud that concentrates around $\mu$: fix any window $[\mu-\epsilon,\mu+\epsilon]$, however narrow, and eventually essentially all of the mass is inside it (Fig. 1.4).
  3. Foundational (B&T Example 5.4). Let $A$ be an event of probability $p$ in some experiment, repeat the experiment independently $n$ times, and let $X_i=1$ if $A$ occurs on trial $i$ and $0$ otherwise, so $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=p$. Then $M_n$ is the empirical frequency of $A$, and the weak law says the empirical frequency converges in probability to $p$. This is the bridge between probability as an axiomatic calculus (G1 §1) and probability as "long-run fraction of the time" — the interpretation we have been using informally since day one is a theorem, not a definition.
Gotcha — what the weak law does not say.
  1. It does not say $M_n$ eventually stays within $\epsilon$ of $\mu$ on a given run. It says the probability of being outside goes to zero, one $n$ at a time. The statement about a single infinite run — that with probability $1$ only finitely many $M_n$ escape the window — is the strong law, a genuinely different and stronger theorem. See the Gotcha in §1.5.
  2. Small $\epsilon$ costs a lot of $n$. The bound is $\sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2)$; halving $\epsilon$ multiplies the required $n$ by $4$. "$M_n\to\mu$" hides a very concrete price list, made explicit in §1.4.
  3. Independence is doing real work, in exactly one place. Step (3) of the variance derivation. Without it $\operatorname{var}(S_n)$ picks up $n(n-1)$ covariance terms and can be as large as $n^2\sigma^2$, in which case $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2$ and nothing concentrates.
  4. $M_n$ is not "getting closer to $\mu$ monotonically." Nothing in the theorem says $\mathbf{P}(|M_{n+1}-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)$ for every $n$ — only that the sequence tends to $0$.

Doing both gives the standardized variable of L19 slide 8, $$Z_n=\frac{S_n-\mathbb{E}[S_n]}{\sigma_{S_n}}=\frac{S_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n},\qquad \mathbb{E}[Z_n]=0,\quad\operatorname{var}(Z_n)=1\ \text{for every }n .$$ Verify those two claims: $\mathbb{E}[Z_n]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[S_n]-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}=\frac{n\mu-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}=0$, and $\operatorname{var}(Z_n)=\frac{\operatorname{var}(S_n)}{(\sigma\sqrt n)^2}=\frac{n\sigma^2}{n\sigma^2}=1$ (subtracting the constant $n\mu$ does not change a variance). Practice 1.6. A fair six-sided die is rolled $n$ times and $M_n$ is the average of the rolls. Using Chebyshev, how large must $n$ be to guarantee $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-3.5|\ge 0.1)\le 0.05$?
Solution

The single-roll mean is $\mu=(1+2+3+4+5+6)/6=21/6=3.5$ ✓. The single-roll variance is $$\sigma^2=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{j=1}^{6}(j-3.5)^2=\frac{(2.5)^2+(1.5)^2+(0.5)^2+(0.5)^2+(1.5)^2+(2.5)^2}{6}=\frac{6.25+2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25+6.25}{6}=\frac{17.5}{6}=\frac{35}{12}=2.9167$$ (I_die_mu, I_die_var). Practice 1.7. Show that $M_n$ converges to $\mu$ in the mean square sense, i.e. $\mathbb{E}[(M_n-\mu)^2]\to 0$, and explain why this immediately re-proves the weak law.
Solution

Since $\mathbb{E}[M_n]=\mu$, the quantity $\mathbb{E}[(M_n-\mu)^2]$ is by definition $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n$, which tends to $0$ as $n\to\infty$ for fixed $\sigma^2$. So $M_n\to\mu$ in mean square (I_ms_Mn).

By the general implication proved in rec20 P2(e) (§1.6 below) — mean-square convergence implies convergence in probability, via Markov applied to $(M_n-\mu)^2$ — this re-proves the WLLN. Indeed it is the same proof: Markov on $(M_n-\mu)^2$ at level $\epsilon^2$ is exactly Chebyshev on $M_n$ at tolerance $\epsilon$.

1.4 The pollster's problem: turning the weak law into a sample size

The weak law is an asymptotic statement, but the Chebyshev bound behind it is a finite-$n$ statement, and that is what makes it useful. L19 slide 6 (= B&T Example 5.5) is the canonical application.

Example 1.5 (the pollster's problem; L19 slide 6, B&T Example 5.5). Let $f$ be the unknown fraction of a large population who would answer "yes" to some question. We poll $n$ people chosen independently and uniformly at random, and record $$X_i=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if the }i\text{th person says yes},\\ 0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad M_n=\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n},$$ so $M_n$ is the fraction of "yes" answers in the sample and is our estimate of $f$. Goal: 95% confidence of at most 1% error, i.e. choose $n$ so that $$\mathbf{P}\big(|M_n-f|\ge 0.01\big)\;\le\;0.05 .$$

Solution, filling in the three steps the slide skips.

(i) The $X_i$ are i.i.d. Bernoulli$(f)$, so $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=f$ and hence $\mathbb{E}[M_n]=f$. "Chosen independently and uniformly" is what makes them independent and identically distributed; $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=1\cdot f+0\cdot(1-f)=f$. By the mean derivation of §1.3, $\mathbb{E}[M_n]=f$. This matters because Chebyshev must be centered at the true mean, and here the true mean is exactly the unknown $f$ we are estimating — which is what makes the deviation $|M_n-f|$ the estimation error.

(ii) The variance. $\operatorname{var}(X_i)=\mathbb{E}[X_i^2]-(\mathbb{E}[X_i])^2 = f-f^2=f(1-f)$, using $X_i^2=X_i$ because $X_i\in\{0,1\}$. Write $\sigma_x^2=f(1-f)$ for this per-person variance (the slide's notation). Then by §1.3, $$\sigma^2_{M_n}=\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\frac{\sigma_x^2}{n}=\frac{f(1-f)}{n}.$$

(iii) Chebyshev, then the worst-case variance. Chebyshev on $M_n$ at tolerance $\epsilon=0.01$: $$\mathbf{P}\big(|M_n-f|\ge 0.01\big)\;\le\;\frac{\sigma^2_{M_n}}{(0.01)^2}\;=\;\frac{f(1-f)}{n(0.01)^2}.$$ We cannot evaluate this, because $f$ is precisely what we do not know. So bound the variance: the parabola $g(f)=f(1-f)=f-f^2$ has $g'(f)=1-2f$, zero at $f=1/2$, with $g''=-2<0$, so $f=1/2$ is the maximum and $$\max_{0\le f\le 1} f(1-f)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac14 .$$ (Equivalently, $f(1-f)=\frac14-\big(f-\frac12\big)^2\le\frac14$.) This is the range bound of Example 1.3 for a variable confined to $[0,1]$. Hence, for every possible value of $f\text{,}$ $$\mathbf{P}\big(|M_n-f|\ge 0.01\big)\;\le\;\frac{1}{4n(0.01)^2}=\frac{1}{4n\times 10^{-4}}=\frac{2500}{n}.$$

(iv) Solve for $n$. Demand $\dfrac{1}{4n(0.01)^2}\le 0.05$: $$4n(0.01)^2\ge\frac{1}{0.05}=20\;\Longrightarrow\; n\ge\frac{20}{4\times 10^{-4}}=\frac{20}{0.0004}=50{,}000 .$$ Check at $n=50{,}000$: $\dfrac{1}{4\times 50000\times 0.0001}=\dfrac{1}{20}=0.05$ ✓ (E_check_50000, E_n_required), matching the slide's number exactly. In general the formula is $$\boxed{\,n\;\ge\;\frac{\sigma^2}{\epsilon^2\delta}\,}\qquad\text{and with the Bernoulli worst case }\sigma^2=\tfrac14:\qquad n\ge\frac{1}{4\epsilon^2\delta},$$ where $\epsilon$ is the accuracy and $\delta$ the allowed failure probability ($\delta=1-\text{confidence}$).

(v) How conservative is "conservative"? The slide labels $50{,}000$ conservative and leaves it there. Three comparisons make it concrete.

  • The bound $1/(4n\epsilon^2)$ at $n=50{,}000$ equals $0.05$, but the true probability (at the worst-case $f=1/2$, computed exactly from the binomial) is $7.90\times 10^{-6}$ — smaller by a factor of about $6300$ (E_exact_50000).
  • The normal approximation of §2 needs only $n=(z_{0.975}/(2\epsilon))^2=(1.95996/0.02)^2=9604$ people for the same specification (E_z975, E_n_clt) — a factor $5.21$ fewer (E_ratio_cheb_clt). Chebyshev makes you pay for about $40{,}000$ extra interviews.
  • If you happened to know $f\approx 0.1$, then $\sigma_x^2=0.1\times 0.9=0.09$ and $n\ge 0.09/(0.01^2\times 0.05)=18{,}000$ suffices (E_var_f01, E_n_required_f01) — the $1/4$ is worst-case insurance you can drop if you have information.

The smaller specification of B&T Example 5.5 works the same way: at $\epsilon=0.1$ and $n=100$, $\mathbf{P}(|M_{100}-f|\ge 0.1)\le 1/(4\times 100\times 0.01)=0.25$ (E_n100_eps01).

Recipe (sample size from Chebyshev).
  1. Name the estimator. Write the quantity you will report as a sample mean $M_n=(X_1+\cdots+X_n)/n$ of i.i.d. terms, and identify $\mu=\mathbb{E}[X_i]$ as the quantity you are estimating. Check $\mathbb{E}[M_n]=\mu$ (unbiasedness).
  2. Name the specification. Accuracy $\epsilon$ (how close) and confidence $1-\delta$ (how often), turned into the single requirement $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\delta$.
  3. Get a variance you can actually use. $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n$. If $\sigma^2$ is unknown, replace it by an upper bound: $1/4$ for a $\{0,1\}$ variable, $(b-a)^2/4$ for a variable confined to $[a,b]$.
  4. Solve $\sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2)\le\delta$ for $n$: $\;n\ge \sigma^2/(\epsilon^2\delta)$, and round up to an integer.
  5. Report it as a guarantee, not an estimate, and say which worst case you insured against. If the number is impractical, either accept an approximation (CLT, §2) or improve your knowledge of $\sigma^2$.
Gotcha — the two sensitivities are not the same. From $n\ge\sigma^2/(\epsilon^2\delta)$: the required $n$ scales like $1/\epsilon^2$ but only like $1/\delta$. Demanding twice the accuracy costs four times the data; demanding twice the confidence level (in the sense of halving $\delta$) costs only twice the data. Accuracy is the expensive axis. A second trap: $\epsilon$ here is an absolute error in the units of $\mu$, not a relative one — "within 1%" in the pollster problem means $\pm 0.01$ in the fraction, not $\pm 1\%$ of $f$.
Interpretation — what the $50{,}000$ really means. It is not a prediction that a poll of $50{,}000$ will be off by about $1\%$; the true error is far smaller (Fig. 1.5). It is a certificate: "whatever the unknown $f$ is, and whatever else may be true about the population, the chance my reported fraction is off by $0.01$ or more is at most $5\%$." Three separate worst cases are stacked inside it — Chebyshev's own worst-case distribution (§1.2), the worst-case variance $f=1/2$, and the fact that only $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ are used. Each layer is individually unimprovable, but their product is what makes the number five times larger than it needs to be. §2 removes the first and largest layer by replacing the bound with the actual limiting shape of $M_n$.
Practice 1.8 (B&T Problem 5.4). Alvin estimates the fraction $f$ of smokers by $M_n=S_n/n$ and picks the smallest $n$ for which Chebyshev guarantees $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-f|\ge\epsilon)\le\delta$. How does the recommended $n$ change if (a) $f$ is halved, (b) $\delta$ is halved?
Solution

Chebyshev gives $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-f|\ge\epsilon)\le \dfrac{f(1-f)}{n\epsilon^2}$, so the smallest recommended sample size is $$n=\frac{f(1-f)}{\epsilon^2\delta}.$$

(a) Replacing $f$ by $f/2$ replaces $f(1-f)$ by $\frac f2\big(1-\frac f2\big)$, so the recommended $n$ is multiplied by $$\frac{\frac f2(1-\frac f2)}{f(1-f)}=\frac{1-\frac f2}{2(1-f)} .$$ This is not a clean factor of one half; it depends on $f$. For small $f$ it tends to $1/2$ (halving a small $f$ roughly halves the required $n$), while for $f$ near $1$ it grows without bound, since the variance $f(1-f)$ is maximal at $f=1/2$. Numerically at $f=0.4$: $f(1-f)=0.24$ becomes $0.2\times 0.8=0.16$, a factor $0.16/0.24=0.6667$ (I_p4_f40, I_p4_f20, I_p4_ratio_f04_02). At $f=0.2\to 0.1$: $0.16\to 0.09$, factor $0.5625$ (I_p4_f10). Caution: if $f<1/2$ then halving it decreases the variance and hence $n$; if $f>1/2$ halving it may move $f$ toward $1/2$ and increase $n$.

(b) $n$ is proportional to $1/\delta$, so halving $\delta$ exactly doubles the recommended $n$, regardless of $f$ and $\epsilon$.

1.5 Convergence in probability

"$M_n$ converges to $\mu$" cannot mean what it means for numbers, because $M_1,M_2,\dots$ is a sequence of random variables, not of numbers. L19 slides 3–4 introduce the right notion by placing the two definitions side by side.

Definition (convergence of a deterministic sequence; L19 slide 3, B&T §5.3). Let $a_1,a_2,\dots$ be real numbers and $a$ a real number. We say $a_n$ converges to $a$, written $\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=a$, if $$\text{for every }\epsilon>0\ \text{ there exists }n_0\ \text{ such that }\ |a_n-a|\le\epsilon\ \text{ for every }n\ge n_0 .$$ Here $\epsilon$ is the accuracy demanded and $n_0$ is how long you must wait to achieve it; $n_0$ is allowed to depend on $\epsilon$. In words (the slide's phrasing): "$a_n$ eventually gets and stays arbitrarily close to $a$."
Definition (convergence in probability; L19 slide 4, B&T §5.3). Let $Y_1,Y_2,\dots$ be random variables (they need not be independent, and need not be sample means) and let $a$ be a real number. We say $Y_n$ converges to $a$ in probability if $$\text{for every }\epsilon>0:\qquad \lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\big(|Y_n-a|\ge\epsilon\big)=0 .$$ Note what changed: $|Y_n-a|$ is now a random quantity, so we cannot demand it be small; instead we demand that the probability of it being large be a sequence of numbers tending to $0$ — and that inner limit is an ordinary deterministic limit in the sense of the previous definition. The slide's gloss: "almost all of the PMF/PDF of $Y_n$ eventually gets concentrated arbitrarily close to $a$."
Interpretation — the two-tolerance form (B&T §5.3). Unwinding the inner limit gives an equivalent and more operational statement: $$\text{for every accuracy }\epsilon>0\ \text{and every confidence tolerance }\delta>0,\ \text{there is }n_0\ \text{with }\ \mathbf{P}\big(|Y_n-a|>\epsilon\big)<\delta\ \text{ for all }n\ge n_0 .$$ "For any prescribed accuracy and confidence, $Y_n$ equals $a$ to within those levels, provided $n$ is large enough." This is exactly the language of the pollster's problem in §1.4, where $\epsilon=0.01$, $\delta=0.05$ and $n_0=50{,}000$ — the definition is the specification, and the Chebyshev computation is the proof that some $n_0$ works.
Example 1.6 (the slide's own [UNSOLVED EXAMPLE]; L19 slide 4). The slide draws the PMF $\mathbf{P}(Y_n=0)=1-\frac1n$, $\mathbf{P}(Y_n=n)=\frac1n$ and asks "Does $Y_n$ converge?" — with no answer. Answer it.

Solution. First, the candidate limit must be $0$, since that is where the mass is piling up. Fix any $\epsilon>0$ and compute the required probability exactly from the PMF (no inequality needed). The random variable $Y_n$ takes only the values $0$ and $n$, so $$|Y_n-0|\ge\epsilon\iff Y_n\ge\epsilon\iff Y_n=n\quad\text{(provided }n\ge\epsilon\text{, i.e. for all large enough }n\text{)},$$ because the value $0$ fails $|0|\ge\epsilon$ for $\epsilon>0$. Hence, for every $n\ge\epsilon$, $$\mathbf{P}\big(|Y_n-0|\ge\epsilon\big)=\mathbf{P}(Y_n=n)=\frac1n .$$ Since $1/n\to 0$ as $n\to\infty$, and this holds for every $\epsilon>0$, the definition is satisfied: $$\boxed{Y_n\ \text{converges to }0\ \text{in probability.}}$$ But look at what does not converge. $$\mathbb{E}[Y_n]=0\cdot\Big(1-\frac1n\Big)+n\cdot\frac1n=1\quad\text{for every }n,$$ so $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]=1\not\to 0$ (section G of the computes; $\operatorname{var}(Y_n)=n-1$ is verified symbolically there as G_varY_check_n2/5/37). And $$\mathbb{E}[Y_n^2]=0^2\cdot\Big(1-\frac1n\Big)+n^2\cdot\frac1n=n\to\infty,$$ so $Y_n$ does not converge to $0$ in mean square either. The single rare value $n$ is negligible for probabilities but dominant for averages: its probability $1/n$ shrinks exactly as fast as its size $n$ grows, so their product stays at $1$. This one example is the counterexample to almost every plausible-but-false statement about convergence in probability, and it reappears as rec20 P2 in §1.6.

Fig. 1.6 — Convergence in probability, and everything it fails to imply (L19 slide 4; rec20 P2; B&T Example 5.8). As $n$ grows the escaping mass $1/n$ shrinks to nothing, so $Y_n\to 0$ in probability; simultaneously $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]$ is stuck at $1$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y_n^2]=n$ diverges.
Gotcha — the four things convergence in probability does not say.
  1. It says nothing about any single realized sequence. The definition is a statement about the sequence of numbers $\mathbf{P}(|Y_n-a|\ge\epsilon)$, one probability per $n$; it never quantifies over infinite runs. Concretely, let $Y_2,Y_3,\dots$ be independent with $\mathbf{P}(Y_n=1)=1/n$ and $\mathbf{P}(Y_n=0)=1-1/n$. Then $\mathbf{P}(|Y_n|\ge\tfrac12)=1/n\to 0$, so $Y_n\to 0$ in probability. Yet the probability that the whole run $n=2,\dots,N$ never produces a $1$ is $$\prod_{n=2}^{N}\Big(1-\frac1n\Big)=\prod_{n=2}^{N}\frac{n-1}{n}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\cdots\frac{N-1}{N}=\frac1N\ \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\ 0$$ (a telescoping product; verified numerically at $N=12$ in J_telescope_check_N12, and equal to $10^{-6}$ at $N=10^{6}$). So with probability $1$ the realized sequence keeps jumping back to $1$ forever, infinitely often, even though it converges in probability to $0$. The statement that a run must settle down is convergence "with probability 1" (the strong law of large numbers), which is strictly stronger and is not proved in 6.041.
  2. It does not imply $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]\to a$. Example 1.6: $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]=1$ for all $n$ while $Y_n\to 0$. (rec20 P2(d).)
  3. It does not imply convergence in mean square. Example 1.6 again: $\mathbb{E}[Y_n^2]=n\to\infty$. (rec20 P2(f).) The implication runs one way only — mean square $\Rightarrow$ probability, proved in rec20 P2(e).
  4. A vacuous Chebyshev bound proves nothing either way. If $\operatorname{var}(Y_n)\to\infty$, Chebyshev is silent; that is not evidence against convergence. In Example 1.6, $\operatorname{var}(Y_n)=n-1\to\infty$, Chebyshev tells you nothing, and yet $Y_n\to 0$ in probability. Always try the exact PMF/CDF calculation before concluding.
Property (the workhorse test; B&T §5.3). If every $Y_n$ has the same mean $\mu$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y_n)\to 0$, then $Y_n\to\mu$ in probability. Why. Chebyshev gives $\mathbf{P}(|Y_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\operatorname{var}(Y_n)/\epsilon^2$ for each fixed $\epsilon>0$; the right-hand side is a sequence of numbers tending to $0/\epsilon^2=0$, and squeezing gives the claim. The WLLN is the special case $Y_n=M_n$, $\operatorname{var}=\sigma^2/n$. A slightly more general version, used in rec20 P2(b): if $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]=\mu_n$ with $\mu_n\to\mu$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y_n)\to 0$, the same conclusion holds, because $$\{|Y_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon\}\subseteq\{|Y_n-\mu_n|\ge\epsilon/2\}\cup\{|\mu_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon/2\}$$ (if both deviations were smaller than $\epsilon/2$, the triangle inequality would give $|Y_n-\mu|<\epsilon$), the second event is empty for $n$ large enough since $\mu_n\to\mu$, and the first has probability at most $4\operatorname{var}(Y_n)/\epsilon^2\to 0$.
Example 1.7 (minimum of uniforms; B&T Example 5.6). Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be i.i.d. uniform on $[0,1]$ and $Y_n=\min\{X_1,\dots,X_n\}$. Show $Y_n\to 0$ in probability.

Solution. $Y_n\ge 0$ always, so $|Y_n-0|\ge\epsilon$ is the event $\{Y_n\ge\epsilon\}$. The minimum of a list is $\ge\epsilon$ exactly when every entry is $\ge\epsilon$, so $$\mathbf{P}(Y_n\ge\epsilon)=\mathbf{P}(X_1\ge\epsilon,\dots,X_n\ge\epsilon)=\mathbf{P}(X_1\ge\epsilon)\cdots\mathbf{P}(X_n\ge\epsilon)=(1-\epsilon)^n$$ for $0<\epsilon<1$, where the middle equality is independence and $\mathbf{P}(X_i\ge\epsilon)=1-\epsilon$ from the uniform CDF. Since $0<1-\epsilon<1$, the geometric sequence $(1-\epsilon)^n\to 0$. For $\epsilon\ge 1$ the probability is $0$ outright. So $Y_n\to 0$ in probability. Numerically at $\epsilon=0.1$: $0.9^1=0.9$, $0.9^5=0.5905$, $0.9^{20}=0.1216$, $0.9^{100}=2.656\times 10^{-5}$ (H_min_tail_n1/5/20/100).

Example 1.8 (a dependent sequence; B&T Example 5.7). Let $Y\sim\text{exponential}(1)$ and set $Y_n=Y/n$ — note these are all built from the same $Y$, hence heavily dependent, which the definition permits. Then for $\epsilon>0$, $$\mathbf{P}\big(|Y_n-0|\ge\epsilon\big)=\mathbf{P}\Big(\frac Yn\ge\epsilon\Big)=\mathbf{P}(Y\ge n\epsilon)=e^{-n\epsilon}\ \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\ 0,$$ using the exponential tail formula $\mathbf{P}(Y\ge t)=e^{-t}$ (G3 §1). So $Y_n\to 0$ in probability. The example is worth keeping in mind whenever you are tempted to think convergence in probability requires independence or i.i.d. structure: it requires neither.

Practice 1.11. Give an example of random variables $Y_n$ with $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]\to 0$ that do not converge to $0$ in probability, showing that the implication in Gotcha item 2 also fails in the other direction.
Solution

The example must keep a non-vanishing chance of a non-vanishing value (so that convergence in probability fails) while still driving the mean to zero, which forces the values to cancel — hence a negative part. Take $$\mathbf{P}(Y_n=1)=\tfrac12,\qquad \mathbf{P}(Y_n=-1+\tfrac1n)=\tfrac12 .$$ Then $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]=\tfrac12\big(1-1+\tfrac1n\big)=\frac{1}{2n}\to 0$, yet $\mathbf{P}(|Y_n|\ge\tfrac12)=1$ for every $n\ge 2$, so $Y_n$ does not converge to $0$ in probability. Conclusion: convergence of means and convergence in probability are logically independent — neither implies the other. (What is true: convergence in mean square implies both.)

Example 1.9 (rec20 P1 — estimating lightbulb quality). You must estimate the probability $p$ that a bulb produced by a factory is defect-free. All bulbs have the same defect probability and different bulbs are independent.

(b) If you test 50 bulbs, what is the probability that your estimate is in the range $p\pm 0.1$?
(c) Management wants the estimate within $p\pm 0.1$ with probability $0.95$. How many bulbs must you test?

Solution

(a) The estimator and why it works. Let $X_i=1$ if the $i$th bulb tested is good and $X_i=0$ if it is defective. By the problem's assumptions the $X_i$ are i.i.d. Bernoulli$(p)$. Take the sample mean $$M_n=\frac{X_1+X_2+\cdots+X_n}{n},$$ the observed fraction of good bulbs. [Notation: the recitation writes $Z_n$ for this estimator. We call it $M_n$ throughout, because $Z_n$ is reserved in these notes for the standardized variable $(S_n-n\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt n)$ of §1.3 and §2 — a different object with mean $0$ and variance $1$.] By §1.3, $$\mathbb{E}[M_n]=p,\qquad \operatorname{var}(M_n)=\frac{n\operatorname{var}(X_i)}{n^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n},\qquad \sigma^2=\operatorname{var}(X_i)=p(1-p).$$ Chebyshev applied to $M_n$ at tolerance $\epsilon>0$ gives $$\mathbf{P}\big(|M_n-p|\ge\epsilon\big)\le\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}.$$ For fixed $\epsilon$, the right-hand side is $\text{constant}/n\to 0$, so by the squeeze $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-p|\ge\epsilon)\to 0$ for every $\epsilon>0$; that is the definition of convergence in probability. Hence $M_n\to p$ in probability. (Note the estimator is also unbiased, $\mathbb{E}[M_n]=p$ for every $n$, which is a separate and stronger-looking property.)

Example 1.10 (rec20 P2 — two-point PMFs and the limits of Chebyshev). Let $$p_{X_n}(x)=\begin{cases}1-\frac1n,&x=0\\ \frac1n,&x=1\end{cases}\qquad\qquad p_{Y_n}(y)=\begin{cases}1-\frac1n,&y=0\\ \frac1n,&y=n\end{cases}$$ (a) Find $\mathbb{E}[X_n],\operatorname{var}(X_n),\mathbb{E}[Y_n],\operatorname{var}(Y_n)$. (b) What does Chebyshev tell us about the convergence of $X_n$? of $Y_n$? (c) Is $Y_n$ convergent in probability, and to what? (d) If $Y_n\to a$ in probability, must $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]\to a$? (e) A sequence converges to $c$ in the mean square if $\lim_n\mathbb{E}[(X_n-c)^2]=0$; use Markov to show mean-square convergence implies convergence in probability. (f) Show the converse fails.
Solution

(a) Means and variances. Both variables take exactly two values, so every expectation is a two-term sum.

$$\mathbb{E}[X_n]=0\cdot\Big(1-\frac1n\Big)+1\cdot\frac1n=\frac1n .$$

For the variance use the definition $\operatorname{var}=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2]$, i.e. sum $(\text{value}-\text{mean})^2\times\text{probability}$. [Gap filled: the official solution skips the algebra to $(n-1)/n^2$.] $$\operatorname{var}(X_n)=\Big(0-\frac1n\Big)^2\Big(1-\frac1n\Big)+\Big(1-\frac1n\Big)^2\frac1n =\frac{1}{n^2}\cdot\frac{n-1}{n}+\frac{(n-1)^2}{n^2}\cdot\frac1n =\frac{n-1}{n^3}+\frac{(n-1)^2}{n^3}=\frac{(n-1)\big[1+(n-1)\big]}{n^3}=\frac{(n-1)n}{n^3}=\frac{n-1}{n^2}.$$ (Cross-check with $\operatorname{var}=\mathbb{E}[X_n^2]-(\mathbb{E}[X_n])^2$: since $X_n^2=X_n$, this is $\frac1n-\frac1{n^2}=\frac{n-1}{n^2}$ ✓. Sample values: $\operatorname{var}(X_{100})=99/10000=0.0099$, $\operatorname{var}(Y_{100})=99$.

(b) What Chebyshev says. For $X_n$, centered at its own mean $1/n$: $$\mathbf{P}\Big(\Big|X_n-\frac1n\Big|\ge\epsilon\Big)\le\frac{\operatorname{var}(X_n)}{\epsilon^2}=\frac{n-1}{n^2\epsilon^2}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,$$ since $(n-1)/n^2\approx 1/n\to 0$. Numerically at $\epsilon=0.1$ the bound is $9.0$ (vacuous) at $n=10$, $0.99$ at $n=100$, $0.0999$ at $n=1000$ (K_Xn_bound_*). So $X_n-\frac1n\to 0$ in probability. [Gap filled: the official solution then jumps to "$X_n\to 0$ in probability" without argument.] Here is the argument, an instance of the Property in §1.5 with $\mu_n=1/n\to 0$. Fix $\epsilon>0$. If $|X_n|\ge\epsilon$ then, by the triangle inequality $|X_n|\le|X_n-\frac1n|+\frac1n$, we must have $|X_n-\frac1n|\ge\epsilon-\frac1n$. For $n>2/\epsilon$ we have $\epsilon-\frac1n>\epsilon/2$, so $$\mathbf{P}(|X_n|\ge\epsilon)\le\mathbf{P}\Big(\Big|X_n-\frac1n\Big|\ge\frac\epsilon2\Big)\le\frac{4(n-1)}{n^2\epsilon^2}\to 0 .$$ Hence $X_n\to 0$ in probability. (Directly from the PMF this is obvious: $\mathbf{P}(|X_n|\ge\epsilon)=\mathbf{P}(X_n=1)=1/n$ for $0<\epsilon\le 1$.)

For $Y_n$, Chebyshev gives $$\mathbf{P}\big(|Y_n-1|\ge\epsilon\big)\le\frac{\operatorname{var}(Y_n)}{\epsilon^2}=\frac{n-1}{\epsilon^2}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\infty .$$ The bound blows up: at $n=100$, $\epsilon=1$ it is $99$. A bound of $99$ on a probability is worthless. Conclusion: Chebyshev tells us nothing at all about $Y_n$. Not "$Y_n$ diverges" — merely that this particular tool is silent.

(c) Does $Y_n$ converge in probability? Yes, to $0$ — and we find out by abandoning Chebyshev and reading the PMF. [Gap filled: the official solution asserts $\mathbf{P}(|Y_n|\ge\epsilon)\le 1/n$ without saying where it comes from.] Fix $\epsilon>0$. $Y_n$ takes only the two values $0$ and $n$. The value $0$ satisfies $|0|<\epsilon$, so it never contributes; the value $n$ satisfies $|n|\ge\epsilon$ as soon as $n\ge\epsilon$. Therefore, for every $n\ge\epsilon$, $$\mathbf{P}\big(|Y_n-0|\ge\epsilon\big)=\mathbf{P}(Y_n=n)=\frac1n\ \longrightarrow\ 0 .$$ Since this holds for every $\epsilon>0$, $Y_n\to 0$ in probability. Note the contrast with (b): the exact calculation succeeds precisely where the variance-based bound fails, because the variance is inflated by a value that is enormous but almost never taken.

(d) Does convergence in probability force $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]\to a$? No — and $Y_n$ itself is the counterexample. It converges in probability to $a=0$ by part (c), yet $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]=1$ for every $n$ by part (a), so $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]\to 1\ne 0$. The reason, again: the escaping value $n$ has probability $1/n$, so its contribution to the mean is $n\times\frac1n=1$, a constant, even as its probability vanishes.

(e) Mean square $\Rightarrow$ probability. Suppose $\mathbb{E}[(X_n-c)^2]\to 0$. Fix $\epsilon>0$. The random variable $W_n=(X_n-c)^2$ is nonnegative, so Markov applies to it at level $a=\epsilon^2>0$: $$\mathbf{P}\big(|X_n-c|\ge\epsilon\big)\overset{(1)}{=}\mathbf{P}\big((X_n-c)^2\ge\epsilon^2\big)\overset{(2)}{\le}\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[(X_n-c)^2\big]}{\epsilon^2}.$$ Step (1) is the equivalence of the events, since squaring is increasing on $[0,\infty)$ and both $|X_n-c|$ and $\epsilon$ are nonnegative; step (2) is Markov. [SOURCE TYPO — corrected: the printed solution writes the denominator as $c^2$; it must be $\epsilon^2$, since Markov's level is $\epsilon^2$. With $c^2$ the statement would be false — take $c$ huge and the "bound" would be vacuous, or $c=0$ and it would be undefined.] [Gap filled: the source takes the limit without naming the hypothesis.] Now let $n\to\infty$: by hypothesis the numerator $\mathbb{E}[(X_n-c)^2]\to 0$ while $\epsilon^2$ is a fixed positive constant, so the right side tends to $0$; squeezing between $0$ and it, $$\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\big(|X_n-c|\ge\epsilon\big)=0 .$$ As $\epsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $X_n\to c$ in probability. $\blacksquare$ (Observe this is a strict generalization of the WLLN proof: put $X_n=M_n$, $c=\mu$, and $\mathbb{E}[(M_n-\mu)^2]=\sigma^2/n\to 0$.)

(f) Probability $\not\Rightarrow$ mean square. Use $Y_n$ once more. It converges to $0$ in probability by (c), but $$\mathbb{E}\big[(Y_n-0)^2\big]=0^2\cdot\Big(1-\frac1n\Big)+n^2\cdot\frac1n=n\ \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\ \infty ,$$ which is as far from $0$ as possible. So $Y_n$ does not converge to $0$ in the mean square, and the implication in (e) cannot be reversed. The hierarchy is therefore strict: mean square $\Rightarrow$ in probability, with no arrow back.

Example 1.11 (rec20 P3 — three transformed i.i.d. uniform sequences). $X$ is uniform on $[-1,1]$ and $X_1,X_2,\dots$ are i.i.d. copies of $X$. Which of the following converge in probability, and to what? (a) $X_i$; (b) $Y_i=X_i/i$; (c) $Z_i=(X_i)^i$.
Solution

Setup. For $X\sim\text{uniform}[-1,1]$ the density is $f_X(x)=1/2$ on $[-1,1]$, so for $0\le t\le 1$ $$\mathbf{P}(X>t)=\int_t^{1}\tfrac12\,dx=\tfrac{1-t}{2},\qquad \mathbf{P}(X<-t)=\tfrac{1-t}{2},\qquad \mathbf{P}(|X|>t)=1-t .$$ Also $\mathbb{E}[X]=0$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)=\frac{(1-(-1))^2}{12}=\frac{4}{12}=\frac13$ (H_var_unif11).

(a) $X_i$ does not converge in probability. [Gap filled: the source gives only the bare observation that the distribution does not change with $i$.] Here is the argument. Suppose, for contradiction, $X_i\to a$ in probability for some number $a$. Take $\epsilon=1/2$. Because every $X_i$ has the same distribution as $X$, the probability $\mathbf{P}(|X_i-a|\ge 1/2)=\mathbf{P}(|X-a|\ge 1/2)$ does not depend on $i$ at all; call it $q$. Convergence in probability would require this constant sequence to tend to $0$, i.e. $q=0$, i.e. $\mathbf{P}(|X-a|<1/2)=1$: all of the mass of $X$ would have to lie in an interval of length $1$. But $X$ is uniform on an interval of length $2$, so any interval of length $1$ captures probability at most $1/2<1$. Contradiction. Hence no limit exists. Concretely, $\mathbf{P}(|X_i|>0.5)=0.5$ for every $i$ (H_Xi_tail) — nothing concentrates anywhere.

(b) $Y_i=X_i/i\to 0$ in probability. Fix $\epsilon>0$. Practice 1.12 (B&T Problem 5.5(c)). With $X_1,X_2,\dots$ i.i.d. uniform on $[-1,1]$, let $W_n=X_1X_2\cdots X_n$. Show $W_n\to 0$ in probability. (Hint: Markov on $|W_n|$.)
Solution

$|W_n|=|X_1||X_2|\cdots|X_n|$ is nonnegative, and the $|X_i|$ are independent, so expectation factorizes: $$\mathbb{E}\big[|W_n|\big]=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[|X_i|\big]=\Big(\mathbb{E}|X|\Big)^n .$$ For $X$ uniform on $[-1,1]$, $\mathbb{E}|X|=\int_{-1}^{1}|x|\tfrac12\,dx=2\int_0^1 x\cdot\tfrac12\,dx=\big[\tfrac{x^2}{2}\big]_0^1=\tfrac12$ (L_EabsX_unif11). Hence $\mathbb{E}|W_n|=(1/2)^n$. Now Markov, applied to the nonnegative $|W_n|$ at level $\epsilon>0$: $$\mathbf{P}\big(|W_n-0|\ge\epsilon\big)=\mathbf{P}\big(|W_n|\ge\epsilon\big)\le\frac{\mathbb{E}|W_n|}{\epsilon}=\frac{(1/2)^n}{\epsilon}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,$$ since $(1/2)^n\to 0$ geometrically and $\epsilon$ is fixed. Therefore $W_n\to 0$ in probability. At $\epsilon=0.1$ the bound is $5.0$ (vacuous) at $n=1$, $0.3125$ at $n=5$, $0.009766$ at $n=10$, $9.54\times 10^{-6}$ at $n=20$ (L_prod_markov_n*), first dropping below $1$ at $n=4$ (L_prod_first_useful_n).

Practice 1.13 (B&T Problem 5.5(d)). Same $X_i$; let $V_n=\max\{X_1,\dots,X_n\}$. Does $V_n$ converge in probability, and to what?
Solution

Guess the limit $1$, the top of the range. Fix $0<\epsilon<2$. Since $V_n\le 1$ always, $|V_n-1|>\epsilon$ means $V_n<1-\epsilon$, i.e. every $X_i$ is below $1-\epsilon$. By independence and the uniform CDF $\mathbf{P}(X_i<t)=\frac{t+1}{2}$ for $t\in[-1,1]$, $$\mathbf{P}\big(|V_n-1|>\epsilon\big)=\mathbf{P}(X_1<1-\epsilon)\cdots\mathbf{P}(X_n<1-\epsilon)=\left(\frac{(1-\epsilon)+1}{2}\right)^{\!n}=\Big(1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\Big)^{\!n}.$$ Since $0<1-\epsilon/2<1$, this geometric sequence tends to $0$, so $V_n\to 1$ in probability. At $\epsilon=0.1$ the values $0.95^n$ are $0.95$, $0.5987$, $0.07694$, $0.005921$ at $n=1,10,50,100$ (L_max_tail_n*), and $n\ge 90$ is needed to push the probability below $0.01$ (L_max_n_for_001). Contrast this with Example 1.7: minima of uniforms slide to the bottom of the range, maxima to the top, and both do so in probability at a geometric rate.

§1 cheatsheet.
  • Markov ($X\ge 0$, $a>0$): $\ \mathbf{P}(X\ge a)\le\dfrac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}$. Proof: $X\ge a\mathbf{1}_{\{X\ge a\}}$, take expectations. Vacuous when $a\le\mathbb{E}[X]$. Attained by the two-point variable $\{0,a\}$.
  • Chebyshev (any $X$ with mean $\mu$, variance $\sigma^2$, $c>0$): $\ \mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge c)\le\dfrac{\sigma^2}{c^2}$, equivalently $\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma)\le 1/k^2$. Proof: Markov on $(X-\mu)^2$ at level $c^2$. Vacuous for $k\le 1$. Attained by the three-point variable $\{-k,0,k\}$ with masses $\frac1{2k^2},1-\frac1{k^2},\frac1{2k^2}$.
  • Unknown variance: if $X\in[a,b]$ then $\sigma^2\le\dfrac{(b-a)^2}{4}$; for $X\in\{0,1\}$ this is $\operatorname{var}=p(1-p)\le 1/4$, attained at $p=1/2$.
  • Sample mean ($X_i$ i.i.d., mean $\mu$, variance $\sigma^2$): $\ \mathbb{E}[M_n]=\mu$ (linearity only), $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n$ (independence used once), $\sigma_{M_n}=\sigma/\sqrt n$.
  • WLLN: $\ \mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}\to 0$ for each fixed $\epsilon>0$; hence $M_n\to\mu$ in probability. Interprets empirical frequency as an estimate of probability.
  • Sample size: $\ \mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\delta$ is guaranteed by $n\ge\dfrac{\sigma^2}{\epsilon^2\delta}$. Bernoulli worst case: $n\ge\dfrac{1}{4\epsilon^2\delta}$. Pollster with $\epsilon=0.01,\delta=0.05$: $n=50{,}000$ (CLT would need $9{,}604$). Accuracy costs $1/\epsilon^2$; confidence costs only $1/\delta$.
  • Convergence in probability: $Y_n\to a$ iff $\mathbf{P}(|Y_n-a|\ge\epsilon)\to 0$ for every $\epsilon>0$. Does not require independence; does not imply $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]\to a$; does not imply mean-square convergence; says nothing about any single realized run.
  • Sufficient test: $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]\to\mu$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y_n)\to 0\ \Rightarrow\ Y_n\to\mu$ in probability. Mean square $\Rightarrow$ in probability (Markov on $(Y_n-c)^2$), never the converse.
  • The canonical counterexample: $\mathbf{P}(Y_n=0)=1-\frac1n$, $\mathbf{P}(Y_n=n)=\frac1n$. Then $Y_n\to 0$ in probability, $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]=1$, $\operatorname{var}(Y_n)=n-1$, $\mathbb{E}[Y_n^2]=n\to\infty$, and Chebyshev is useless.
  • Method choice: known distribution → exact; sample mean and want a number → CLT (§2); know $\mu,\sigma^2$ → Chebyshev; know only $\mathbb{E}[X]$ with $X\ge 0$ → Markov (Fig. 1.3).
