Q26. ★ · Building a PMF · G2 §1.2

A drawer holds five raffle tickets numbered $1,2,3,4,5$. You reach in and pull out two tickets at once, all $\binom{5}{2}$ two-ticket subsets being equally likely. Let $X$ be the larger of the two numbers you draw. (a) Find the PMF $p_X(x)$ and verify it sums to $1$. (b) Compute $\mathbb{E}[X]$. (c) Compute $\mathbf{P}(X \ge 4)$.

Solution

Setup. The sample space is the set of unordered pairs $\{i,j\}$ with $1\le i<j\le5$; there are $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ of them and each has probability $1/10$ by the discrete uniform law. $X$ is the function that maps a pair to its maximum, so $X$ takes values in $\{2,3,4,5\}$ (the value $1$ is impossible — $1$ can never be the larger of two distinct numbers).

Step 1 — count the outcomes in $\{X=x\}$. The event $\{X=x\}$ holds exactly when one drawn ticket is $x$ and the other is any number strictly below $x$. There are $x-1$ such partners, so $\{X=x\}$ contains $x-1$ of the ten equally likely pairs, and

$$p_X(x) = \frac{x-1}{10}, \qquad x = 2,3,4,5,$$

and $p_X(x)=0$ otherwise. Explicitly $p_X(2) = 1/10 = 0.1$, $p_X(3) = 2/10 = 0.2$, $p_X(4) = 3/10 = 0.3$, $p_X(5) = 4/10 = 0.4$.

Step 2 — normalization. $\;0.1+0.2+0.3+0.4 = \frac{1+2+3+4}{10} = \frac{10}{10} = 1$ ✓. (The four counts $1,2,3,4$ must add to $\binom52 = 10$, since every pair has exactly one maximum.)

Step 3 — the expectation, by the definition $\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_x x\,p_X(x)$.

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = 2(0.1) + 3(0.2) + 4(0.3) + 5(0.4) = 0.2 + 0.6 + 1.2 + 2.0 = 4.0 .$$

Step 4 — the tail probability. By additivity over the disjoint values, $\mathbf{P}(X\ge4) = p_X(4)+p_X(5) = 0.3+0.4 = 0.7$.

Answer. $\;p_X(x) = (x-1)/10$ for $x=2,3,4,5$; $\;\mathbb{E}[X] = 4$; $\;\mathbf{P}(X\ge4)=0.7$.

Trap. A careless reader averages the ticket numbers and answers $\mathbb{E}[X]=3$ (the mean of $1,\dots,5$). That is the mean of a single uniformly drawn ticket. Taking a maximum tilts mass upward, which is why the answer $4$ sits above $3$; the PMF is increasing in $x$, not flat.

Q27. ★ · Recognizing the named families · G2 §1.6

For each story, say which named discrete family (Bernoulli, binomial, geometric, discrete uniform, or none of them) describes the random variable, give its parameters, and give its mean.

(a) A tester draws components one at a time from a very large lot in which $4\%$ are defective, independently, and stops at the first defective one. $N$ = number tested.
(b) Twelve patients are treated independently, each cured with probability $0.7$. $C$ = number cured.
(c) A fair $20$-sided die is rolled once. $D$ = the face shown.
(d) A box holds $10$ fuses of which exactly $3$ are bad. Four fuses are drawn without replacement. $B$ = number of bad fuses drawn.

Solution

(a) Geometric with $p = 0.04$. The defining picture is a stream of independent identical trials, and the variable counts trials up to and including the first success ("success" here = defective). "Very large lot" is what makes the per-draw probability stay at $0.04$ and the draws independent. $\;\mathbb{E}[N] = 1/p = 1/0.04 = 25$, and $\operatorname{var}(N) = (1-p)/p^2 = 0.96/0.0016 = 600$.

(b) Binomial with $n=12$, $p=0.7$. A fixed number of independent trials, and the variable counts successes. $\;\mathbb{E}[C] = np = 12(0.7) = 8.4$, $\operatorname{var}(C) = np(1-p) = 12(0.7)(0.3) = 2.52$.

(c) Discrete uniform on $\{1,\dots,20\}$. Every value equally likely, $p_D(d) = 1/20$. $\;\mathbb{E}[D] = (a+b)/2 = (1+20)/2 = 10.5$ and $\operatorname{var}(D) = \frac{(b-a)(b-a+2)}{12} = \frac{19\cdot21}{12} = \frac{399}{12} = 33.25$.

(d) None of them. Four trials, two outcomes each — it looks binomial, but the draws are not independent: removing a bad fuse changes the composition of the box, so the success probability of the second draw depends on the first. The correct PMF is hypergeometric,

$$p_B(k) = \frac{\binom{3}{k}\binom{7}{4-k}}{\binom{10}{4}}, \qquad k = 0,1,2,3,$$

giving $p_B(0) = \frac{1\cdot35}{210} = 0.1667$, $p_B(1) = \frac{3\cdot35}{210} = 0.5$, $p_B(2) = \frac{3\cdot 21}{210} = \frac{63}{210} = 0.3$, $p_B(3) = \frac{1\cdot7}{210} = \frac{7}{210} = 0.0333$ (sum $=1$ ✓). The mean is nevertheless $\mathbb{E}[B] = 4\cdot\frac{3}{10} = 1.2$, exactly the binomial value: writing $B = I_1+I_2+I_3+I_4$ with $I_j$ the indicator that the $j$th draw is bad, each $\mathbb{E}[I_j] = 0.3$ by symmetry, and linearity of expectation never asks whether the $I_j$ are independent.

Answer. $\;$(a) geometric($0.04$), mean $25$; (b) binomial($12,0.7$), mean $8.4$; (c) uniform on $\{1,\dots,20\}$, mean $10.5$; (d) not a named family (hypergeometric), mean $1.2$.

Trap. Calling (d) binomial($4,0.3$) gives $\mathbf{P}(B=1) = \binom41(0.3)(0.7)^3 = 0.4116$, whereas the truth is $0.5$ — an error of $0.088$. The mean survives the mistake; the PMF does not.

Q28. ★ · Expected-value rule · G2 §2.2

A relief agency ships exactly $60$ tents to a region. The number of camps $X$ that will need to share them is not known in advance and is modeled as uniform on $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$. The tents are split evenly, so each camp receives $T = 60/X$ tents. Compute $\mathbb{E}[T]$, and compare it with $60/\mathbb{E}[X]$.

Solution

Setup. $X$ is uniform on five values, so $p_X(x) = 1/5$ for $x=1,\dots,5$. $T = g(X)$ with $g(x) = 60/x$. The expected-value rule says $\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)\,p_X(x)$ — you reweight the outputs of $g$ by the PMF of $X$; you never need the PMF of $T$.

Step 1 — apply the rule.

$$\mathbb{E}[T] = \sum_{x=1}^{5} \frac{60}{x}\cdot\frac15 = \frac{60}{5}\left(1 + \frac12 + \frac13 + \frac14 + \frac15\right) = 12\left(\frac{137}{60}\right) = \frac{137}{5} = 27.4 .$$

(The bracket is $1+0.5+0.3333+0.25+0.2 = 2.283333 = 137/60$; equivalently $\mathbb{E}[1/X] = 137/300 = 0.456667$ and $60\times0.456667 = 27.4$.)

Step 2 — the substitution answer, for contrast. $\mathbb{E}[X] = (1+2+3+4+5)/5 = 15/5 = 3$, so $60/\mathbb{E}[X] = 60/3 = 20$.

Step 3 — why they differ. $g(x)=60/x$ is convex on $x>0$, and averaging a convex function exceeds the function of the average. The gap here is $27.4 - 20 = 7.4$ tents per camp, a $37\%$ overshoot of the naive figure.

Answer. $\;\mathbb{E}[T] = 27.4$ tents per camp, while $60/\mathbb{E}[X] = 20$.

Trap. Answering $20$ is the classic $\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = g(\mathbb{E}[X])$ error. That identity is valid only for linear $g$; $60/x$ is not linear, so the two numbers are genuinely different quantities, and neither rounding nor a finer grid will reconcile them.

Q29. ★ · var$(aX+b)$ · G2 §3.2

A supplier's daily surplus $Y$ (in thousands of dollars) is related to the number of units sold $X$ by $Y = 7 - 2X$. You are told $\mathbb{E}[X] = 3$ and $\operatorname{var}(X) = 4$. Find $\mathbb{E}[Y]$, $\operatorname{var}(Y)$, and the standard deviation $\sigma_Y$.

Solution

Setup. $Y = aX + b$ with $a = -2$ and $b = 7$. Two identities apply: linearity $\mathbb{E}[aX+b] = a\mathbb{E}[X]+b$, and $\operatorname{var}(aX+b) = a^2\operatorname{var}(X)$.

Step 1 — the mean. $\mathbb{E}[Y] = -2\,\mathbb{E}[X] + 7 = -2(3)+7 = -6+7 = 1$ thousand dollars.

Step 2 — the variance. The additive constant $b=7$ shifts the whole distribution without changing any deviation from the mean, so it drops out; the multiplier scales every deviation by $a$, and variance is quadratic in deviations:

$$\operatorname{var}(Y) = a^2\operatorname{var}(X) = (-2)^2(4) = 4\cdot4 = 16 .$$

Step 3 — the standard deviation. $\sigma_X = \sqrt{4} = 2$ and $\sigma_Y = \sqrt{16} = 4 = |a|\,\sigma_X = 2\cdot 2$.

Answer. $\;\mathbb{E}[Y] = 1$, $\;\operatorname{var}(Y) = 16$, $\;\sigma_Y = 4$.

Trap. Two wrong answers are common: $\operatorname{var}(Y) = -2(4) = -8$ (carrying $a$ instead of $a^2$ — and a negative variance is impossible), and $\sigma_Y = -4$ (carrying the sign into a standard deviation). The scaling factor for a standard deviation is $|a|$, never $a$; spread has no sign.

Q30. ★ · Memorylessness · G2 §3.5

A modem redials a busy line; each attempt connects with probability $p = 0.2$, independently of the others. Let $X$ be the number of the attempt on which the first connection occurs, so $X$ is geometric with parameter $0.2$. You have watched four attempts fail, i.e. you know $X > 4$. Compute (a) $\mathbf{P}(X > 7 \mid X > 4)$, (b) $\mathbf{P}(X = 7\mid X>4)$, and (c) $\mathbb{E}[X \mid X > 4]$.

Solution

Setup. For a geometric variable with parameter $p$, $\mathbf{P}(X > k) = (1-p)^k$: the event $\{X > k\}$ says the first $k$ attempts all failed, and the attempts are independent. With $p = 0.2$ write $q = 1-p = 0.8$.

(a) Step 1 — both tail probabilities. $\mathbf{P}(X>4) = 0.8^4 = 0.4096$ and $\mathbf{P}(X>7) = 0.8^7 = 0.2097152$. Since $\{X>7\}\subset\{X>4\}$, the intersection is $\{X>7\}$ itself.

Step 2 — divide.

$$\mathbf{P}(X>7\mid X>4) = \frac{\mathbf{P}(X>7)}{\mathbf{P}(X>4)} = \frac{0.2097152}{0.4096} = 0.512 = 0.8^{3} = \mathbf{P}(X>3).$$

The four wasted attempts have vanished: conditioned on $X>4$, the number of further attempts is again geometric($0.2$).

(b) $\mathbf{P}(X=7\mid X>4) = \dfrac{p_X(7)}{\mathbf{P}(X>4)} = \dfrac{0.8^{6}(0.2)}{0.8^{4}} = 0.8^{2}(0.2) = 0.64(0.2) = 0.128 = p_X(3)$ — the same memorylessness, one value at a time.

(c) Because $X - 4$ given $\{X>4\}$ is geometric($0.2$) with mean $1/p = 5$,

$$\mathbb{E}[X\mid X>4] = 4 + \frac1p = 4 + 5 = 9 .$$

(Monte Carlo over $400{,}000$ geometric draws: conditional probability $0.5108$ and conditional mean $8.985$ — matching $0.512$ and $9$.)

Answer. $\;$(a) $0.512$, (b) $0.128$, (c) $\mathbb{E}[X\mid X>4] = 9$.

Trap. The tempting answer to (c) is $\mathbb{E}[X\mid X>4] = 1/p = 5$ — "it's memoryless, so nothing changed." What is memoryless is the remaining wait, $X-4$; the variable $X$ itself counts from the original start, so it must exceed $4$. Answering $5$ contradicts the conditioning event.

Q31. ★ · Marginals and conditionals · G2 §4.2

Two random variables $X\in\{1,2,3\}$ (number of items a customer buys) and $Y\in\{0,1,2\}$ (number of coupons used) have the joint PMF below; each table entry is the numerator over $20$, so e.g. $p_{X,Y}(2,1) = 3/20$.

$$p_{X,Y}(x,y) = \frac{1}{20}\times\begin{array}{c|ccc} x\backslash y & 0 & 1 & 2\\\hline 1 & 2 & 1 & 1\\ 2 & 1 & 3 & 4\\ 3 & 3 & 4 & 1 \end{array}$$

(a) Find both marginal PMFs. (b) Find $\mathbb{E}[X]$. (c) Find the conditional PMF $p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid 1)$ and $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=1]$. (d) Are $X$ and $Y$ independent?

Solution

Setup. The entries total $2+1+1+1+3+4+3+4+1 = 20$, so the table is a legitimate PMF. Marginals are obtained by summing the joint over the other variable: $p_X(x) = \sum_y p_{X,Y}(x,y)$.

(a) Row and column sums. Rows: $2+1+1 = 4$, $1+3+4 = 8$, $3+4+1 = 8$, so

$$p_X(1) = \tfrac{4}{20} = 0.2,\qquad p_X(2) = \tfrac{8}{20} = 0.4,\qquad p_X(3) = \tfrac{8}{20} = 0.4 .$$

Columns: $2+1+3 = 6$, $1+3+4 = 8$, $1+4+1 = 6$, so

$$p_Y(0) = \tfrac{6}{20} = 0.3,\qquad p_Y(1) = \tfrac{8}{20} = 0.4,\qquad p_Y(2) = \tfrac{6}{20} = 0.3 .$$

Both sets sum to $1$ ✓.

(b) $\mathbb{E}[X] = 1(0.2) + 2(0.4) + 3(0.4) = 0.2+0.8+1.2 = 2.2$.

(c) Condition on the column $Y=1$ by dividing that column by its total $p_Y(1) = 8/20$:

$$p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid 1) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x,1)}{p_Y(1)} = \frac{1/20}{8/20},\;\frac{3/20}{8/20},\;\frac{4/20}{8/20} = 0.125,\; 0.375,\; 0.5 \quad (x=1,2,3),$$

which sums to $1$ ✓ — conditioning renormalizes a slice into a PMF of its own. Then

$$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=1] = 1(0.125) + 2(0.375) + 3(0.5) = 0.125 + 0.75 + 1.5 = 2.375 .$$

(d) Not independent. Independence requires $p_{X,Y}(x,y) = p_X(x)p_Y(y)$ for every pair. Test $(1,1)$: $p_X(1)p_Y(1) = (0.2)(0.4) = 0.08$, but the table says $p_{X,Y}(1,1) = 1/20 = 0.05$. One violated cell settles it. (Consistently, $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=1] = 2.375 \neq 2.2 = \mathbb{E}[X]$: learning $Y$ changed the outlook for $X$.)

Answer. $\;p_X = (0.2,0.4,0.4)$, $p_Y = (0.3,0.4,0.3)$; $\mathbb{E}[X] = 2.2$; $p_{X\mid Y}(\cdot\mid1) = (0.125,0.375,0.5)$ with $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=1] = 2.375$; $X$ and $Y$ are dependent.

Trap. The common slip in (c) is to weight $x$ by the raw joint entries and skip the division by $p_Y(1)$: $1(\tfrac1{20}) + 2(\tfrac3{20}) + 3(\tfrac4{20}) = \tfrac{19}{20} = 0.95$. That is $\mathbb{E}[X\,\mathbf{1}\{Y=1\}]$, not a conditional expectation — and $0.95$ is below every value $X$ can take, which is the giveaway. A conditional expectation must be an average against a PMF that sums to $1$; here the slice sums only to $p_Y(1) = 0.4$, and indeed $0.95/0.4 = 2.375$ recovers the right answer.

Tool: G2 §4.2–4.3 (marginals, conditional PMFs) · B&T §2.5–2.6

Q32. ★ · Total expectation theorem · G2 §3.6

A print queue receives two kinds of job. With probability $0.4$ the job is a form, whose page count is uniform on $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$. With probability $0.6$ it is a report, whose page count is geometric with parameter $0.25$ (pages $1,2,3,\dots$). Let $X$ be the number of pages of the next job. Find $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\mathbf{P}(X = 2)$.

Solution

Setup. The events $A = \{\text{form}\}$ and $B = \{\text{report}\}$ partition the sample space with $\mathbf{P}(A) = 0.4$, $\mathbf{P}(B) = 0.6$. The total expectation theorem says $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbf{P}(A)\,\mathbb{E}[X\mid A] + \mathbf{P}(B)\,\mathbb{E}[X\mid B]$, and the matching statement for probabilities is the total probability theorem.

Step 1 — the two conditional means. Uniform on $\{1,\dots,5\}$: $\mathbb{E}[X\mid A] = (1+5)/2 = 3$. Geometric($0.25$): $\mathbb{E}[X\mid B] = 1/0.25 = 4$.

Step 2 — weight and add.

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = 0.4(3) + 0.6(4) = 1.2 + 2.4 = 3.6 \text{ pages}.$$

Step 3 — the same partition for $\mathbf{P}(X=2)$. Conditional values: $\mathbf{P}(X=2\mid A) = 1/5 = 0.2$, and $\mathbf{P}(X=2\mid B) = (1-0.25)^{1}(0.25) = (0.75)(0.25) = 0.1875$. Hence

$$\mathbf{P}(X=2) = 0.4(0.2) + 0.6(0.1875) = 0.08 + 0.1125 = 0.1925 .$$

Answer. $\;\mathbb{E}[X] = 3.6$ pages and $\mathbf{P}(X=2) = 0.1925$.

Trap. Averaging the two conditional means without their weights gives $(3+4)/2 = 3.5$. The mixture weights are $0.4$ and $0.6$, not $0.5$ and $0.5$; the correct $3.6$ leans toward the more likely report branch.

Tool: G2 §3.6 (total expectation theorem) · B&T §2.6

Q33. ★★ · The binomial PMF · G2 §1.4

A drug is given to $6$ patients who respond independently, each cured with probability $0.3$. Let $X$ be the number cured. (a) Compute $\mathbf{P}(X=2)$. (b) Without tabulating the whole PMF, use the ratio $p_X(k)/p_X(k-1)$ to find the most likely value of $X$. (c) Give $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)$.

Solution

Setup. $X$ is binomial with $n=6$, $p=0.3$: $p_X(k) = \binom6k (0.3)^k(0.7)^{6-k}$, $k=0,\dots,6$.

(a) $\binom62 = 15$, $(0.3)^2 = 0.09$, $(0.7)^4 = 0.2401$, so

$$p_X(2) = 15(0.09)(0.2401) = 15(0.0216090) = 0.324135 .$$

(b) Step 1 — form the ratio. Using $\binom{n}{k}/\binom{n}{k-1} = (n-k+1)/k$,

$$\frac{p_X(k)}{p_X(k-1)} = \frac{n-k+1}{k}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p} = \frac{7-k}{k}\cdot\frac{0.3}{0.7} = \frac{7-k}{k}\cdot 0.428571 .$$

Step 2 — find where it crosses $1$. At $k=2$: $\frac{5}{2}(0.428571) = 1.071429 > 1$, so $p_X(2) > p_X(1)$ — the PMF is still rising. At $k=3$: $\frac{4}{3}(0.428571) = 0.571429 < 1$, so $p_X(3) < p_X(2)$ — it has started falling. The ratio is decreasing in $k$, so the PMF rises to $k=2$ then falls: the mode is $k = 2$. Equivalently the mode is $\lfloor (n+1)p\rfloor = \lfloor 7(0.3)\rfloor = \lfloor 2.1\rfloor = 2$.

Step 3 — sanity check by direct evaluation. $p_X(1) = 6(0.3)(0.7)^5 = 0.302526$, $p_X(2) = 0.324135$, $p_X(3) = 20(0.027)(0.343) = 0.185220$. Indeed $k=2$ is the peak.

(c) $\mathbb{E}[X] = np = 6(0.3) = 1.8$ and $\operatorname{var}(X) = np(1-p) = 6(0.3)(0.7) = 1.26$.

Answer. $\;\mathbf{P}(X=2) = 0.324135$; the most likely value is $2$; $\mathbb{E}[X] = 1.8$, $\operatorname{var}(X) = 1.26$.

Trap. Do not conclude "the mode is the mean rounded." Here $\mathbb{E}[X] = 1.8$ rounds to $2$ and agrees by luck, but for $n=6$, $p=0.5$ the mean $3$ is the mode while for $n=5$, $p=0.5$ the mean $2.5$ is not an attainable value at all. The ratio test is the reliable route because it compares actual probabilities.

Tool: G2 §1.4 (binomial PMF) · B&T §2.2, §2.4

Q34. ★★ · Linearity via indicators · G2 §4.8

Five passengers board an elevator on the ground floor. Each independently selects one of the $8$ upper floors uniformly at random. Let $S$ be the number of floors at which the elevator stops. Find $\mathbb{E}[S]$, and explain why linearity of expectation is legitimate here even though the stop indicators for different floors are dependent.

Solution

Setup. Trying to build the PMF of $S$ directly is painful (it involves counting surjections). Instead decompose by floor: for $j = 1,\dots,8$ let

$$I_j = \begin{cases}1, & \text{the elevator stops at floor } j,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases} \qquad S = I_1 + I_2 + \cdots + I_8 .$$

Step 1 — the mean of one indicator. For a $0$–$1$ variable, $\mathbb{E}[I_j] = \mathbf{P}(I_j = 1)$. Floor $j$ is skipped exactly when all five passengers choose one of the other $7$ floors; each does so with probability $7/8$, independently, so

$$\mathbf{P}(I_j = 0) = \left(\frac78\right)^5 = \frac{16807}{32768} = 0.512909, \qquad \mathbb{E}[I_j] = 1 - 0.512909 = 0.487091 .$$

Step 2 — add up. By linearity,

$$\mathbb{E}[S] = \sum_{j=1}^{8}\mathbb{E}[I_j] = 8(0.487091) = 3.896729 \approx 3.90 \text{ stops}.$$

(Monte Carlo, $20{,}000$ trials: $3.9011$ ✓.)

Step 3 — why dependence does not matter. The $I_j$ are certainly dependent: if seven floors are known to be skipped, the eighth must be a stop. But $\mathbb{E}[I_1+\cdots+I_8] = \sum_j\mathbb{E}[I_j]$ follows from the expected-value rule applied to the joint PMF and requires no independence assumption whatsoever. Independence would only be needed if the question asked for $\operatorname{var}(S)$ or for $\mathbb{E}[I_iI_j]$ to factor.

Answer. $\;\mathbb{E}[S] = 8\big(1-(7/8)^5\big) = 3.8967$ stops.

Trap. Two wrong answers: $5$ ("one stop per passenger" — ignores collisions) and $8\cdot\frac58 = 5$ (a garbled proportionality). Both exceed the truth, because every pair of passengers sharing a floor removes a stop.

Tool: G2 §4.8 (indicator decomposition, linearity) · B&T §2.4

Q35. ★★ · $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]$ vs $g(\mathbb{E}[X])$ · G2 §2.2

A stall sells $X$ crates of fruit per hour with PMF $p_X(0) = 0.1$, $p_X(1) = 0.2$, $p_X(2) = 0.4$, $p_X(3) = 0.3$. Revenue net of spoilage is $g(X) = 3X - \tfrac12 X^2$ (in hundreds of dollars). (a) Compute $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]$. (b) Compute $g(\mathbb{E}[X])$. (c) Show that the difference equals $\tfrac12\operatorname{var}(X)$ and explain why.

Solution

Setup. The PMF sums to $0.1+0.2+0.4+0.3 = 1$ ✓. Because $g$ is a quadratic, the expected-value rule reduces to knowing the first two moments.

Step 1 — the moments.

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = 0(0.1)+1(0.2)+2(0.4)+3(0.3) = 0 + 0.2 + 0.8 + 0.9 = 1.9,$$

$$\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 0(0.1)+1(0.2)+4(0.4)+9(0.3) = 0 + 0.2 + 1.6 + 2.7 = 4.5 .$$

Step 2 — the variance, from $\operatorname{var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}[X])^2 = 4.5 - (1.9)^2 = 4.5 - 3.61 = 0.89$.

(a) By linearity applied after the expected-value rule,

$$\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = 3\,\mathbb{E}[X] - \tfrac12\,\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 3(1.9) - 0.5(4.5) = 5.7 - 2.25 = 3.45 .$$

(b) $g(\mathbb{E}[X]) = 3(1.9) - 0.5(1.9)^2 = 5.7 - 0.5(3.61) = 5.7 - 1.805 = 3.895 .$

(c) Subtract:

$$g(\mathbb{E}[X]) - \mathbb{E}[g(X)] = 3.895 - 3.45 = 0.445 = \tfrac12(0.89) = \tfrac12\operatorname{var}(X).$$

The reason is exact, not approximate: the only nonlinear term is $-\tfrac12X^2$, and $\mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2 = \operatorname{var}(X)$, so the two evaluations differ by precisely $\tfrac12\operatorname{var}(X)$. The curvature of $g$ is negative (concave), so the substituted value overstates the true mean revenue.

Answer. $\;\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = 3.45$, $\;g(\mathbb{E}[X]) = 3.895$, difference $0.445 = \tfrac12\operatorname{var}(X)$.

Trap. Reporting $3.895$ as "expected revenue" overstates it by $\$44.50$ per hour. Any time $g$ has curvature, plugging in the mean is a different computation from averaging the outputs — and here the size of the error is exactly a variance.

Q36. ★★ · Variance from a PMF · G2 §3.2

On a commute you hit $X$ red lights, with $p_X(0) = 0.2$, $p_X(1) = 0.5$, $p_X(2) = 0.2$, $p_X(3) = 0.1$. Each red light costs exactly $40$ seconds, and the rest of the trip has a fixed overhead of $5$ seconds of delay, so total delay is $T = 40X + 5$ seconds. Solution

Setup. The PMF sums to $0.2+0.5+0.2+0.1 = 1$ ✓. Use $\operatorname{var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}[X])^2$, which is almost always less work than $\mathbb{E}[(X-\mathbb{E}[X])^2]$ directly.

Step 1 — first moment.

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = 0(0.2) + 1(0.5) + 2(0.2) + 3(0.1) = 0 + 0.5 + 0.4 + 0.3 = 1.2 .$$

$$\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 0(0.2) + 1(0.5) + 4(0.2) + 9(0.1) = 0 + 0.5 + 0.8 + 0.9 = 2.2 .$$

$$\operatorname{var}(X) = 2.2 - (1.2)^2 = 2.2 - 1.44 = 0.76, \qquad \sigma_X = \sqrt{0.76} = 0.871780 .$$

$$\mathbb{E}[T] = 40(1.2) + 5 = 48 + 5 = 53 \text{ s},$$

$$\operatorname{var}(T) = 40^2(0.76) = 1600(0.76) = 1216 \text{ s}^2, \qquad \sigma_T = \sqrt{1216} = 34.871192 \text{ s} = 40\,\sigma_X .$$

Answer. $\;\operatorname{var}(X) = 0.76$, $\sigma_X = 0.8718$; $\mathbb{E}[T] = 53$ s, $\operatorname{var}(T) = 1216\,\text{s}^2$, $\sigma_T = 34.87$ s.

Q37. ★★ · Truncated geometric wait · G2 §3.6

A robot arm attempts to grip a part; each attempt succeeds with probability $p = 0.3$, independently. Policy allows at most $m = 4$ attempts: the arm stops at the first success, or after the fourth attempt, whichever comes first. Let $N$ be the number of attempts actually made, so $N = \min(X,4)$ where $X$ is geometric($0.3$). Derive a recursion for $a_m = \mathbb{E}[\min(X,m)]$ using the total expectation theorem, solve it for $m=4$, and compare with $\mathbb{E}[X]$.

Solution

Setup. Write $q = 1-p = 0.7$. Define $a_m = \mathbb{E}[\min(X,m)]$ for $m = 0,1,2,\dots$, with $a_0 = \mathbb{E}[\min(X,0)] = 0$ (no attempts are allowed).

Step 1 — condition on the first attempt. Let $A$ be the event that attempt $1$ succeeds, $\mathbf{P}(A) = p$, and $A^c$ its complement, $\mathbf{P}(A^c) = q$. Given $A$, exactly one attempt is made, so $\mathbb{E}[\min(X,m)\mid A] = 1$. Given $A^c$, one attempt has been spent and, by memorylessness, the process restarts with a budget of $m-1$ further attempts:

$$\mathbb{E}[\min(X,m)\mid A^c] = 1 + \mathbb{E}[\min(X,m-1)] = 1 + a_{m-1}.$$

Step 2 — total expectation theorem.

$$a_m = p(1) + q(1 + a_{m-1}) = (p+q) + q\,a_{m-1} = 1 + q\,a_{m-1}, \qquad a_0 = 0 .$$

Step 3 — iterate with $q = 0.7$.

$$a_1 = 1 + 0.7(0) = 1,\qquad a_2 = 1 + 0.7(1) = 1.7,$$

$$a_3 = 1 + 0.7(1.7) = 1 + 1.19 = 2.19,\qquad a_4 = 1 + 0.7(2.19) = 1 + 1.533 = 2.533 .$$

Step 4 — closed form and check. Unrolling the recursion gives a geometric sum:

$$a_m = 1 + q + q^2 + \cdots + q^{m-1} = \frac{1-q^m}{1-q} = \frac{1-q^m}{p}.$$

With $m=4$: $q^4 = (0.7)^4 = 0.2401$, so $a_4 = \dfrac{1-0.2401}{0.3} = \dfrac{0.7599}{0.3} = 2.533$ ✓. (Direct summation of $\sum_k \min(k,4)\,q^{k-1}p$ over $k\le 2000$ gives $2.5330$ ✓.)

Step 5 — compare. The untruncated mean is $\mathbb{E}[X] = 1/p = 3.3333$ attempts; the cap removes $3.3333 - 2.533 = 0.8003$ attempts of expected work. Note also that the policy fails to grip with probability $q^4 = 0.2401$.

Answer. $\;a_m = 1 + q\,a_{m-1}$ with $a_0 = 0$, i.e. $a_m = (1-q^m)/p$; for $m=4$, $\mathbb{E}[N] = 2.533$ attempts versus $\mathbb{E}[X] = 3.3333$ uncapped.

Trap. $\mathbb{E}[\min(X,4)] \ne \min(\mathbb{E}[X],4) = \min(3.3333,4) = 3.3333$. The minimum is a nonlinear function, so it cannot be pushed through an expectation; the truth $2.533$ is strictly smaller because truncation only ever shortens outcomes.

Q38. ★★ · Independence of random variables · G2 §4.5

Let $X\in\{0,1\}$ and $Y\in\{0,1,2\}$ have joint PMF given by the table (entries are numerators over $24$):

$$p_{X,Y}(x,y) = \frac{1}{24}\times\begin{array}{c|ccc} x\backslash y & 0 & 1 & 2\\\hline 0 & 2 & 4 & 6\\ 1 & 4 & 2 & 6 \end{array}$$

(a) Compute both marginals. (b) Decide whether $X$ and $Y$ are independent, checking every cell. (c) Compute $\mathbb{E}[XY]$ and $\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$ and comment.

Solution

(a) Marginals. Row sums: $2+4+6 = 12$ and $4+2+6 = 12$, so $p_X(0) = p_X(1) = 12/24 = 0.5$. Column sums: $2+4 = 6$, $4+2 = 6$, $6+6 = 12$, so

$$p_Y(0) = \tfrac{6}{24} = 0.25,\qquad p_Y(1) = \tfrac{6}{24} = 0.25,\qquad p_Y(2) = \tfrac{12}{24} = 0.5 .$$

(b) The independence test, cell by cell. Independence means $p_{X,Y}(x,y) = p_X(x)p_Y(y)$ for all six pairs. The products $p_X(x)p_Y(y)$ are $0.5(0.25) = 0.125$ in the $y=0$ and $y=1$ columns and $0.5(0.5) = 0.25$ in the $y=2$ column. Compare with the joint values:

$$\begin{array}{c|ccc} (x,y) & \text{joint} & p_X p_Y & \text{match?}\\\hline (0,0) & 2/24 = 0.08333 & 0.125 & \text{no}\\ (0,1) & 4/24 = 0.16667 & 0.125 & \text{no}\\ (0,2) & 6/24 = 0.25 & 0.25 & \text{yes}\\ (1,0) & 4/24 = 0.16667 & 0.125 & \text{no}\\ (1,1) & 2/24 = 0.08333 & 0.125 & \text{no}\\ (1,2) & 6/24 = 0.25 & 0.25 & \text{yes} \end{array}$$

Two cells agree, four do not. Independence is a statement about all pairs, so $X$ and $Y$ are dependent. Concretely, $p_{Y\mid X}(0\mid 0) = \frac{2/24}{12/24} = 1/6 = 0.1667$ while $p_{Y\mid X}(0\mid 1) = \frac{4/24}{12/24} = 1/3 = 0.3333$: knowing $X$ changes the odds on $Y$.

(c) $\mathbb{E}[X] = 0(0.5)+1(0.5) = 0.5$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y] = 0(0.25)+1(0.25)+2(0.5) = 0 + 0.25 + 1 = 1.25$, so $\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y] = 0.625$. Directly, only the $x=1$ row contributes to $XY$:

$$\mathbb{E}[XY] = 1\cdot1\cdot\tfrac{2}{24} + 1\cdot2\cdot\tfrac{6}{24} = \tfrac{2}{24} + \tfrac{12}{24} = \tfrac{14}{24} = 0.583333 .$$

Since $0.583333 \ne 0.625$, the product rule fails, which independently confirms dependence (the gap $-0.041667$ is the covariance, formalized in G3 §4).

Answer. $\;p_X = (0.5,0.5)$, $p_Y = (0.25,0.25,0.5)$; $X$ and $Y$ are not independent; $\mathbb{E}[XY] = 0.5833 \ne 0.625 = \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$.

Trap. Spot-checking a single cell such as $(0,2)$, where $0.25 = 0.25$, and declaring independence. Agreement on one cell — even on a whole column — proves nothing; the factorization must hold everywhere.

Q39. ★★ · Two-stage experiment · G2 §3.6

First a number $N$ is drawn uniformly from $\{1,2,3\}$; then a fair coin is tossed $N$ times. Let $X$ be the number of heads obtained. (a) Find the PMF of $X$. (b) Find $\mathbb{E}[X]$ two ways: from the PMF, and by the total expectation theorem. (c) Find $\operatorname{var}(X)$.

Solution

Setup. Conditioned on $N = n$, $X$ is binomial($n, 1/2$), so $p_{X\mid N}(k\mid n) = \binom{n}{k}/2^n$. Each value of $N$ has probability $1/3$.

(a) Total probability over the three branches. $p_X(k) = \sum_{n=1}^{3}\tfrac13\,p_{X\mid N}(k\mid n)$. Branch by branch, in units of $\tfrac13$:

$$n=1:\ \left(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right);\qquad n=2:\ \left(\tfrac14,\tfrac24,\tfrac14\right);\qquad n=3:\ \left(\tfrac18,\tfrac38,\tfrac38,\tfrac18\right).$$

Adding the columns with weight $1/3$ (common denominator $24$):

$$p_X(0) = \tfrac13\left(\tfrac12+\tfrac14+\tfrac18\right) = \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac78 = \tfrac{7}{24} = 0.291667,$$

$$p_X(1) = \tfrac13\left(\tfrac12+\tfrac12+\tfrac38\right) = \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac{11}{8} = \tfrac{11}{24} = 0.458333,$$

$$p_X(2) = \tfrac13\left(0+\tfrac14+\tfrac38\right) = \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac58 = \tfrac{5}{24} = 0.208333, \qquad p_X(3) = \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac18 = \tfrac{1}{24} = 0.041667 .$$

Check: $(7+11+5+1)/24 = 24/24 = 1$ ✓.

(b) The mean, from the PMF:

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = 0\cdot\tfrac{7}{24} + 1\cdot\tfrac{11}{24} + 2\cdot\tfrac{5}{24} + 3\cdot\tfrac{1}{24} = \frac{0 + 11 + 10 + 3}{24} = \frac{24}{24} = 1 .$$

By the total expectation theorem: $\mathbb{E}[X\mid N=n] = n/2$, so

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \tfrac13\left(\tfrac12\right) + \tfrac13\left(\tfrac22\right) + \tfrac13\left(\tfrac32\right) = \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac{6}{2} = 1 \ \checkmark$$

— the same answer with far less arithmetic, and it is just $\mathbb{E}[N]/2 = 2/2$.

(c) Variance. Second moment from the PMF:

$$\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 0 + 1\cdot\tfrac{11}{24} + 4\cdot\tfrac{5}{24} + 9\cdot\tfrac{1}{24} = \frac{11+20+9}{24} = \frac{40}{24} = \frac53 = 1.666667,$$

$$\operatorname{var}(X) = \frac53 - 1^2 = \frac23 = 0.666667 .$$

(Monte Carlo, $40{,}000$ two-stage trials: mean $0.9987$, variance $0.6637$ ✓.)

Answer. $\;p_X = \left(\tfrac{7}{24},\tfrac{11}{24},\tfrac{5}{24},\tfrac{1}{24}\right)$, $\;\mathbb{E}[X] = 1$, $\;\operatorname{var}(X) = 2/3 = 0.6667$.

Trap. Averaging the conditional variances gives $\mathbb{E}[N]\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = 2(0.25) = 0.5$, which is too small. That number accounts only for the coin-tossing noise within a branch and forgets the extra spread created by not knowing $N$. (The missing $0.1667$ is the variance of the conditional means $N/2$ — the law of total variance, developed in G4 §1.)

Q40. ★★ · Inverting a size-biased sample · G2 §2.5

One evening a campus shuttle service ran an unknown number of vans, each carrying an unknown number of riders. Every rider was surveyed and asked how many people rode their van; because the survey reached all of them, picking a survey form at random is exactly picking a rider uniformly at random. Let $X$ be the number of riders on the van of a uniformly chosen rider. The completed survey gives

$$p_X(8) = 0.1,\qquad p_X(12) = 0.15,\qquad p_X(20) = 0.25,\qquad p_X(40) = 0.5,$$

and no other values occur. Assume no two vans carried the same number of riders. Let $Y$ be the number of riders on the van of a uniformly chosen driver. (a) Recover the total number of riders $T$ and the number of vans $k$ from the survey alone, and hence $\mathbb{E}[Y]$. (Hint for $k$: compute $\mathbb{E}[1/X]$.) (b) Compute $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and say which of $\mathbb{E}[X]$, $\mathbb{E}[Y]$ a rider experiences. (c) Each driver's attention is split equally among their riders, so a rider on a van of $n$ gets a share $1/n$. Compute $\mathbb{E}[1/Y]$ and compare it with $\mathbb{E}[1/X]$.

Solution

Setup — what a rider-sampled PMF looks like. Write $n_1,\dots,n_k$ for the van loads and $T=\sum_i n_i$ for the total ridership. A uniformly chosen rider has probability $1/T$; the event $\{X = n\}$ collects the riders of the (unique) van of load $n$, and there are $n$ of them, so

$$p_X(n) = \frac{n}{T} .$$

Probability proportional to the value itself: this is the size-biased PMF. Everything in this problem is read out of that one formula.

(a) Step 1 — recover $T$. Invert $p_X(n) = n/T$ at any reported value: $T = n/p_X(n) = 8/0.1 = 80$. The other three must agree, and they do — $12/0.15 = 80$, $20/0.25 = 80$, $40/0.5 = 80$ — which is the check that the survey really is size-biased. (Equivalently the four probabilities sum to $(8+12+20+40)/80 = 1$ ✓.)

Step 2 — recover $k$. The number of vans is not visible in $T$, but it is visible in $\mathbb{E}[1/X]$, because the weight $n/T$ cancels the $1/n$ exactly:

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac1X\right] = \sum_n \frac1n\cdot\frac{n}{T} = \frac{k}{T} .$$

Numerically $\mathbb{E}[1/X] = \frac{0.1}{8}+\frac{0.15}{12}+\frac{0.25}{20}+\frac{0.5}{40} = 0.0125\times 4 = 0.05$ (each term is the same $1/T$, as the identity predicts), so $k = T\,\mathbb{E}[1/X] = 80(0.05) = 4$ vans. The loads are therefore $8,12,20,40$.

Step 3 — the driver-sampled mean. Four drivers, one per van, each with probability $1/4$:

$$\mathbb{E}[Y] = \frac{8+12+20+40}{4} = \frac{80}{4} = 20 = \frac{T}{k} = \frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[1/X]} \text{ riders}.$$

(b) The rider-sampled mean.

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_n n\cdot\frac{n}{T} = \frac{8^2+12^2+20^2+40^2}{80} = \frac{64+144+400+1600}{80} = \frac{2208}{80} = 27.6 \text{ riders}.$$

A rider experiences $\mathbb{E}[X] = 27.6$; the figure $20$ is what a driver sees. Cross-check with the variance-to-mean identity: $\mathbb{E}[Y^2] = 2208/4 = 552$, so $\operatorname{var}(Y) = 552 - 20^2 = 152$, and

$$\mathbb{E}[Y] + \frac{\operatorname{var}(Y)}{\mathbb{E}[Y]} = 20 + \frac{152}{20} = 20 + 7.6 = 27.6 = \mathbb{E}[X]\ \checkmark$$

a $38\%$ overshoot, and the excess is exactly the dispersion of the loads.

(c) The attention share. From Step 2, $\mathbb{E}[1/X] = 0.05 = 1/20$: the average rider gets one-twentieth of a driver, i.e. total drivers over total riders. Averaging over drivers instead,

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac1Y\right] = \frac14\left(\frac18+\frac1{12}+\frac1{20}+\frac1{40}\right) = \frac14(0.125+0.083333+0.05+0.025) = \frac{0.283333}{4} = 0.070833 .$$

Interpretation. The service can truthfully advertise "the average van gives each rider $0.0708$ of a driver's attention", while the average rider gets only $0.05$ — because half the riders sit in the $40$-seat van. Note which quantity is clean: it is $\mathbb{E}[1/X]$, the rider-averaged share, that equals the aggregate ratio $k/T$ exactly.

Answer. $\;$(a) $T = 80$ riders, $k = 4$ vans, $\mathbb{E}[Y] = 20$; (b) $\mathbb{E}[X] = 27.6$, which is the figure a rider experiences; (c) $\mathbb{E}[1/Y] = 0.0708$ versus $\mathbb{E}[1/X] = 0.05$.

Trap. Reading the identity backwards and reporting $1/\mathbb{E}[1/Y] = 1/0.070833 = 14.12$ as "the average van load". That is the harmonic mean of $8,12,20,40$, and it is smaller than every route to the truth: the plain mean is $20$ and the rider-experienced mean is $27.6$. Only $\mathbb{E}[1/X]$ — the rider-side average — inverts to a plain mean.

Tool: G2 §2.5 (size-biased vs uniform sampling), §2.2 (expected-value rule) · B&T §2.4

Q41. ★★ · Spot the error · G2 §4.5

Let $X$ be uniform on $\{-1,0,1\}$ and set $Y = |X|$. A student writes:

"$\mathbb{E}[X] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \frac13(-1)(1) + \frac13(0)(0) + \frac13(1)(1) = 0$, so $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$. Therefore $X$ and $Y$ are independent, and in particular $\mathbf{P}(X = 0, Y = 0) = \mathbf{P}(X=0)\mathbf{P}(Y=0) = \frac13\cdot\frac13 = \frac19$."

Identify the error, give the correct value of $\mathbf{P}(X=0,Y=0)$, and state precisely what the computation $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$ does and does not establish.

Solution

Step 1 — the arithmetic is right. $\mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{-1+0+1}{3} = 0$; $\;\mathbb{E}[Y] = \frac{1+0+1}{3} = \frac23 = 0.666667$; $\;\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}\big[X|X|\big] = \frac{(-1)(1) + 0 + (1)(1)}{3} = 0$. So indeed $\mathbb{E}[XY] = 0 = 0\cdot\frac23 = \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$.

Step 2 — the error is the implication. The factorization $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$ (equivalently $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y) = 0$, "uncorrelated") is a consequence of independence, not a criterion for it. Independence is the much stronger requirement $p_{X,Y}(x,y) = p_X(x)p_Y(y)$ for every pair $(x,y)$; a single scalar equation cannot certify a whole table.

Step 3 — the correct probability. Here $Y$ is a function of $X$, so knowing $X$ determines $Y$ completely. The event $\{X=0, Y=0\}$ is just $\{X=0\}$, because $X=0$ forces $Y = |0| = 0$:

$$\mathbf{P}(X=0,\,Y=0) = \mathbf{P}(X=0) = \frac13 = 0.333333 \;\neq\; \frac19 = 0.111111 .$$

The claimed value is off by a factor of $3$. Equivalently $\mathbf{P}(Y=0\mid X=0) = 1$ while $\mathbf{P}(Y=0) = \frac13$ — conditioning changes the distribution, the definition of dependence.

Step 4 — what the zero covariance does buy. It gives $\operatorname{var}(X+Y) = \operatorname{var}(X)+\operatorname{var}(Y)$, since the cross term $2\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)$ vanishes. Check: $\operatorname{var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - 0 = \frac23 = 0.666667$; $\operatorname{var}(Y) = \frac23 - \left(\frac23\right)^2 = \frac23-\frac49 = \frac29 = 0.222222$; sum $= \frac89 = 0.888889$. Directly, $X+Y$ takes values $0,0,2$ each with the respective probabilities $\frac13,\frac13,\frac13$, so $\mathbb{E}[X+Y] = \frac23$, $\mathbb{E}[(X+Y)^2] = \frac43$, and $\operatorname{var}(X+Y) = \frac43-\frac49 = \frac89 = 0.888889$ ✓.

Answer. $\;$The error is inferring independence from $\mathbb{E}[XY]=\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$. The correct value is $\mathbf{P}(X=0,Y=0) = 1/3$, not $1/9$. Uncorrelated does not imply independent; it only licenses variances to add.

Trap. The example is the canonical one: a symmetric $X$ and an even function of it are always uncorrelated and never independent (unless degenerate). Any argument of the form "the means multiplied, so the variables are independent" is unsalvageable.

Tool: G2 §4.5 (independence), §4.7 (variance of a sum) · B&T §2.7

Q42. ★★ · True or false, with reason · G2 §5.4

Decide true or false, and justify each in one or two lines. (a) If $\operatorname{var}(X) = 0$ then $X$ equals a constant with probability $1$. (b) $\operatorname{var}(2X-5) = 4\operatorname{var}(X) $ for every $X$ with finite variance. (c) For every positive random variable, $\mathbb{E}[1/X] = 1/\mathbb{E}[X]$. (d) If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then $\operatorname{var}(X-Y) = \operatorname{var}(X) - \operatorname{var}(Y)$.

Solution

(a) True. By definition $\operatorname{var}(X) = \sum_x (x-\mathbb{E}[X])^2 p_X(x)$, a sum of nonnegative terms. It is zero only if every term is zero, i.e. $p_X(x) = 0$ for every $x \ne \mathbb{E}[X]$. So all the mass sits at the single point $\mathbb{E}[X]$.

(b) True. $\operatorname{var}(aX+b) = a^2\operatorname{var}(X)$ with $a = 2$, $b = -5$: the shift $-5$ moves the mean by $-5$ as well, leaving every deviation unchanged, and the factor $2$ doubles each deviation, hence quadruples each squared deviation. So the multiplier is $a^2 = 4$.

(c) False. Counterexample: $X$ uniform on $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ gives $\mathbb{E}[X] = 3$, so $1/\mathbb{E}[X] = 0.333333$, whereas $\mathbb{E}[1/X] = \frac15\left(1+\frac12+\frac13+\frac14+\frac15\right) = \frac{137}{300} = 0.456667$. The reciprocal is a nonlinear (convex) function, so it cannot be moved through an expectation; in fact $\mathbb{E}[1/X] \ge 1/\mathbb{E}[X]$ always, with equality only for a constant $X$.

(d) False. Variances never subtract. Writing $X - Y = X + (-1)Y$ and using independence,

$$\operatorname{var}(X-Y) = \operatorname{var}(X) + (-1)^2\operatorname{var}(Y) = \operatorname{var}(X) + \operatorname{var}(Y).$$

Subtracting an independent variable adds noise; it does not cancel it. The claimed rule would also allow negative variances (take $\operatorname{var}(Y) > \operatorname{var}(X)$), which is impossible.

Answer. $\;$(a) True; (b) True; (c) False ($0.4567 \ne 0.3333$); (d) False — the variances add.

Trap. Statement (d) catches readers who remember "independence lets variances combine" but not that the combination is always addition, because the sign enters squared.

Q43. ★★ · Minimum of two geometrics · G2 §3.5

Two servers retry a task in synchronized rounds. In each round, server A succeeds with probability $0.25$ and server B with probability $0.40$, all attempts independent of each other and across rounds. Let $T$ be the number of the first round in which at least one server succeeds. Identify the distribution of $T$, then compute $\mathbb{E}[T]$, $\operatorname{var}(T)$ and $\mathbf{P}(T = 3)$.

Solution

Step 1 — the per-round success probability. A round fails (for the pair) only if both servers fail. By independence within the round,

$$\mathbf{P}(\text{round fails}) = (1-0.25)(1-0.40) = (0.75)(0.60) = 0.45,$$

so the round succeeds with probability $p_T = 1 - 0.45 = 0.55$.

Step 2 — identify the family. Rounds are independent and identical, and $T$ counts rounds up to and including the first success. Hence $T$ is geometric with parameter $0.55$. The tail confirms it: $\mathbf{P}(T > k) = 0.45^k$, since the first $k$ rounds must all fail.

Step 3 — mean and variance, from the geometric formulas $1/p$ and $(1-p)/p^2$:

$$\mathbb{E}[T] = \frac{1}{0.55} = 1.818182 \text{ rounds},\qquad \operatorname{var}(T) = \frac{0.45}{0.55^2} = \frac{0.45}{0.3025} = 1.487603 .$$

Step 4 — a specific value. $\mathbf{P}(T=3) = (0.45)^2(0.55) = (0.2025)(0.55) = 0.111375$.

(Monte Carlo, $300{,}000$ paired runs: $\mathbb{E}[T] \approx 1.8137$, $\operatorname{var}(T) \approx 1.4760$ ✓.)

Answer. $\;T\sim$ geometric($0.55$); $\mathbb{E}[T] = 1.8182$, $\operatorname{var}(T) = 1.4876$, $\mathbf{P}(T=3) = 0.111375$.

Trap. Two tempting wrong routes: (i) "the faster server dominates, so $\mathbb{E}[T] = \min(1/0.25,\,1/0.40) = 2.5$" — the minimum of the two waits is faster than either alone, not equal to the faster one; (ii) adding probabilities, $0.25+0.40 = 0.65$ — that double-counts rounds in which both succeed, which have probability $0.25(0.40) = 0.10$; note $0.65 - 0.10 = 0.55$ ✓, recovering the correct $p_T$.

Q44. ★★★ · State recursion · G2 §3.8

A fair coin is tossed repeatedly. Let $L$ be the number of tosses needed until the pattern HT (a head immediately followed by a tail) first appears. Set up and solve a system of conditional-expectation equations for $\mathbb{E}[L]$. Then compare with the known answer $6$ for the pattern HH, and explain in words why the two differ even though both patterns have probability $1/4$ in any fixed pair of tosses.

Solution

Setup — define states by "useful progress so far". What matters about the past is only whether the previous toss was an H waiting for its T. Define

$$\mu_0 = \mathbb{E}[\text{further tosses needed}\mid \text{no pending H}],\qquad \mu_1 = \mathbb{E}[\text{further tosses needed}\mid \text{last toss was H}].$$

We want $\mathbb{E}[L] = \mu_0$, the value at the start.

Step 1 — the equation at state 1. From state $1$ we toss once (cost $1$). With probability $\tfrac12$ we get T and the pattern HT is complete; with probability $\tfrac12$ we get H, and the last toss is again a pending H, i.e. we return to state $1$. By the total expectation theorem,

$$\mu_1 = 1 + \tfrac12(0) + \tfrac12\,\mu_1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \tfrac12\mu_1 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mu_1 = 2 .$$

Step 2 — the equation at state 0. Toss once (cost $1$): with probability $\tfrac12$ an H moves us to state $1$; with probability $\tfrac12$ a T leaves us in state $0$ (a T is useless as the first symbol of HT). So

$$\mu_0 = 1 + \tfrac12\mu_1 + \tfrac12\mu_0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \tfrac12\mu_0 = 1 + \tfrac12(2) = 2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mu_0 = 4 .$$

Step 3 — the HH comparison. For HH the same method gives $\nu_1 = 1 + \tfrac12(0) + \tfrac12\nu_0$ and $\nu_0 = 1 + \tfrac12\nu_1 + \tfrac12\nu_0$, whose solution is $\nu_0 = 6$ and $\nu_1 = 4$. So HT takes $4$ tosses on average and HH takes $6$; the gap is $2$.

(Monte Carlo, $60{,}000$ runs each: HT $\to 4.0044$, HH $\to 5.9913$ ✓.)

Step 4 — why they differ. The two patterns have the same probability $1/4$ of occupying any given pair of positions, but waiting times depend on how a failed attempt sets you back. For HT, once you have seen an H you can never lose that progress: every subsequent H keeps you in state $1$, so you sit there until the first T. For HH, a mismatch (an H followed by a T) destroys the progress entirely and sends you back to state $0$. Self-overlapping patterns are "clumpy": HH occurrences cluster (HHH contains two), and to keep the long-run rate at $1/4$ the gaps between clusters must be longer.

Answer. $\;\mathbb{E}[L] = 4$ tosses for HT, versus $6$ for HH.

Trap. The tempting answer is "both are $4$" (or "both are $1/(1/4) = 4$"), reasoning that a pattern of probability $1/4$ should take $4$ trials as with a geometric variable. Successive overlapping pairs are not independent trials, so the geometric formula does not apply — it happens to give the right number for HT and the wrong one for HH.

Q45. ★★★ · Variance of a sum of dependent indicators · G2 §4.10

A fair coin is tossed $5$ times. Let $X$ be the number of positions $i \in\{1,2,3,4\}$ such that tosses $i$ and $i+1$ are both heads (so $X$ counts adjacent HH pairs, and overlapping pairs are counted separately: HHH contributes $2$). Compute $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)$ using indicators, showing the cross terms explicitly.

Solution

Setup. For $i=1,\dots,4$ let $I_i$ indicate that tosses $i$ and $i+1$ are both heads, so $X = I_1+I_2+I_3+I_4$. Each $I_i$ is Bernoulli with $\mathbb{E}[I_i] = \mathbf{P}(\text{two specified tosses are H}) = \tfrac14$.

Step 1 — the mean. By linearity (no independence needed),

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = 4\left(\tfrac14\right) = 1 .$$

Step 2 — expand the second moment. Since $I_i^2 = I_i$ for a $0$–$1$ variable,

$$\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_i I_i^2 + \sum_{i\ne j} I_iI_j\Big] = \sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[I_i] + \sum_{i\ne j}\mathbb{E}[I_iI_j] = 1 + \sum_{i\ne j}\mathbb{E}[I_iI_j],$$

where the sum runs over all $4\cdot3 = 12$ ordered pairs $i \ne j$.

Step 3 — the two kinds of cross term. $\mathbb{E}[I_iI_j] = \mathbf{P}(I_i = I_j = 1)$.

Overlapping pairs ($|i-j| = 1$): $I_iI_{i+1} = 1$ requires three consecutive heads, so $\mathbb{E}[I_iI_{i+1}] = (1/2)^3 = 1/8 = 0.125$. The ordered pairs of this type are $(1,2),(2,3),(3,4)$ and their reverses: $6$ of them.

Disjoint pairs ($|i-j| \ge 2$): the two indicators involve four distinct tosses, and distinct tosses are independent, so $\mathbb{E}[I_iI_j] = \tfrac14\cdot\tfrac14 = 1/16 = 0.0625$. The ordered pairs of this type are $(1,3),(2,4),(1,4)$ and their reverses: $6$ of them.

Step 4 — add.

$$\sum_{i\ne j}\mathbb{E}[I_iI_j] = 6\left(\tfrac18\right) + 6\left(\tfrac1{16}\right) = 0.75 + 0.375 = 1.125,$$

$$\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 1 + 1.125 = 2.125,\qquad \operatorname{var}(X) = 2.125 - 1^2 = 1.125 = \tfrac98 .$$

Step 5 — verification by full enumeration. Over all $2^5 = 32$ equally likely sequences the PMF is $p_X(0) = 13/32 = 0.40625$, $p_X(1) = 10/32 = 0.3125$, $p_X(2) = 6/32 = 0.1875$, $p_X(3) = 2/32 = 0.0625$, $p_X(4) = 1/32 = 0.03125$ (summing to $1$), giving mean $1$ and variance $1.125$ ✓. Monte Carlo over $300{,}000$ runs: $0.9959$ and $1.1215$ ✓.

Answer. $\;\mathbb{E}[X] = 1$ and $\operatorname{var}(X) = 9/8 = 1.125$.

Trap. Treating $X$ as binomial($4,1/4$) gives $\operatorname{var}(X) = 4(0.25)(0.75) = 0.75$ — too small by $0.375$. The mean survives that mistake (linearity never needed independence), but the variance does not: overlapping indicators are positively correlated, since a run of heads triggers several at once, and positive correlation inflates the spread.

Q46. ★★★ · A sum of geometrics · G2 §3.7

A cereal box contains one of three equally likely toy types, independently across boxes. Let $T$ be the number of boxes you must buy to collect all three types. Compute $\mathbb{E}[T]$ and $\operatorname{var}(T)$ by decomposing $T$ into independent geometric stages.

Solution

Setup — the stage decomposition. Split the purchase sequence at the moments when your collection grows. Let $T_1$ be the number of boxes needed to get your first distinct type, $T_2$ the number of further boxes needed to get a second distinct type, and $T_3$ the number of further boxes to get the third. Then $T = T_1+T_2+T_3$.

Step 1 — each stage is geometric. During stage $i$ you already hold $i-1$ distinct types, so each new box is "useful" with probability $p_i = \frac{3-(i-1)}{3}$, independently of everything before it (each box is an independent draw). Hence $T_i$ is geometric($p_i$) with

$$p_1 = \frac33 = 1,\qquad p_2 = \frac23 = 0.666667,\qquad p_3 = \frac13 = 0.333333 .$$

Moreover the $T_i$ are independent, because the stages depend on disjoint sets of boxes.

Step 2 — the mean, by linearity and $\mathbb{E}[\text{geom}(p)] = 1/p$.

$$\mathbb{E}[T] = \frac1{p_1} + \frac1{p_2} + \frac1{p_3} = 1 + 1.5 + 3 = 3\left(1 + \frac12 + \frac13\right) = 3\left(\frac{11}{6}\right) = \frac{11}{2} = 5.5 \text{ boxes}.$$

Step 3 — the variance. Because the stages are independent, variances add, and $\operatorname{var}(\text{geom}(p)) = (1-p)/p^2$:

$$\operatorname{var}(T_1) = \frac{0}{1} = 0,\qquad \operatorname{var}(T_2) = \frac{1/3}{(2/3)^2} = \frac{1/3}{4/9} = 0.75,\qquad \operatorname{var}(T_3) = \frac{2/3}{(1/3)^2} = \frac{2/3}{1/9} = 6 ,$$

$$\operatorname{var}(T) = 0 + 0.75 + 6 = 6.75,\qquad \sigma_T = \sqrt{6.75} = 2.598076 .$$

(The first stage has zero variance because the very first box is always useful: $T_1 \equiv 1$. Monte Carlo, $50{,}000$ runs: $\mathbb{E}[T]\approx 5.518$ ✓.)

Interpretation. Almost all of the waiting — and nearly all of the uncertainty — sits in the last stage, where only one of three types is still useful. This is the general shape of the coupon-collector problem: the mean is $n\sum_{k=1}^{n}1/k$ and the final coupon dominates.

Answer. $\;\mathbb{E}[T] = 5.5$ boxes, $\operatorname{var}(T) = 6.75$, $\sigma_T = 2.598$.

Trap. Answering $3$ ("three types, three boxes") ignores repeats entirely. Note also that the variances may be added only because the stages are independent; the linearity step for the mean would have been valid regardless.

Q47. ★★★ · The tail-sum formula · G2 §2.2

(a) Let $X$ be a random variable taking values in $\{0,1,2,\dots,n\}$. Prove that

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \mathbf{P}(X \ge k).$$

(b) Use it to compute $\mathbb{E}[M]$ where $M$ is the maximum of two independent rolls of a fair six-sided die, and verify the result against the direct definition.

Solution

(a) Step 1 — write each value as a count of thresholds. For an integer $x \ge 0$,

$$x = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \mathbf{1}\{x \ge k\},$$

because exactly the $x$ thresholds $k = 1,2,\dots,x$ are met and the rest are not.

Step 2 — take expectations and exchange the two finite sums.

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{x=0}^{n} x\,p_X(x) = \sum_{x=0}^{n} \left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{x\ge k\}\right)p_X(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{x=0}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{x\ge k\}\,p_X(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}(X\ge k).$$

The exchange is legitimate because both sums are finite. The inner sum $\sum_x \mathbf{1}\{x\ge k\}p_X(x)$ is precisely $\mathbf{P}(X\ge k)$, by additivity over the values $x \ge k$. $\blacksquare$

(b) Step 1 — the tail probabilities of the maximum. $M \ge k$ fails only if both dice show at most $k-1$; by independence,

$$\mathbf{P}(M \le k-1) = \left(\frac{k-1}{6}\right)^2,\qquad \mathbf{P}(M \ge k) = 1 - \frac{(k-1)^2}{36}.$$

Numerically: $\mathbf{P}(M\ge1) = 1$, $\mathbf{P}(M\ge2) = 1-\tfrac1{36} = 0.972222$, $\mathbf{P}(M\ge3) = 1-\tfrac4{36} = 0.888889$, $\mathbf{P}(M\ge4) = 1-\tfrac9{36} = 0.75$, $\mathbf{P}(M\ge5) = 1-\tfrac{16}{36} = 0.555556$, $\mathbf{P}(M\ge6) = 1-\tfrac{25}{36} = 0.305556$.

Step 2 — add them.

$$\mathbb{E}[M] = \sum_{k=1}^{6}\left(1 - \frac{(k-1)^2}{36}\right) = 6 - \frac{0+1+4+9+16+25}{36} = 6 - \frac{55}{36} = 6 - 1.527778 = 4.472222 = \frac{161}{36}.$$

Step 3 — verification by the definition. The PMF of the maximum is $p_M(m) = \mathbf{P}(M\le m) - \mathbf{P}(M\le m-1) = \frac{m^2-(m-1)^2}{36} = \frac{2m-1}{36}$, i.e. $\tfrac1{36},\tfrac3{36},\tfrac5{36},\tfrac7{36},\tfrac9{36},\tfrac{11}{36}$ for $m=1,\dots,6$ (summing to $36/36 = 1$ ✓). Then

$$\mathbb{E}[M] = \frac{1(1) + 2(3) + 3(5) + 4(7) + 5(9) + 6(11)}{36} = \frac{1+6+15+28+45+66}{36} = \frac{161}{36} = 4.472222 \ \checkmark$$

(Monte Carlo, $400{,}000$ paired rolls: $4.4776$ ✓.)

Answer. $\;\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{k\ge1}\mathbf{P}(X\ge k)$, and for the maximum of two fair dice $\mathbb{E}[M] = 161/36 = 4.4722$.

Trap. Answering $3.5$ — the mean of a single die — or "$3.5$ for each die so $3.5$ for the max". Taking a maximum is nonlinear and strictly raises the mean whenever the two rolls can differ; the excess here is $4.4722 - 3.5 = 0.9722$. A second common slip is summing $\mathbf{P}(X > k)$ from $k=1$, which drops the $k=1$ term and undercounts by $\mathbf{P}(X\ge1)$.

Tool: G2 §2.2 (expected-value rule), §1.2 (PMF of a derived variable) · B&T §2.4
