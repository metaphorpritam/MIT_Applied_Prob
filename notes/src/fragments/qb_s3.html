Q48. ★ · Normalizing a PDF · G3 §1.1–1.2

A machine's daily downtime $X$, in hours, has the "tent" density $$f_X(x) = \begin{cases} cx, & 0 \le x \le 1,\\ c(2-x), & 1 \lt x \le 2,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$ (a) Find $c$.   (b) Find $\mathbf{P}(X \ge 1.5)$.   (c) Find $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)$.

Solution

Setup. A PDF must be nonnegative and integrate to $1$ over the whole line (G3 §1.1). The density is piecewise, so every integral splits at $x=1$.

(a) Normalization. Integrate each piece: $$\int_0^1 cx\,dx = c\Big[\frac{x^2}{2}\Big]_0^1 = \frac{c}{2}, \qquad \int_1^2 c(2-x)\,dx = c\Big[2x - \frac{x^2}{2}\Big]_1^2 = c\big[(4-2)-(2-0.5)\big] = \frac{c}{2}.$$ Summing, $\frac{c}{2}+\frac{c}{2} = c = 1$, so $c = 1$. (Geometric shortcut: the graph is a triangle of base $2$ and height $c$, area $\frac12(2)(c)=c$.)

(b) A tail probability. Only the second piece is involved: $$\mathbf{P}(X \ge 1.5) = \int_{1.5}^{2}(2-x)\,dx = \Big[2x-\frac{x^2}{2}\Big]_{1.5}^{2} = (4-2) - (3 - 1.125) = 2 - 1.875 = 0.125 .$$

(c) Mean and variance. The density is symmetric about $x=1$ (reflecting $x \mapsto 2-x$ swaps the two pieces), so $\mathbb{E}[X]=1$. Longhand, as a check: $$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[X] &= \int_0^1 x\cdot x\,dx + \int_1^2 x(2-x)\,dx = \frac13 + \Big[x^2-\frac{x^3}{3}\Big]_1^2\\ &= 0.333333 + \Big(\frac43 - \frac23\Big) = 0.333333+0.666667 = 1 . \end{aligned} $$ For the second moment, $$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[X^2] &= \int_0^1 x^3dx + \int_1^2 x^2(2-x)\,dx = 0.25 + \Big[\frac{2x^3}{3}-\frac{x^4}{4}\Big]_1^2\\ &= 0.25 + (5.333333-4) - (0.666667-0.25) = 1.166667, \end{aligned} $$ i.e. $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \frac{7}{6}$. Hence $$\operatorname{var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2 = 1.166667 - 1 = 0.166667 = \tfrac16 .$$ A $4\times10^6$-sample simulation (the tent is the sum of two independent $U(0,1)$'s) gives $\mathbf{P}(X\ge1.5)\approx0.124830$ and $\operatorname{var}\approx0.166715$ — both confirmed.

Answer. $\;c=1$, $\;\mathbf{P}(X\ge1.5)=0.125$, $\;\mathbb{E}[X]=1$, $\;\operatorname{var}(X)=1/6\approx0.166667$.

Trap. Answering $\mathbf{P}(X\ge1.5) = 0.25$: the right half $[1,2]$ carries mass $0.5$, and $1.5$ is its midpoint, so "half of a half" feels right. But the density is falling across that half, so the piece beyond the midpoint is the smaller one — and by similar triangles it is a quarter, not a half, of the right-hand mass: $0.125$. Splitting a non-uniform density at a midpoint never splits its probability in two.

Q49. ★ · CDF to PDF · G3 §1.4

A continuous random variable $X$ has CDF $$F_X(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x \lt 0,\\ x^2/4, & 0 \le x \le 2,\\ 1, & x \gt 2.\end{cases}$$ (a) Find the PDF $f_X$.   (b) Compute $\mathbf{P}(1 \le X \le 1.5)$.   (c) Find the median of $X$ — the value $m$ with $F_X(m)=1/2$ — and say whether it is above or below $\mathbb{E}[X] = 4/3$.

Solution

Setup. For a continuous random variable, $f_X(x) = \dfrac{dF_X}{dx}(x)$ wherever the derivative exists (G3 §1.4), and $\mathbf{P}(a \le X \le b) = F_X(b)-F_X(a)$ — endpoints do not matter because single points carry zero probability.

(a) Differentiate. On $0 \lt x \lt 2$, $\;f_X(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\frac{x^2}{4} = \frac{2x}{4} = \frac{x}{2}$, and $f_X(x)=0$ outside $[0,2]$. Sanity check: $\int_0^2 \frac{x}{2}dx = \big[\frac{x^2}{4}\big]_0^2 = 1$ ✓, and $f_X \ge 0$ on the support ✓. Sample value: $f_X(1.5) = 0.75$.

(b) A probability from the CDF. No integration needed — that is the point of having $F_X$: $$\mathbf{P}(1 \le X \le 1.5) = F_X(1.5) - F_X(1) = \frac{1.5^2}{4} - \frac{1^2}{4} = \frac{2.25}{4} - \frac{1}{4} = 0.5625 - 0.25 = 0.3125 .$$

(c) Median. Solve $F_X(m) = \dfrac{m^2}{4} = \dfrac12 \Rightarrow m^2 = 2 \Rightarrow m = \sqrt2 = 1.414214$ (take the root inside $[0,2]$). Since $\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_0^2 x\cdot\frac{x}{2}dx = \big[\frac{x^3}{6}\big]_0^2 = \frac{8}{6} = 1.333333$, the median $1.414214$ lies above the mean. That is the signature of a left-skewed density: $f_X(x)=x/2$ increases, piling mass near $x=2$, and the long thin tail runs to the left, dragging the mean down.

Answer. $\;f_X(x)=x/2$ on $[0,2]$; $\;\mathbf{P}(1\le X\le1.5)=0.3125$; $\;m=\sqrt2\approx1.414214 \gt \mathbb{E}[X]=1.333333$.

Traps. (i) Reporting the median as $4/3 = 1.333333$ — that is the mean, which was handed to you in the question. Mean and median agree only for a symmetric density; here they differ by $0.080880$, and part (c) is asking precisely which is larger. (ii) Using $F_X$ itself as the density, i.e. answering $f_X(x) = x^2/4$. It fails the first check every candidate density must pass: $\int_0^2 x^2/4\,dx = 2/3 \ne 1$. A CDF climbs to $1$; a PDF encloses area $1$.

Q50. ★ · Memorylessness · G3 §1.5

A network router's time to failure $T$ is exponential with mean $30$ months. Your router has been running for $18$ months without failing. What is the probability it lasts at least $12$ months more? Answer in two ways: from the definition of conditional probability, and in one line from memorylessness.

Solution

Setup. $T \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$ with $\mathbb{E}[T]=1/\lambda = 30$, so $\lambda = 1/30 = 0.033333$ per month. The exponential tail is $\mathbf{P}(T \gt t) = e^{-\lambda t}$ for $t \ge 0$ (G3 §1.5). The event asked for is $\{T \gt 30\}$ conditioned on $\{T \gt 18\}$.

Method 1 — conditional probability. Since $\{T \gt 30\} \subset \{T \gt 18\}$, the intersection is just $\{T \gt 30\}$: $$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{P}(T \gt 30 \mid T \gt 18) &= \frac{\mathbf{P}(T \gt 30)}{\mathbf{P}(T \gt 18)} = \frac{e^{-30/30}}{e^{-18/30}}\\ &= \frac{e^{-1}}{e^{-0.6}} = \frac{0.367879}{0.548812} = 0.670320 . \end{aligned} $$

Method 2 — memorylessness. The exponential's defining property is $\mathbf{P}(T \gt t+s \mid T \gt t) = \mathbf{P}(T \gt s)$: a used router that still works is stochastically brand new. So the answer is simply $$\mathbf{P}(T \gt 12) = e^{-12/30} = e^{-0.4} = 0.670320 ,$$ the same number, with no division. Simulation of $4\times10^6$ exponentials gives $0.669630$ ✓.

Answer. $\;e^{-0.4} = 0.670320$.

Trap. The tempting wrong answer is $e^{-30/30}=0.367879$ — reading "lasts to month 30" off the unconditional tail and forgetting that you have already been told it survived to month 18. Memorylessness resets the clock; it does not delete the question.

Q51. ★ · Standardization and the $\Phi$ table · G3 §1.7

Exam scores in a large class are modeled as $X \sim N(70, 25)$ (mean $70$, variance $25$). Using the standard normal table values $\Phi(1.60)=0.9452$ and $\Phi(1.40)=0.9192$, find $\mathbf{P}(63 \le X \le 78)$.

Solution

Setup. $\operatorname{var}(X)=25$ means $\sigma = \sqrt{25} = 5$, not $25$. Standardize: $Z = (X-\mu)/\sigma \sim N(0,1)$, and $\mathbf{P}(a \le X \le b) = \Phi\!\big(\frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\big) - \Phi\!\big(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\big)$ (G3 §1.7).

Step 1 — the two $z$-scores. $$z_{\text{lo}} = \frac{63-70}{5} = \frac{-7}{5} = -1.40, \qquad z_{\text{hi}} = \frac{78-70}{5} = \frac{8}{5} = 1.60 .$$

Step 2 — the negative argument. Tables list $\Phi$ only for $z \ge 0$; use the symmetry $\Phi(-z) = 1-\Phi(z)$: $$\Phi(-1.40) = 1 - \Phi(1.40) = 1 - 0.9192 = 0.0808 .$$

Step 3 — subtract. $$\mathbf{P}(63 \le X \le 78) = \Phi(1.60) - \Phi(-1.40) = 0.9452 - 0.0808 = 0.8644 .$$ Exact value from the error function: $0.864444$ — the table rounding costs about $4\times10^{-5}$.

Answer. $\;\mathbf{P}(63 \le X \le 78) = 0.8644$.

Trap. Using $\sigma = 25$ gives $z_{\text{lo}}=-0.28$, $z_{\text{hi}}=0.32$ and an answer near $0.236$ — the single most common normal-distribution error. $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ names the variance in the second slot.

Q52. ★ · Joint PDF and marginals · G3 §2.1–2.2

Two sensor readings $(X,Y)$ have joint PDF $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = c\,(x+2y)$ for $0 \le x \le 1$, $0 \le y \le 1$, and $0$ elsewhere. (a) Find $c$.   (b) Find the marginal PDF $f_X$.   (c) Find $\mathbb{E}[X]$.

Solution

Setup. A joint PDF integrates to $1$ over the plane; the marginal is obtained by integrating out the other variable, $f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dy$ (G3 §2.1–2.2).

(a) Normalize. Integrate over the unit square, inner integral in $y$ first: $$\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 (x+2y)\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1 \Big[xy + y^2\Big]_{y=0}^{y=1} dx = \int_0^1 (x+1)\,dx = \Big[\frac{x^2}{2}+x\Big]_0^1 = 0.5 + 1 = 1.5 .$$ So $c \cdot 1.5 = 1$ and $c = 1/1.5 = 2/3 = 0.666667$.

(b) Marginal of $X$. Reusing the inner integral just computed, $$f_X(x) = \int_0^1 \frac23 (x+2y)\,dy = \frac23 (x+1), \qquad 0 \le x \le 1,$$ and $0$ elsewhere. Check: $\int_0^1 \frac23(x+1)dx = \frac23 (1.5) = 1$ ✓. Endpoint values: $f_X(0)=0.666667$, $f_X(1)=1.333333$ — the density rises linearly, so larger $x$ is twice as likely per unit length at the top of the range as at the bottom.

(c) Mean of $X$. Use the marginal — never the joint, when only $X$ is involved: $$\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_0^1 x\cdot\frac23(x+1)\,dx = \frac23\int_0^1 (x^2+x)\,dx = \frac23\Big(\frac13+\frac12\Big) = \frac23\cdot\frac56 = \frac{5}{9} = 0.555556 .$$ It exceeds $0.5$, as the upward-sloping marginal demands.

Answer. $\;c = 2/3$, $\;f_X(x) = \tfrac23(x+1)$ on $[0,1]$, $\;\mathbb{E}[X] = 5/9 \approx 0.555556$.

Trap. Answering $\mathbb{E}[X] = 0.5$ "because $X$ lives on the unit square". The region is symmetric in $x$ and $y$; the density is not, since $y$ carries the weight $2$ and $x$ only $1$. That asymmetry is exactly what separates $\mathbb{E}[X]=5/9=0.555556$ from $\mathbb{E}[Y]=11/18=0.611111$ — a gap of $1/18$. Read the tilt off the marginal before you trust a symmetry argument.

Q53. ★ · Covariance algebra · G3 §4.8

Two portfolio returns satisfy $\operatorname{var}(X)=4$, $\operatorname{var}(Y)=9$, $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=-3$. (a) Find the correlation coefficient $\rho(X,Y)$.   (b) Find $\operatorname{var}(X+Y)$ and $\operatorname{var}(2X-Y)$.   (c) True or false, with reason: whenever $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y) \lt 0$, the sum $X+Y$ is less variable than it would be if $X$ and $Y$ were independent with the same individual variances.

Solution

Setup. $\rho = \dfrac{\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}$, and for any $a,b$, $\;\operatorname{var}(aX+bY) = a^2\operatorname{var}(X) + b^2\operatorname{var}(Y) + 2ab\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)$ (G3 §4.8).

(a) Correlation. $\sigma_X = \sqrt4 = 2$, $\sigma_Y = \sqrt9 = 3$, so $$\rho = \frac{-3}{2\cdot3} = \frac{-3}{6} = -0.5 ,$$ inside $[-1,1]$ as it must be.

(b) Two linear combinations. With $a=b=1$: $$\operatorname{var}(X+Y) = 4 + 9 + 2(1)(1)(-3) = 13 - 6 = 7 .$$ With $a=2$, $b=-1$: $$\operatorname{var}(2X-Y) = 2^2(4) + (-1)^2(9) + 2(2)(-1)(-3) = 16 + 9 + 12 = 37 .$$ Note the sign bookkeeping: $2ab = 2(2)(-1) = -4$, times $\operatorname{cov} = -3$, gives $+12$ — a negative covariance increases the variance of a difference.

(c) True. Under independence the covariance term vanishes and $\operatorname{var}(X+Y)$ would be $4+9=13$; with $\operatorname{cov}=-3$ it is $7 \lt 13$. In general $\operatorname{var}(X+Y) = \operatorname{var}(X)+\operatorname{var}(Y)+2\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)$, and a negative covariance subtracts. This is the whole mathematical content of diversification: assets that move oppositely cancel each other's swings.

Answer. $\;\rho = -0.5$; $\;\operatorname{var}(X+Y)=7$; $\;\operatorname{var}(2X-Y)=37$; (c) true.

Trap. Writing $\operatorname{var}(2X-Y) = 4\cdot4 + 9 - 12 = 13$ — that is subtracting the cross term instead of tracking the two sign flips ($b=-1$ and $\operatorname{cov}\lt0$), which multiply to a plus.

Q54. ★ · The uniform, then a monotone transformation · G3 §1.3, §4.2

A resistor's value $X$ is uniform on $[1,3]$ (in ohms). (a) Write $f_X$ and $F_X$, and give $\mathbb{E}[X]$, $\operatorname{var}(X)$ and $\mathbf{P}(1.5 \le X \le 2.5)$.   (b) The corresponding conductance is $Y = 1/X$. Find the PDF of $Y$, verify it integrates to $1$, and compute $\mathbb{E}[Y]$.

Solution

(a) The continuous uniform. On $[a,b]$ the density is constant, and constant $=1/(b-a)$ is forced by normalization (G3 §1.3). With $a=1$, $b=3$: $$f_X(x) = \frac{1}{b-a} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5 \;\;(1 \le x \le 3), \qquad F_X(x) = \frac{x-a}{b-a} = \frac{x-1}{2} \;\;(1 \le x \le 3),$$ with $F_X = 0$ below $1$ and $1$ above $3$; e.g. $F_X(2.5) = 0.75$. The moments are the two formulas worth memorizing, $$\mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{a+b}{2} = \frac{1+3}{2} = 2 \;\Omega, \qquad \operatorname{var}(X) = \frac{(b-a)^2}{12} = \frac{4}{12} = \frac13 = 0.333333 ,$$ so $\sigma_X = 0.577350$. (Check the variance the long way: $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \int_1^3 \frac{x^2}{2}dx = \frac{13}{3} = 4.333333$, and $4.333333 - 2^2 = 0.333333$ ✓.) Probabilities of intervals are pure length ratios — the position inside $[a,b]$ is irrelevant: $$\mathbf{P}(1.5 \le X \le 2.5) = \frac{2.5-1.5}{3-1} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5 .$$

(b) Setup for the transformation. The map $g(x)=1/x$ is strictly decreasing on $[1,3]$, hence strictly monotonic, so the change-of-variables formula applies (G3 §4.2): $$f_Y(y) = \frac{f_X(x)}{\big|\,dg/dx\,\big|}\bigg|_{x = g^{-1}(y)} .$$

Step 1 — the range. As $x$ runs from $1$ up to $3$, $y=1/x$ runs from $1$ down to $1/3 = 0.333333$. So $Y$ lives on $[1/3, 1]$; $f_Y = 0$ outside.

Step 2 — the inverse and the derivative. $y = 1/x \Rightarrow x = 1/y$, and $\dfrac{dg}{dx} = -\dfrac{1}{x^2}$, so $\left|\dfrac{dg}{dx}\right| = \dfrac{1}{x^2}$. The absolute value is what keeps the density positive; dropping it would give a negative "density".

Step 3 — substitute. With $f_X(x)=0.5$ and $x = 1/y$, $$f_Y(y) = \frac{0.5}{1/x^2} = \frac{x^2}{2} = \frac{1}{2y^2}, \qquad \frac13 \le y \le 1 .$$ Endpoint values: $f_Y(1/3)= \frac{1}{2(1/9)} = 4.5$, $f_Y(1)=0.5$. Note the reciprocal map has piled the mass up at the small-$y$ end, even though $X$ was flat.

Step 4 — check. $\displaystyle\int_{1/3}^{1} \frac{dy}{2y^2} = \frac12\Big[-\frac1y\Big]_{1/3}^{1} = \frac12(-1 + 3) = 1$ ✓.

Step 5 — the mean. $$\mathbb{E}[Y] = \int_{1/3}^1 y\cdot\frac{1}{2y^2}dy = \frac12\int_{1/3}^1\frac{dy}{y} = \frac12\big[\ln y\big]_{1/3}^1 = \frac{\ln 3}{2} = 0.549306 .$$ Simulation with $4\times10^6$ draws: $0.549182$ ✓. A second check by a different route: $\mathbf{P}(Y \le 0.75) = \mathbf{P}(X \ge 4/3) = \frac{3-4/3}{2} = 0.833333$, and integrating $f_Y$ over $[1/3, 0.75]$ gives the same $0.833333$ ✓ (simulation $0.833558$).

Answer. $\;f_X = 1/2$ on $[1,3]$, $F_X(x)=(x-1)/2$, $\mathbb{E}[X]=2$, $\operatorname{var}(X)=1/3$, $\mathbf{P}(1.5\le X\le2.5)=0.5$; $\;f_Y(y) = 1/(2y^2)$ on $[1/3,1]$, $\;\mathbb{E}[Y] = \tfrac12\ln 3 \approx 0.549306$.

Traps. (i) Reading $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$-style habits into the uniform and reporting $\operatorname{var}(X) = (b-a)^2 = 4$ or $(b-a)/12$; the formula is $(b-a)^2/12 = 0.333333$, and only the width enters, never the location. (ii) Answering $\mathbb{E}[Y] = 1/\mathbb{E}[X] = 1/2 = 0.5$. Expectation does not pass through nonlinear functions; the true value $0.549306$ is larger, as Jensen's inequality guarantees for the convex map $1/x$.

Q55. ★ · Conditional density · G3 §2.3

Continuing the model of Q52 — $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = \frac23(x+2y)$ on the unit square — find the conditional PDF $f_{Y|X}(y \mid 0.5)$ and the conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = 0.5]$. Compare with the unconditional $\mathbb{E}[Y] = 11/18$. Does the comparison prove $X$ and $Y$ are independent?

Solution

Setup. The conditional density is the slice of the joint at $X=x$, renormalized by the marginal: $f_{Y|X}(y\mid x) = \dfrac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_X(x)}$ wherever $f_X(x) \gt 0$ (G3 §2.3). From Q52, $f_X(x) = \frac23(x+1)$.

Step 1 — the denominator. $f_X(0.5) = \frac23(0.5+1) = \frac23(1.5) = 1$.

Step 2 — the slice. For $0 \le y \le 1$, $$f_{Y|X}(y \mid 0.5) = \frac{\frac23(0.5+2y)}{1} = \frac{0.5+2y}{1.5} .$$ Check that it is a density: $\int_0^1 \frac{0.5+2y}{1.5}dy = \frac{1}{1.5}\big(0.5 + 1\big) = 1$ ✓. Values: $f_{Y|X}(0\mid0.5)=0.333333$, $f_{Y|X}(1\mid0.5)=1.666667$.

Step 3 — conditional expectation. $$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = 0.5] &= \int_0^1 y\,\frac{0.5+2y}{1.5}\,dy = \frac{1}{1.5}\Big(0.5\cdot\frac12 + 2\cdot\frac13\Big)\\ &= \frac{0.25+0.666667}{1.5} = \frac{0.916667}{1.5} = 0.611111 . \end{aligned} $$

Step 4 — the comparison. $\mathbb{E}[Y] = 11/18 = 0.611111$ too — the two agree exactly. This does not prove independence. The general conditional mean is $$\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x] = \frac{\frac{x}{2}+\frac23}{x+1},$$ which equals $0.666667$ at $x=0$ and $0.583333$ at $x=1$ — it genuinely varies with $x$. It crosses the unconditional mean $11/18$ at exactly $x=1/2$: $$\begin{aligned} \frac{\frac{x}{2}+\frac23}{x+1} = \frac{11}{18} \;&\Longrightarrow\; 18\Big(\frac{x}{2}+\frac23\Big) = 11(x+1)\\ &\Longrightarrow\; 9x+12 = 11x+11 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = \tfrac12 , \end{aligned} $$ which is precisely why the value we conditioned on produced the coincidence — not luck, and not evidence of anything. Independence requires $f_{Y|X}(y\mid x)$ to be the same function of $y$ for every $x$, which fails here: at $x=0$ the marginal is $f_X(0)=\frac23(0+1)=\frac23$ (not $1$ — that was $f_X(0.5)$), so $$f_{Y|X}(y \mid 0) = \frac{\frac23(0+2y)}{\frac23} = \frac{\frac43 y}{\frac23} = 2y ,$$ a different function of $y$ from $f_{Y|X}(y\mid0.5) = \frac{0.5+2y}{1.5}$.

Answer. $\;f_{Y|X}(y\mid0.5) = \dfrac{0.5+2y}{1.5}$ on $[0,1]$, $\;\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=0.5] = 11/18 \approx 0.611111$; the coincidence with $\mathbb{E}[Y]$ does not imply independence.

Trap. "One conditional mean equals the unconditional mean, so $X$ and $Y$ are independent." Even the stronger statement $\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x] = \mathbb{E}[Y]$ for all $x$ (mean-independence) would not suffice — see Q64.

Q56. ★★ · Heavy tails and moments · G3 §1.1–1.2

File sizes on a server (in megabytes) are modeled by $f_X(x) = c\,x^{-3}$ for $x \ge 1$, and $0$ for $x \lt 1$. (a) Find $c$.   (b) Find $\mathbf{P}(X \gt 2)$ and the median.   (c) Find $\mathbb{E}[X]$.   (d) Find $\operatorname{var}(X)$.

Solution

Setup. All four parts are improper integrals on $[1,\infty)$; the general antiderivative $\int x^{-p}dx = \frac{x^{1-p}}{1-p}$ converges at $\infty$ exactly when $p \gt 1$. Keep that condition in view — it decides part (d).

(a) Normalization. $$\int_1^\infty c\,x^{-3}dx = c\Big[\frac{x^{-2}}{-2}\Big]_1^\infty = c\Big(0 - \big(-\tfrac12\big)\Big) = \frac{c}{2} = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; c = 2 .$$

(b) Tail and median. The tail is $$\mathbf{P}(X \gt 2) = \int_2^\infty 2x^{-3}dx = \Big[-x^{-2}\Big]_2^\infty = 0 + \frac{1}{4} = 0.25 .$$ In general $\mathbf{P}(X \gt t) = t^{-2}$ for $t \ge 1$, so $F_X(t) = 1 - t^{-2}$ and the median solves $1 - m^{-2} = 0.5 \Rightarrow m^{-2} = 0.5 \Rightarrow m = \sqrt2 = 1.414214$ MB.

(c) Mean. Here $p = 2 \gt 1$, so the integral converges: $$\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_1^\infty x\cdot 2x^{-3}dx = \int_1^\infty 2x^{-2}dx = \Big[-2x^{-1}\Big]_1^\infty = 0 + 2 = 2 \text{ MB}.$$ Note the mean ($2$) sits well above the median ($1.414$) — the standard heavy-tail signature.

(d) Variance — the point of the problem. $$\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \int_1^\infty x^2\cdot2x^{-3}dx = \int_1^\infty \frac{2}{x}\,dx = \big[2\ln x\big]_1^\infty = \lim_{M\to\infty} 2\ln M = \infty .$$ The integrand is $2/x$, the borderline case $p=1$, which diverges — logarithmically, hence slowly: truncating at $M=10$ gives $2\ln10 = 4.605170$, at $M=1000$ gives $13.815511$, and it keeps climbing without limit. So $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ does not exist (as a finite number), and consequently $\operatorname{var}(X)$ is infinite. The distribution has a perfectly good finite mean and no finite variance at all.

Answer. $\;c=2$; $\;\mathbf{P}(X \gt 2)=1/4$, median $\sqrt2 \approx 1.414214$; $\;\mathbb{E}[X]=2$; $\;\operatorname{var}(X) = \infty$.

Trap. Reporting $\operatorname{var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mathbb{E}[X]^2 = 4-4 = 0$. That comes from mis-integrating $\int 2x^{-1}dx$ as $-2x^{-2}\big/\!\ldots$ or from assuming a finite mean forces a finite variance, and a variance of $0$ for a spread-out density should be an immediate alarm: zero variance means the random variable is a constant.

Tool: G3 §1.1 (normalization), §1.2 (moments by integration) · B&T §3.1

Q57. ★★ · A mixed distribution · G3 §1.4

You arrive at a traffic light. With probability $0.3$ the light is green and you wait $W = 0$; otherwise your wait $W$ is uniform on $[0,2]$ minutes. (a) Write the CDF $F_W$ and identify the jump and the ramp.   (b) Compute $\mathbf{P}(W \le 1)$, $\mathbf{P}(0 \lt W \le 1)$ and the median.   (c) Compute $\mathbb{E}[W]$ and $\operatorname{var}(W)$.

Solution

Setup. $W$ is neither discrete nor continuous: it puts an atom of probability at $0$ and spreads the rest as a density. The CDF handles all three worlds uniformly (G3 §1.4) — jumps for atoms, slopes for density.

(a) The CDF. Condition on the light. For $0 \le w \lt 2$, $$F_W(w) = \mathbf{P}(\text{green}) \cdot 1 + \mathbf{P}(\text{red}) \cdot \mathbf{P}(U \le w) = 0.3 + 0.7\cdot\frac{w}{2} = 0.3 + 0.35\,w ,$$ with $F_W(w)=0$ for $w \lt 0$ and $F_W(w)=1$ for $w \ge 2$. Check the top end: $0.3 + 0.35(2) = 1$ ✓. So the CDF jumps by $0.3$ at $w=0$ — that jump is $\mathbf{P}(W=0)=0.3$ — and then ramps with slope $0.35$ across $[0,2]$; the slope is the density on the continuous part, $f(w)=0.35$ for $0 \lt w \lt 2$ (which is $0.7 \times \frac12$, the red probability times the uniform density).

(b) Probabilities. $$\mathbf{P}(W \le 1) = F_W(1) = 0.3 + 0.35(1) = 0.65 ; \qquad \mathbf{P}(0 \lt W \le 1) = 0.65 - 0.3 = 0.35 .$$ Here, unlike in the purely continuous case, the strict inequality matters: excluding the atom removes $0.3$. The median solves $0.3+0.35m = 0.5 \Rightarrow m = 0.2/0.35 = 0.571429$ minutes.

(c) Moments. Expectation of a mixed variable = atom contribution + integral against the density: $$\mathbb{E}[W] = \underbrace{0.3 \times 0}_{\text{atom}} + \int_0^2 w\,(0.35)\,dw = 0.35\Big[\frac{w^2}{2}\Big]_0^2 = 0.35(2) = 0.7 \text{ min}.$$ Cross-check by total expectation: $0.3(0) + 0.7(1) = 0.7$ ✓. Second moment: $$\mathbb{E}[W^2] = 0 + \int_0^2 w^2(0.35)\,dw = 0.35\Big[\frac{w^3}{3}\Big]_0^2 = 0.35\cdot\frac83 = 0.933333 ,$$ so $$\operatorname{var}(W) = 0.933333 - (0.7)^2 = 0.933333 - 0.49 = 0.443333 .$$ Simulation of $4\times10^6$ arrivals: $\mathbb{E}[W]\approx0.700$, $\operatorname{var}\approx0.4434$ ✓.

Answer. $\;F_W(w) = 0.3+0.35w$ on $[0,2)$ (jump $0.3$ at $0$, slope $0.35$); $\;\mathbf{P}(W\le1)=0.65$, $\;\mathbf{P}(0\lt W\le1)=0.35$, median $= 4/7 \approx 0.571429$; $\;\mathbb{E}[W]=0.7$, $\;\operatorname{var}(W)=0.443333$.

Trap. Treating $W$ as $U(0,2)$ conditional on red and forgetting the atom, giving $\mathbb{E}[W]=1$ and $\operatorname{var}(W)=1/3=0.333$. Also common: "$\mathbf{P}(W\le1)=\mathbf{P}(W\lt1)$ because $W$ is continuous" — false at $w=0$, where the CDF genuinely jumps.

Tool: G3 §1.4 (CDFs cover discrete, continuous and mixed) · B&T §3.2

Q58. ★★ · Competing exponentials · G3 §1.6

A server has two independent failure modes: a disk failure at time $X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda_1)$ with $\mathbb{E}[X]=4$ hours, and a power failure at time $Y \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda_2)$ with $\mathbb{E}[Y]=6$ hours. The server goes down at $M = \min\{X,Y\}$ and is fully repaired only at $L = \max\{X,Y\}$. (a) What is $\mathbf{P}(X \lt Y)$, i.e. that the disk fails first?   (b) What is the distribution of $M$, and $\mathbb{E}[M]$?   (c) What is $\mathbb{E}[L]$?

Solution

Setup. $\lambda_1 = 1/4 = 0.25$ and $\lambda_2 = 1/6 = 0.166667$ per hour. The tools are the tail form $\mathbf{P}(X \gt t)=e^{-\lambda_1 t}$ and the min-of-independent-exponentials argument of G3 §1.6.

(a) Who fails first. Condition on $Y$ and integrate: $$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{P}(X \lt Y) &= \int_0^\infty \mathbf{P}(X \lt y)\,f_Y(y)\,dy = \int_0^\infty \big(1-e^{-\lambda_1 y}\big)\lambda_2 e^{-\lambda_2 y}\,dy\\ &= 1 - \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2} = \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}, \end{aligned} $$ where the middle integral used $\int_0^\infty \lambda_2 e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)y}dy = \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}$. Substituting, $\lambda_1+\lambda_2 = 0.25+0.166667 = 0.416667$ and $$\mathbf{P}(X \lt Y) = \frac{0.25}{0.416667} = 0.6 .$$ Interpretation: the winner is chosen in proportion to rates, not to means. Simulation: $0.599707$ ✓.

(b) The minimum. For $m \ge 0$, using independence, $$\mathbf{P}(M \gt m) = \mathbf{P}(X \gt m,\, Y \gt m) = e^{-\lambda_1 m}e^{-\lambda_2 m} = e^{-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)m},$$ which is exactly the tail of an exponential with rate $\lambda_1+\lambda_2 = 0.416667$ — rates add. Therefore $$\mathbb{E}[M] = \frac{1}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2} = \frac{1}{0.416667} = 2.4 \text{ hours},$$ shorter than either individual mean ($4$ and $6$), as two ways to fail must be. As a bonus, $\mathbf{P}(M \gt 3) = e^{-0.416667\times3} = e^{-1.25} = 0.286505$.

(c) The maximum, without any new integral. Pointwise, $\min\{X,Y\}+\max\{X,Y\} = X+Y$ — whichever is smaller, the two sides list the same two numbers. Taking expectations and using linearity, $$\mathbb{E}[L] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y] - \mathbb{E}[M] = 4 + 6 - 2.4 = 7.6 \text{ hours}.$$ Simulation: $\mathbb{E}[M]\approx2.4003$, $\mathbb{E}[L]\approx7.5972$ ✓.

Answer. $\;\mathbf{P}(X\lt Y)=0.6$; $\;M \sim \text{Exp}(0.416667)$ with $\mathbb{E}[M]=2.4$ h; $\;\mathbb{E}[L]=7.6$ h.

Traps. (i) Answering $\mathbf{P}(X\lt Y)=\frac{4}{4+6}=0.4$ by mixing means with rates — it is backwards, and the faster-failing disk must win more than half the time. (ii) Claiming $L$ is exponential: it is not, since $\mathbb{E}[L]=7.6 \ne \mathbb{E}[X]+\mathbb{E}[Y]=10$ and its density $\lambda_1e^{-\lambda_1\ell}(1-e^{-\lambda_2\ell}) + \lambda_2e^{-\lambda_2\ell}(1-e^{-\lambda_1\ell})$ is not a single exponential. Only the minimum inherits the family.

Q59. ★★ · Inverse normal / design · G3 §1.7

A bottling machine dispenses a volume that is normal with standard deviation $\sigma = 1.2$ ml; the mean $\mu$ is set by a dial. Regulation requires that at most $1\%$ of bottles contain less than the labeled $500$ ml. Using $\Phi(2.33) = 0.9901$ (so $z_{0.99} \approx 2.33$), find the smallest admissible $\mu$. Explain in one sentence why the answer scales linearly in $\sigma$.

Solution

Setup. Let $X \sim N(\mu, 1.2^2)$ be the fill volume. The requirement is $\mathbf{P}(X \lt 500) \le 0.01$. This is an inverse normal problem: the probability is given and the parameter is unknown, so we standardize and then read the table backwards (G3 §1.7).

Step 1 — standardize the constraint. $$\mathbf{P}(X \lt 500) = \Phi\!\Big(\frac{500-\mu}{1.2}\Big) \le 0.01 .$$

Step 2 — invert $\Phi$. Since $\Phi$ is strictly increasing, the constraint is $\dfrac{500-\mu}{1.2} \le \Phi^{-1}(0.01)$. By symmetry $\Phi^{-1}(0.01) = -\Phi^{-1}(0.99) = -2.33$ (the exact value is $-2.326348$). So $$\frac{500-\mu}{1.2} \le -2.33 .$$

Step 3 — solve for $\mu$. Multiply by $1.2$ (positive, so the inequality direction survives): $$500 - \mu \le -2.796 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mu \ge 500 + 2.796 = 502.796 \text{ ml}.$$ With the exact quantile $2.326348$ the bound is $500 + 2.791617 = 502.791617$ ml; the table rounding costs about $0.004$ ml. Verification: at $\mu = 502.796$, $\mathbf{P}(X\lt500) = \Phi(-2.33) = 0.009903 \le 0.01$ ✓.

Why linear in $\sigma$. The admissible mean is $\mu = 500 + z_{0.99}\,\sigma$: the safety margin is a fixed number of standard deviations, so halving the machine's variability to $\sigma=0.6$ halves the overfill to $1.398$ ml — which is precisely the business case for a more precise filler.

Answer. $\;\mu \ge 502.796$ ml (exact: $502.7916$ ml).

Trap. Using $z = 1.96$ and answering $502.352$ ml. $1.96$ is the two-sided $95\%$ value; here the requirement is one-sided at the $1\%$ level, whose quantile is $2.33$. A one-sided requirement never uses a two-sided quantile.

Q60. ★★ · Continuous prior, discrete data · G3 §3.5

A newly assembled sensor fails each self-test independently with an unknown probability $\Theta$. From the batch's history, the prior density is $f_\Theta(\theta) = 2\theta$ for $0 \le \theta \le 1$ (a poor batch: large $\theta$ is more likely a priori). You run $n=3$ independent self-tests and observe exactly $X = 1$ failure. (a) Write the Bayes rule for this case — continuous unknown, discrete observation — and evaluate its denominator $\mathbf{P}(X=1)$.   (b) Find the posterior density $f_{\Theta \mid X}(\theta \mid 1)$ and check it integrates to $1$.   (c) Find the posterior mean $\mathbb{E}[\Theta \mid X=1]$ and compare it with the prior mean and with the maximum-likelihood estimate $1/3$.   (d) Find $\mathbf{P}(\Theta \gt 1/2 \mid X=1)$ and compare with the prior $\mathbf{P}(\Theta \gt 1/2)$.

Solution

Setup. Bayes' rule has four faces, one for each combination of discrete/continuous unknown and discrete/continuous observation (G3 §3.1). Here the unknown $\Theta$ is continuous and the data $X$ is discrete, so the posterior is a density in $\theta$ built from a PMF in $x$, and the normalizer is an integral over $\theta$: $$f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta \mid x) = \frac{f_\Theta(\theta)\,p_{X\mid\Theta}(x \mid \theta)}{\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_\Theta(t)\,p_{X\mid\Theta}(x \mid t)\,dt} .$$ Mixing the two — summing where you should integrate, or vice versa — is the one structural error in this topic. Given $\Theta=\theta$, $X \sim \text{Bin}(3,\theta)$, so $p_{X\mid\Theta}(1\mid\theta) = \binom{3}{1}\theta(1-\theta)^2 = 3\theta(1-\theta)^2$.

(a) The denominator. It is the total-probability integral, and it is a genuine probability — the chance of seeing one failure before you knew $\theta$: $$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{P}(X=1) &= \int_0^1 2\theta \cdot 3\theta(1-\theta)^2\,d\theta = 6\int_0^1 \theta^2(1-\theta)^2\,d\theta\\ &= 6\int_0^1 \big(\theta^2 - 2\theta^3 + \theta^4\big)d\theta = 6\Big(\frac13-\frac12+\frac15\Big) = 6\cdot\frac{1}{30} = \frac15 = 0.2 . \end{aligned} $$ (The middle integral $\int_0^1\theta^2(1-\theta)^2d\theta = 1/30 = 0.033333$ is the Beta integral $B(3,3) = \frac{2!\,2!}{5!}$; the factorial form saves the expansion.)

(b) The posterior. Divide the numerator by that constant: $$f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta \mid 1) = \frac{2\theta \cdot 3\theta(1-\theta)^2}{1/5} = 30\,\theta^2(1-\theta)^2, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 1 ,$$ and $0$ elsewhere. Check: $30 \cdot \frac{1}{30} = 1$ ✓. This is the Beta$(3,3)$ density — symmetric about $\theta = 1/2$, with $f_{\Theta\mid X}(0.5\mid1) = 30(0.25)(0.25) = 1.875$ and $f_{\Theta\mid X}(0.25\mid1) = 1.054688$. Note what happened to the shape: the prior $2\theta$ was increasing and pinned $f_\Theta(0)=0$; the likelihood $3\theta(1-\theta)^2$ pushes back against large $\theta$ because two of the three tests passed. The product is a hump.

(c) Posterior mean. Integrate against the posterior: $$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\Theta \mid X=1] &= \int_0^1 \theta \cdot 30\theta^2(1-\theta)^2\,d\theta = 30\int_0^1\big(\theta^3 - 2\theta^4 + \theta^5\big)d\theta\\ &= 30\Big(\frac14 - \frac25 + \frac16\Big) = 30\cdot\frac{1}{60} = \frac12 = 0.5 . \end{aligned} $$ The prior mean was $\mathbb{E}[\Theta] = \int_0^1 \theta\cdot2\theta\,d\theta = 2/3 = 0.666667$, and the data alone (maximum likelihood, one failure in three) point at $1/3 = 0.333333$. The posterior mean $0.5$ sits between them — that is what a posterior always does with a proper prior and finite data: it is a compromise, pulled toward the data and away from the prior in proportion to how much data there is. With $n=3$ the pull is only halfway.

(d) A posterior probability. Beta$(3,3)$ is symmetric about $1/2$, so $$\mathbf{P}(\Theta \gt 1/2 \mid X=1) = \int_{0.5}^{1} 30\theta^2(1-\theta)^2\,d\theta = 0.5 ,$$ against the prior value $\mathbf{P}(\Theta \gt 1/2) = \int_{0.5}^1 2\theta\,d\theta = \big[\theta^2\big]_{0.5}^1 = 1 - 0.25 = 0.75$. One observed failure out of three has cut the odds that the batch is worse than a coin flip from $3{:}1$ down to even. Simulation ($4\times10^6$ prior draws $\Theta = \sqrt{U}$, then $X\sim\text{Bin}(3,\Theta)$, keeping the draws with $X=1$): $\mathbf{P}(X=1)\approx0.199974$, posterior mean $\approx0.499877$, $\mathbf{P}(\Theta\gt1/2\mid X=1)\approx0.499411$ ✓.

Answer. $\;\mathbf{P}(X=1)=1/5$; $\;f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid1) = 30\theta^2(1-\theta)^2$ on $[0,1]$ (Beta$(3,3)$); $\;\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=1]=1/2$; $\;\mathbf{P}(\Theta\gt1/2\mid X=1)=1/2$ (prior: $3/4$).

Traps. (i) Reporting $1/3$ — the maximum-likelihood estimate — as the answer to (c). That throws the prior away; the question asked for a posterior quantity. (ii) Skipping the normalization and calling $6\theta^2(1-\theta)^2$ the posterior. It integrates to $1/5$, not $1$, so every probability read off it is five times too small. (iii) Writing $\sum_\theta$ in the denominator out of habit from discrete Bayes: $\Theta$ is continuous, so the total-probability step is $\int f_\Theta(t)p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid t)\,dt$.

Tool: G3 §3.5 (C$\,|\,$D: continuous unknown, discrete evidence), §3.1 (one rule, four faces) · B&T §3.6

Q61. ★★ · Conditional density on a wedge · G3 §2.3, §2.5

A job arrives at time $Y$ and is started at some earlier-recorded time $X$, with joint PDF $$f_{X,Y}(x,y) = c\,e^{-y}, \qquad 0 \le x \le y \lt \infty,$$ and $0$ elsewhere. (a) Find $c$.   (b) Find $f_Y$ and $f_X$, and name each distribution.   (c) Find $f_{X|Y}(x \mid y)$ and $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y=y]$.   (d) Use the total expectation theorem to get $\mathbb{E}[X]$, and confirm it against the marginal.

Solution

Setup. The support is the wedge above the diagonal in the first quadrant. Order the integration so the inner limits are finite: for a fixed $y$, $x$ ranges over $[0,y]$.

(a) Normalization. $$\begin{aligned} \int_0^\infty\!\!\int_0^{y} c\,e^{-y}\,dx\,dy &= \int_0^\infty c\,y\,e^{-y}\,dy = c\cdot\Gamma(2) = c \cdot 1 = 1\\ &\Longrightarrow\; c = 1, \end{aligned} $$ using $\int_0^\infty y e^{-y}dy = \big[-(y+1)e^{-y}\big]_0^\infty = 1$.

(b) Marginals. Integrating out $x$ (the inner integral above, with $c=1$): $$f_Y(y) = \int_0^{y} e^{-y}\,dx = y\,e^{-y}, \qquad y \ge 0,$$ the Erlang/Gamma$(2,1)$ density — the sum of two independent $\text{Exp}(1)$'s. Values: $f_Y(1)=0.367879$, $f_Y(2)=0.270671$. Integrating out $y$ (now $y$ runs from $x$ to $\infty$): $$f_X(x) = \int_{x}^{\infty} e^{-y}\,dy = e^{-x}, \qquad x \ge 0,$$ so $X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ exactly. Check: $\mathbf{P}(Y \le 1) = \int_0^1 ye^{-y}dy = 1-2e^{-1} = 0.264241$.

(c) The conditional density — a flat slice. For $y \gt 0$, $$f_{X|Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)} = \frac{e^{-y}}{y\,e^{-y}} = \frac{1}{y}, \qquad 0 \le x \le y .$$ The $e^{-y}$ cancels completely: given $Y=y$, $X$ is uniform on $[0,y]$. This is the general fact that renormalizing a constant slice gives a uniform conditional. Hence $$\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y=y] = \frac{y}{2},$$ and e.g. $\mathbf{P}(X \le 1 \mid Y = 3) = 1/3$.

(d) Total expectation. First $\mathbb{E}[Y] = \int_0^\infty y\cdot ye^{-y}dy = \Gamma(3) = 2$ (or: the mean of a sum of two $\text{Exp}(1)$'s). Then, since $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y] = Y/2$ is linear in $Y$, $$\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\big] = \mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{Y}{2}\Big] = \frac{\mathbb{E}[Y]}{2} = \frac{2}{2} = 1 .$$ Confirmation from the marginal: $X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ has mean $1$ ✓. Simulation ($4\times10^6$ draws of $Y\sim\text{Gamma}(2,1)$ then $X\mid Y \sim U(0,Y)$): $\mathbb{E}[X]\approx0.999$, $\mathbb{E}[Y]\approx1.999$, $\mathbf{P}(X\le1)\approx0.6323$ vs. the exact $1-e^{-1}=0.632121$ ✓.

Answer. $\;c=1$; $\;f_Y(y)=ye^{-y}$, $\;f_X(x)=e^{-x}$; $\;f_{X|Y}(x\mid y)=1/y$ on $[0,y]$ with $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]=y/2$; $\;\mathbb{E}[X]=1$.

Trap. Writing $f_Y(y) = \int_0^\infty e^{-y}dx$ (limits $0$ to $\infty$ for $x$) and getting $\infty$. On a non-rectangular support the inner limits depend on the outer variable — here $x$ stops at $y$.

Tool: G3 §2.2 (marginals), §2.3 (conditional PDFs), §2.5 (total expectation) · B&T §3.5

Q62. ★★ · Which tool? Derived distribution · G3 §4.1–4.2

Let $X \sim U(0,1)$ and $Y = \tan\!\big(\pi(X - \tfrac12)\big)$ — the horizontal position where a beam pivoted at unit distance and pointed in a uniformly random direction strikes a wall. (a) Which tool applies — the CDF method or the monotonic change-of-variables formula — and why?   (b) Find $f_Y$.   (c) Find $\mathbf{P}(|Y| \le 1)$.   (d) Does $\mathbb{E}[Y]$ exist?

Solution

(a) Tool selection. On the open interval $(0,1)$ the argument $\pi(x-\frac12)$ runs over $(-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2)$, where $\tan$ is strictly increasing and differentiable with nonzero derivative. So $g$ is strictly monotonic, one-to-one from $(0,1)$ onto all of $\mathbb{R}$, and the change-of-variables formula of G3 §4.2 applies directly: $$f_Y(y) = \frac{f_X(x)}{|dg/dx|}\bigg|_{x=g^{-1}(y)} .$$ (The CDF method would work too and gives the same answer — monotonicity is exactly the condition that makes the shortcut legal. Reach for the CDF method only when $g$ folds two $x$'s onto one $y$, as in Q68.)

(b) The density. The derivative is $$\frac{dg}{dx} = \pi\sec^2\!\big(\pi(x-\tfrac12)\big) = \pi\big(1+\tan^2(\pi(x-\tfrac12))\big) = \pi(1+y^2),$$ using the identity $\sec^2\theta = 1+\tan^2\theta$ and then substituting $y = \tan(\cdot)$ — a neat trick that avoids ever writing $x$ in terms of $y$. It is positive, so the absolute value changes nothing. With $f_X(x)=1$, $$\boxed{\;f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{\pi(1+y^2)}, \qquad -\infty \lt y \lt \infty\;}$$ — the standard Cauchy density. Values: $f_Y(0) = 1/\pi = 0.318310$, $f_Y(1) = 1/(2\pi) = 0.159155$. Check: $\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{dy}{\pi(1+y^2)} = \frac{1}{\pi}\big[\arctan y\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = \frac1\pi\big(\frac\pi2+\frac\pi2\big) = 1$ ✓.

(c) A probability. $$\mathbf{P}(|Y| \le 1) = \frac{1}{\pi}\big[\arctan y\big]_{-1}^{1} = \frac{1}{\pi}\Big(\frac\pi4 + \frac\pi4\Big) = 0.5 .$$ Sanity check with no calculus: $|Y| \le 1 \iff |\pi(X-\frac12)| \le \frac\pi4 \iff X \in [0.25, 0.75]$, an interval of length $0.5$ ✓. Simulation: $0.499451$ ✓. (For scale, $\mathbf{P}(|Y|\le10) = 0.936549$ — still $6\%$ of beams land beyond ten units.)

(d) No. $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{|y|}{\pi(1+y^2)}dy = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \frac{y\,dy}{1+y^2} = \frac{1}{\pi}\big[\ln(1+y^2)\big]_0^\infty = \infty$: truncating at $M=1000$ already gives $4.397614$ and it grows like $\frac2\pi\ln M$ forever. Since $\mathbb{E}[|Y|]=\infty$, $\mathbb{E}[Y]$ is undefined — the two tails are individually infinite and their "cancellation" is not a legitimate value.

Answer. $\;f_Y(y) = \dfrac{1}{\pi(1+y^2)}$ (standard Cauchy); $\;\mathbf{P}(|Y|\le1)=0.5$; $\;\mathbb{E}[Y]$ does not exist.

Trap. Answering $\mathbb{E}[Y]=0$ "by symmetry". Symmetry gives the median $0$ (simulation median: $-0.0014$), but a mean requires absolute integrability first. Averaging Cauchy samples does not converge: the sample mean of $n$ Cauchy draws is itself Cauchy, no matter how large $n$ is.

Q63. ★★ · Convolution · G3 §4.6

A packet's total delay is $Z = X + Y$, where the queueing delay $X \sim U(0,1)$ and the transmission delay $Y \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ are independent (milliseconds). Find $f_Z$, verify it integrates to $1$, and compute $\mathbb{E}[Z]$ and $\operatorname{var}(Z)$.

Solution

Setup. For independent continuous $X,Y$, the density of the sum is the convolution (G3 §4.6) $$f_Z(z) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x)\,f_Y(z-x)\,dx .$$ Here $f_X(x)=1$ only for $0 \le x \le 1$, and $f_Y(z-x) = e^{-(z-x)}$ only for $z-x \ge 0$, i.e. $x \le z$. The integrand is therefore supported on $x \in [0, \min(z,1)]$, and $f_Z(z)=0$ for $z \lt 0$. Two cases follow.

Case 1: $0 \le z \le 1$. The upper limit is $z$: $$f_Z(z) = \int_0^{z} e^{-(z-x)}dx = e^{-z}\int_0^z e^{x}dx = e^{-z}\big(e^{z}-1\big) = 1 - e^{-z} .$$ At $z=0.5$: $1-e^{-0.5} = 0.393469$. At $z=1$: $1-e^{-1}=0.632121$.

Case 2: $z \gt 1$. The uniform runs out first, so the upper limit is $1$: $$f_Z(z) = \int_0^{1} e^{-(z-x)}dx = e^{-z}\big(e^{1}-1\big) = (e-1)e^{-z} = 1.718282\,e^{-z} .$$ At $z=1$ this gives $1.718282 \times 0.367879 = 0.632121$ — matching Case 1, so $f_Z$ is continuous at the seam ✓. At $z=2$: $1.718282 \times 0.135335 = 0.232544$.

Normalization check. $$\int_0^1\big(1-e^{-z}\big)dz = 1 - \big(1-e^{-1}\big) = e^{-1} = 0.367879, \qquad \int_1^\infty (e-1)e^{-z}dz = (e-1)e^{-1} = 1-e^{-1} = 0.632121 ,$$ and $0.367879+0.632121 = 1$ ✓.

Moments — no integration needed. Linearity of expectation gives $\mathbb{E}[Z] = \mathbb{E}[X]+\mathbb{E}[Y] = 0.5 + 1 = 1.5$ ms; independence lets variances add, $$\operatorname{var}(Z) = \frac{(1-0)^2}{12} + \frac{1}{1^2} = 0.083333 + 1 = 1.083333 = \tfrac{13}{12}.$$ Simulation ($4\times10^6$): $\mathbb{E}[Z]\approx1.5007$, $\operatorname{var}\approx1.0849$, $\mathbf{P}(Z\le1)\approx0.36796$ vs. the exact $e^{-1}=0.367879$ ✓.

Answer. $\;f_Z(z) = 1-e^{-z}$ for $0\le z\le 1$ and $(e-1)e^{-z}$ for $z \gt 1$; $\;\mathbb{E}[Z]=1.5$, $\;\operatorname{var}(Z)=13/12\approx1.083333$.

Trap. Using the single formula $\int_0^z e^{-(z-x)}dx$ for all $z$, giving $f_Z(z) = 1-e^{-z} \to 1$ as $z\to\infty$ — a density that does not decay, and whose integral diverges. The finite support of $f_X$ must cap the integration limit.

Q64. ★★ · Uncorrelated but dependent · G3 §4.8

A point is chosen uniformly on the unit circle: $\Theta \sim U(0,2\pi)$, $X = \cos\Theta$, $Y = \sin\Theta$. (a) Compute $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)$ and $\rho(X,Y)$.   (b) True or false, with reason: $X$ and $Y$ are independent.   (c) Give a single conditional probability that settles (b) beyond argument.

Solution

Setup. $\Theta$ has density $\frac{1}{2\pi}$ on $[0,2\pi]$; expectations of functions of $\Theta$ are integrals against it (the expected-value rule).

(a) Covariance. Each of $\cos$ and $\sin$ integrates to zero over a full period: $$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[X] &= \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\theta\,d\theta = \frac{1}{2\pi}\big[\sin\theta\big]_0^{2\pi} = 0,\\ \mathbb{E}[Y] &= \frac{1}{2\pi}\big[-\cos\theta\big]_0^{2\pi} = 0 . \end{aligned} $$ For the cross term use the double-angle identity $\sin\theta\cos\theta = \frac12\sin2\theta$: $$\mathbb{E}[XY] = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\tfrac12\sin2\theta\,d\theta = \frac{1}{2\pi}\Big[-\frac{\cos2\theta}{4}\Big]_0^{2\pi} = 0 .$$ Therefore $$\operatorname{cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y] = 0 - 0 = 0, \qquad \rho(X,Y) = 0$$ (the correlation is defined, since $\operatorname{var}(X) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \frac12 \gt 0$). Simulation of $4\times10^6$ angles: sample covariance $-0.000057$ ✓.

(b) False. $X$ and $Y$ are as dependent as possible: they satisfy the exact relation $X^2+Y^2 = \cos^2\Theta+\sin^2\Theta = 1$, so knowing $X$ pins $|Y|$ completely. Zero correlation only says there is no linear trend; the dependence here is perfectly circular, and the positive contributions to $\mathbb{E}[XY]$ from the first and third quadrants exactly cancel the negative ones from the second and fourth.

(c) The decisive conditional probability. Unconditionally, $\mathbf{P}(|Y| \le 0.1) = \frac{2\arcsin(0.1)}{\pi} = 0.063769 \gt 0$. But given $X = 0.9$ we must have $|Y| = \sqrt{1-0.81} = \sqrt{0.19} = 0.435890$, so $$\mathbf{P}(|Y| \le 0.1 \mid X = 0.9) = 0 \ne 0.063769 = \mathbf{P}(|Y| \le 0.1) .$$ Conditioning changed the probability, which is the definition of dependence. (Simulation restricted to $|X-0.9| \lt 0.01$: the empirical conditional frequency is exactly $0$ ✓.)

Answer. $\;\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=0$ and $\rho=0$, yet $X$ and $Y$ are dependent (they satisfy $X^2+Y^2=1$).

Trap. "$\rho = 0 \Rightarrow$ independent." The implication runs one way only: independence forces $\rho=0$, never the reverse. (The one important exception, worth remembering: for a jointly normal pair, zero correlation does imply independence.)

Q65. ★★ · Sum of independent normals · G3 §4.7

A part is made in two stages. Stage one adds length $X \sim N(10, 0.04)$ mm and stage two adds $Y \sim N(15, 0.09)$ mm, independently (the second arguments are variances). The part is scrapped if the total exceeds $25.5$ mm. What fraction is scrapped? Use $\Phi(1.39) = 0.9177$.

Solution

Setup. The sum of independent normals is normal (G3 §4.7) — a fact you may quote, and one that is special to the normal family. Means add always; variances add because of independence.

Step 1 — the distribution of the total. $T = X+Y$ with $$\mathbb{E}[T] = 10+15 = 25 \text{ mm}, \qquad \operatorname{var}(T) = 0.04+0.09 = 0.13 \text{ mm}^2, \qquad \sigma_T = \sqrt{0.13} = 0.360555 \text{ mm},$$ so $T \sim N(25, 0.13)$.

Step 2 — standardize. $$z = \frac{25.5-25}{0.360555} = \frac{0.5}{0.360555} = 1.386750 \approx 1.39 .$$

Step 3 — the tail. $$\mathbf{P}(T \gt 25.5) = 1 - \Phi(1.39) = 1 - 0.9177 = 0.0823 ,$$ i.e. about $8.2\%$ of parts are scrapped. Exact value with $z = 1.386750$: $1 - 0.917241 = 0.082759$; a $4\times10^6$-part simulation gives $0.082992$ ✓.

Answer. $\;\mathbf{P}(T \gt 25.5) \approx 0.0823$ (exact $0.08276$) — roughly $8\%$ scrapped.

Trap. Adding standard deviations: $0.2+0.3 = 0.5$, giving $z = 0.5/0.5 = 1$ and the answer $1-\Phi(1) = 0.158655$ — almost double the truth. Variances add under independence; standard deviations never do (here $\sqrt{0.13}=0.3606 \lt 0.5$, since $\sqrt{a^2+b^2} \lt a+b$).

Q66. ★★ · Spot the error: independence · G3 §2.4

A student writes: "Let $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = 8xy$ for $0 \le y \le x \le 1$ and $0$ elsewhere. Since $8xy = (8x)\cdot(y)$ factors into a function of $x$ times a function of $y$, the variables $X$ and $Y$ are independent." Verify that $f_{X,Y}$ is a legitimate joint PDF, then find and explain the error, and demonstrate it with a numerical counterexample.

Solution

Step 1 — it is a legitimate PDF. It is nonnegative on its support, and $$\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{x} 8xy\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1 8x\Big[\frac{y^2}{2}\Big]_0^{x}dx = \int_0^1 4x^3\,dx = \big[x^4\big]_0^1 = 1 \;\text{✓}$$ So the setup is sound; only the conclusion is wrong.

Step 2 — the error. The factorization criterion (G3 §2.4) is $$X \perp Y \iff f_{X,Y}(x,y) = f_X(x)f_Y(y) \quad \text{for every } (x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 ,$$ and the words for every are the whole of it — the identity must hold at all points of the plane, including the points where the joint density is $0$. Here the formula $8xy$ factors, but it holds only on the triangle $\{0 \le y \le x \le 1\}$; off the triangle the joint density is $0$ while the product of two positive marginals is not. Equivalently: if the support is not a rectangle (a product set), the variables cannot be independent, whatever the algebra of the formula looks like. The constraint $Y \le X$ is itself a statement about $X$ and $Y$ jointly.

Step 3 — the marginals. $$\begin{aligned} f_X(x) &= \int_0^{x} 8xy\,dy = 4x^3, &&0 \le x \le 1;\\ f_Y(y) &= \int_{y}^{1} 8xy\,dx = 8y\Big[\frac{x^2}{2}\Big]_{y}^{1} = 4y\big(1-y^2\big), &&0 \le y \le 1 . \end{aligned} $$ Both check: $\int_0^1 4x^3dx = 1$ and $\int_0^1 4y(1-y^2)dy = 4(\frac12-\frac14) = 1$ ✓. Note that the true marginals are not the naive factors $8x$ and $y$ — another sign something was wrong.

Step 4 — the counterexample. Take the point $(x,y) = (0.5, 0.75)$, which lies outside the triangle since $y \gt x$: $$f_{X,Y}(0.5,0.75) = 0, \qquad f_X(0.5)\,f_Y(0.75) = 4(0.5)^3 \cdot 4(0.75)\big(1-0.5625\big) = 0.5 \times 1.3125 = 0.65625 .$$ Since $0 \ne 0.65625$, the factorization identity fails and $X$ and $Y$ are dependent. Common sense agrees: observing $X = 0.5$ tells you $Y \le 0.5$ with certainty.

Answer. The claim is false. The formula factors but the support does not: $f_{X,Y}(0.5,0.75) = 0 \ne 0.65625 = f_X(0.5)f_Y(0.75)$, so $X$ and $Y$ are dependent.

Trap. "It factors, so they're independent" is the single most reliable way to get an independence question wrong. Before factoring, always draw the support: rectangle (with sides parallel to the axes) means independence is possible; anything else — triangle, disk, wedge — rules it out immediately.

Q67. ★★ · Spot the error: memorylessness · G3 §1.5

A help-desk call duration $T$ is exponential with mean $5$ minutes. A student argues: "The exponential is memoryless, so if a call has already lasted $3$ minutes, then $\mathbb{E}[T \mid T \gt 3] = \mathbb{E}[T] = 5$ minutes." Is this right? Compute $\mathbb{E}[T \mid T \gt 3]$ correctly, in two ways.

Solution

The verdict: wrong. Memorylessness says the remaining time is a fresh $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$, not that the total time is. The student has conflated $T$ with $T-3$.

Method 1 — memorylessness used correctly. With $\lambda = 1/5 = 0.2$ per minute, memorylessness says $$(T - 3) \mid \{T \gt 3\} \;\sim\; \text{Exp}(0.2), \qquad \text{so } \mathbb{E}[T-3 \mid T \gt 3] = \frac{1}{0.2} = 5 .$$ Adding back the elapsed $3$ minutes, which are not random once you condition on them, $$\mathbb{E}[T \mid T \gt 3] = 3 + 5 = 8 \text{ minutes}.$$

Method 2 — brute force from the definition. The conditional density is $f_{T\mid T\gt3}(t) = \dfrac{\lambda e^{-\lambda t}}{\mathbf{P}(T\gt3)}$ for $t \gt 3$, with $\mathbf{P}(T \gt 3) = e^{-0.6} = 0.548812$. Then $$\mathbb{E}[T\mid T\gt3] = \frac{\int_3^\infty t\,(0.2)e^{-0.2t}dt}{e^{-0.6}} .$$ Integrating by parts, $\int_3^\infty t\lambda e^{-\lambda t}dt = \big[-\big(t+\tfrac1\lambda\big)e^{-\lambda t}\big]_3^\infty = (3+5)e^{-0.6}$, so the ratio is $(3+5) = 8$ exactly — the exponential factor cancels, which is memorylessness showing up in the algebra. Simulation of $4\times10^6$ calls: $8.0026$ ✓.

Interpretation. The "inspection" effect is real: a call you find in progress at minute $3$ is expected to run $60\%$ longer in total than a call picked at random. Nothing has been forgotten — you simply selected a long call by conditioning on it.

Answer. The claim is false; $\;\mathbb{E}[T \mid T \gt 3] = 3 + 1/\lambda = 8$ minutes.

Trap. The tempting wrong answer is $5$. Memorylessness is a statement about the residual lifetime; the correct slogan is "$\mathbb{E}[\text{remaining}] = 1/\lambda$ regardless of age", never "$\mathbb{E}[\text{total}] = 1/\lambda$ regardless of age".

Q68. ★★★ · Two-branch CDF method · G3 §4.1

A calibration offset $X$ is uniform on $[-1,2]$ and the reported energy is $Y = X^2$. Find the CDF and PDF of $Y$ on the whole range, sketch-describe the density, and compute $\mathbb{E}[Y]$ two ways.

Solution

Setup. $f_X(x) = \frac{1}{2-(-1)} = \frac13$ on $[-1,2]$. The map $g(x)=x^2$ is not monotonic on $[-1,2]$ — it decreases on $[-1,0]$ and increases on $[0,2]$ — so the change-of-variables formula of §4.2 does not apply as stated. Use the CDF method (G3 §4.1): compute $\mathbf{P}(Y \le y)$ as the probability that $X$ lands in the preimage set, then differentiate. The range of $Y$ is $[0,4]$, and the asymmetry of $[-1,2]$ about $0$ splits it at $y=1$.

Step 1 — the preimage, case $0 \le y \le 1$. $\{X^2 \le y\} = \{-\sqrt y \le X \le \sqrt y\}$. Since $\sqrt y \le 1$, both endpoints lie inside $[-1,2]$, so the whole interval counts. Its length is $2\sqrt y$, and $$F_Y(y) = \frac{2\sqrt y}{3} .$$ Check: $F_Y(0)=0$ and $F_Y(1) = 2/3$. Sample: $F_Y(0.25) = 2(0.5)/3 = 0.333333$.

Step 2 — the preimage, case $1 \lt y \le 4$. Now $\sqrt y \gt 1$, so the left endpoint $-\sqrt y$ falls below the support; the interval is clipped to $[-1, \sqrt y]$ (and $\sqrt y \le 2$ keeps the right endpoint inside). Its length is $\sqrt y + 1$, giving $$F_Y(y) = \frac{\sqrt y + 1}{3} .$$ Continuity at the seam: $\frac{1+1}{3} = \frac23$, matching Step 1 ✓. At the top, $F_Y(4) = \frac{2+1}{3} = 1$ ✓. Sample: $F_Y(2) = (1.414214+1)/3 = 0.804738$.

Step 3 — differentiate on each branch. $$f_Y(y) = \frac{d}{dy}\frac{2y^{1/2}}{3} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt y} \;\;(0 \lt y \lt 1); \qquad f_Y(y) = \frac{d}{dy}\frac{y^{1/2}+1}{3} = \frac{1}{6\sqrt y} \;\;(1 \lt y \lt 4).$$ Values: $f_Y(0.25) = 0.666667$, $f_Y(0.9) = 0.351364$, $f_Y(1.1) = 0.158910$. So the density blows up like $y^{-1/2}$ at $0$ (integrably), and drops by a factor of exactly $2$ at $y=1$: for $y \lt 1$ there are two source values of $x$ (one negative, one positive) feeding each $y$, while for $y \gt 1$ only the positive branch survives. That halving is the whole content of the problem.

Step 4 — check the total mass. $$\int_0^1\frac{dy}{3\sqrt y} = \frac{2\sqrt y}{3}\Big|_0^1 = 0.666667, \qquad \int_1^4\frac{dy}{6\sqrt y} = \frac{\sqrt y}{3}\Big|_1^4 = \frac{2-1}{3} = 0.333333 ,$$ totaling $1$ ✓. (The split $\frac23:\frac13$ just re-states $\mathbf{P}(|X|\le1) = 2/3$.)

Step 5 — the mean, two ways. Directly from $f_Y$: $$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[Y] &= \int_0^1 \frac{y}{3\sqrt y}dy + \int_1^4\frac{y}{6\sqrt y}dy = \frac13\cdot\frac23 + \frac16\cdot\frac23\big(4^{3/2}-1\big)\\ &= 0.222222 + \frac{7}{9} = 0.222222+0.777778 = 1 . \end{aligned} $$ Or without any derived distribution at all, by the expected-value rule and $\operatorname{var}(X) = \frac{(2-(-1))^2}{12} = 0.75$, $\mathbb{E}[X] = 0.5$: $$\mathbb{E}[Y] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] = \operatorname{var}(X) + \big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2 = 0.75 + 0.25 = 1 \;\text{✓}$$ Simulation ($4\times10^6$): $\mathbf{P}(Y \le 1) \approx 0.666640$, $\mathbb{E}[Y]\approx0.999735$ ✓.

Answer. $\;f_Y(y) = \dfrac{1}{3\sqrt y}$ on $(0,1)$ and $\dfrac{1}{6\sqrt y}$ on $(1,4)$; $\;\mathbb{E}[Y]=1$.

Trap. Writing $f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{6\sqrt y}$ on the whole of $(0,4)$ — the answer you get from the monotonic formula $f_X(x)/|2x|$ applied blindly with one root. It gives $f_Y(0.25) = 0.333333$ instead of $0.666667$ and integrates to only $\frac{2}{6}\cdot 2 = \frac23$, missing a third of the probability. Whenever $g$ is non-monotone, count the roots of $g(x)=y$ separately on each $y$-interval.

Q69. ★★★ · Conditioning on a curved region · G3 §2.2–2.5

$(X,Y)$ is uniform on the lens-shaped region $S = \{(x,y): 0 \le x \le 1,\; x^2 \le y \le x\}$ between the parabola $y=x^2$ and the line $y=x$. (a) Find $f_{X,Y}$.   (b) Find both marginals.   (c) Find $f_{X|Y}(x\mid y)$ and $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y=y]$.   (d) Compute $\mathbb{E}[X]$ from the marginal and verify it via the total expectation theorem.

Solution

Setup. On $[0,1]$ we have $x^2 \le x$, so $S$ is nonempty and pinched shut at both $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. Uniform on $S$ means density $=1/\text{area}(S)$ (G3 §2.1).

(a) The joint density. $$\text{area}(S) = \int_0^1 (x - x^2)\,dx = \Big[\frac{x^2}{2}-\frac{x^3}{3}\Big]_0^1 = 0.5-0.333333 = 0.166667 = \tfrac16 ,$$ so $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = 6$ on $S$ and $0$ elsewhere.

(b) Marginals. Vertical slice at $x$: $y$ runs from $x^2$ to $x$, length $x-x^2$, so $$f_X(x) = 6\,(x - x^2), \qquad 0 \le x \le 1 .$$ (This is the Beta$(2,2)$ density; $f_X(0.5) = 6(0.5-0.25) = 1.5$, its peak.) Horizontal slice at $y$: the constraints $y \le x$ and $x^2 \le y$ give $y \le x \le \sqrt y$, of length $\sqrt y - y$, so $$f_Y(y) = 6\big(\sqrt y - y\big), \qquad 0 \le y \le 1 .$$ Checks: $\int_0^1 6(x-x^2)dx = 6(\frac12-\frac13)=1$ ✓ and $\int_0^1 6(\sqrt y - y)dy = 6(\frac23-\frac12)=1$ ✓. Sample: $f_Y(0.25) = 6(0.5-0.25) = 1.5$.

(c) Conditional density. For $0 \lt y \lt 1$, $$f_{X|Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)} = \frac{6}{6(\sqrt y - y)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt y - y}, \qquad y \le x \le \sqrt y ,$$ i.e. given $Y=y$, $X$ is uniform on $[\,y,\ \sqrt y\,]$ — a constant slice renormalizes to a uniform. The conditional mean is the midpoint, $$\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y=y] = \frac{y + \sqrt y}{2} .$$ At $y=0.25$: $(0.25+0.5)/2 = 0.375$; at $y = 0.81$: $(0.81+0.9)/2 = 0.855$. Notice the slice width $\sqrt y - y$ vanishes at both $y=0$ and $y=1$, which is why $f_Y$ dips to zero at both ends.

(d) $\mathbb{E}[X]$, two ways. From the marginal, $$\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_0^1 x\cdot6(x-x^2)\,dx = 6\Big(\frac13-\frac14\Big) = 6\cdot\frac{1}{12} = 0.5 .$$ By the total expectation theorem $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]$, and the algebra collapses beautifully because $(\sqrt y + y)(\sqrt y - y) = y - y^2$: $$\int_0^1 \frac{y+\sqrt y}{2}\cdot 6\big(\sqrt y - y\big)\,dy = 3\int_0^1 \big(y - y^2\big)\,dy = 3\Big(\frac12-\frac13\Big) = 3\cdot\frac16 = 0.5 \;\text{✓}$$ For contrast, $\mathbb{E}[Y] = \int_0^1 y\cdot6(\sqrt y - y)dy = 6\big(\frac25-\frac13\big) = 0.4 \lt 0.5$, as it must be since $Y \le X$ on $S$. (Rejection-sampling $3\times10^6$ points of the unit square, acceptance rate $0.166736 \approx 1/6$: $\mathbb{E}[X]\approx0.499936$, $\mathbb{E}[Y]\approx0.399917$ ✓.)

Answer. $\;f_{X,Y}=6$ on $S$; $\;f_X(x)=6(x-x^2)$, $\;f_Y(y)=6(\sqrt y - y)$; $\;X\mid Y=y \sim U[y,\sqrt y\,]$ with $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y] = \frac{y+\sqrt y}{2}$; $\;\mathbb{E}[X]=0.5$.

Trap. Getting the conditional support backwards as $[\sqrt y,\, y]$: on $(0,1)$, $\sqrt y \gt y$, so $\sqrt y$ is the upper limit. Solve the two inequalities $y \le x$ and $x^2 \le y$ separately and intersect — never guess which curve is on top.

Q70. ★★★ · Sum and difference · G3 §4.8

Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent, each uniform on $[0,1]$, and set $U = X+Y$, $V = X-Y$. (a) Show $\operatorname{cov}(U,V)=0$ and compute $\operatorname{var}(U)$, $\operatorname{var}(V)$.   (b) True or false, with reason: $U$ and $V$ are independent.   (c) State the condition under which the analogous $U,V$ would be independent.

Solution

Setup. $\operatorname{var}(X) = \operatorname{var}(Y) = \frac{(1-0)^2}{12} = 0.083333$, and $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=0$ by independence. Covariance is bilinear (G3 §4.8): $\operatorname{cov}(aX+bY,\,cX+dY) = ac\operatorname{var}(X) + bd\operatorname{var}(Y) + (ad+bc)\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)$.

(a) The computation. With $a=b=1$ and $c=1$, $d=-1$: $$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{cov}(U,V) &= (1)(1)\operatorname{var}(X) + (1)(-1)\operatorname{var}(Y) + \big((1)(-1)+(1)(1)\big)\cdot 0\\ &= \operatorname{var}(X) - \operatorname{var}(Y) = 0.083333 - 0.083333 = 0 . \end{aligned} $$ The cancellation is structural: sum and difference are uncorrelated whenever the two variables are uncorrelated and equally variable. The variances are $$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{var}(U) &= \operatorname{var}(X)+\operatorname{var}(Y) = 0.166667,\\ \operatorname{var}(V) &= \operatorname{var}(X)+(-1)^2\operatorname{var}(Y) = 0.166667 , \end{aligned} $$ both equal to $1/6$, so $\rho(U,V) = 0/(0.166667) = 0$. Simulation: sample covariance $-0.000036$ ✓.

(b) False — they are dependent. The support settles it. Since $X,Y \ge 0$, $$|V| = |X-Y| \le X+Y = U \quad\text{always},$$ so the pair $(U,V)$ is confined to the rotated square $\{|v| \le u,\ |v| \le 2-u\}$ — a diamond, not a rectangle. Knowing $U$ is small forces $V$ to be small. Concretely, $$\mathbf{P}\big(|V| \gt 0.5\big) = 0.25 \quad\text{(two corner triangles of the unit square, each of area } 0.125),$$ $$\text{but}\qquad \mathbf{P}\big(|V| \gt 0.5 \;\big|\; U \lt 0.5\big) = 0 ,$$ because $|V| \le U \lt 0.5$ on that event. The conditional probability differs from the unconditional one, so $U$ and $V$ are dependent. (Simulation confirms: $\mathbf{P}(|V|\gt0.5)\approx0.249989$; the joint event $\{|V|\gt0.5\} \cap \{U\lt0.5\}$ never occurred in $4\times10^6$ trials.)

(c) The normal exception. If instead $X,Y$ were i.i.d. $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, then $(U,V)$ would be jointly normal (a linear map of a jointly normal pair is jointly normal), and for jointly normal variables zero correlation is independence. So $U=X+Y$ and $V=X-Y$ would be genuinely independent — a fact used constantly in statistics, where it separates the sample mean from the sample spread. Uniformity is what breaks it here: the support of $(X,Y)$ is bounded, so the support of $(U,V)$ is a diamond, and no bounded non-rectangular support can host independent coordinates.

Answer. $\;\operatorname{cov}(U,V)=0$, $\;\operatorname{var}(U)=\operatorname{var}(V)=1/6$; $U,V$ are not independent; they would be if $X,Y$ were i.i.d. normal.

Trap. Concluding independence from $\operatorname{cov}=0$ plus "$U$ and $V$ look unrelated". A quick support sketch — the diamond — kills the claim faster than any integral.

Q71. ★★★ · Convolving two exponentials · G3 §4.6

A request passes through two independent stages: stage one takes $X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ and stage two takes $Y \sim \text{Exp}(2)$ (seconds; the rates differ). Let $Z = X+Y$. (a) Derive $f_Z$ by convolution.   (b) Verify it is a density and locate its maximum.   (c) Compute $\mathbf{P}(Z \gt 1)$, $\mathbb{E}[Z]$ and $\operatorname{var}(Z)$.

Solution

Setup. $f_X(x) = e^{-x}$ for $x\ge0$, $f_Y(y) = 2e^{-2y}$ for $y\ge0$. Both are supported on $[0,\infty)$, so in the convolution $f_Z(z) = \int f_X(x)f_Y(z-x)dx$ the integrand is nonzero exactly when $0 \le x \le z$ — a single finite window, and $f_Z(z)=0$ for $z \lt 0$.

(a) The convolution. For $z \ge 0$, $$f_Z(z) = \int_0^{z} e^{-x}\cdot 2e^{-2(z-x)}\,dx = 2e^{-2z}\int_0^{z} e^{-x}e^{2x}\,dx = 2e^{-2z}\int_0^z e^{x}\,dx = 2e^{-2z}\big(e^{z}-1\big),$$ where the key move was collecting the exponents: $e^{-x}e^{-2z+2x} = e^{-2z}e^{x}$. Distributing, $$\boxed{\;f_Z(z) = 2\big(e^{-z} - e^{-2z}\big), \qquad z \ge 0 .\;}$$ This is the hypoexponential density — a difference of two exponentials, not a single one.

(b) Checks and the mode. Nonnegative, since $e^{-z} \ge e^{-2z}$ for $z\ge0$ ✓. Total mass: $$\int_0^\infty 2\big(e^{-z}-e^{-2z}\big)dz = 2\Big(1 - \frac12\Big) = 1 \;\text{✓}$$ Also $f_Z(0) = 2(1-1) = 0$: the density starts at zero, unlike either exponential factor. Setting the derivative to zero, $$f_Z'(z) = 2\big(-e^{-z}+2e^{-2z}\big) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2e^{-2z} = e^{-z} \;\Longrightarrow\; e^{-z} = \tfrac12 \;\Longrightarrow\; z^\star = \ln 2 = 0.693147 ,$$ with peak height $f_Z(\ln2) = 2(0.5 - 0.25) = 0.5$. So the sum has a genuine interior mode, whereas each summand peaks at $0$ — the shape signature of a sum of stages.

(c) Numbers. $$\mathbf{P}(Z \gt 1) = \int_1^\infty 2\big(e^{-z}-e^{-2z}\big)dz = 2\Big(e^{-1} - \frac{e^{-2}}{2}\Big) = 2e^{-1} - e^{-2} = 0.735759 - 0.135335 = 0.600424 .$$ By linearity and independence, $$\mathbb{E}[Z] = \frac11 + \frac12 = 1.5 \text{ s}, \qquad \operatorname{var}(Z) = \frac{1}{1^2} + \frac{1}{2^2} = 1 + 0.25 = 1.25 .$$ (Direct integration of $\int_0^\infty z f_Z(z)dz$ returns $1.5$ ✓.) Simulation of $4\times10^6$ requests: $\mathbb{E}[Z]\approx1.499827$, $\operatorname{var}\approx1.250191$, $\mathbf{P}(Z\gt1)\approx0.600394$ ✓.

Answer. $\;f_Z(z) = 2(e^{-z}-e^{-2z})$ for $z \ge 0$; mode at $\ln 2 \approx 0.693147$; $\;\mathbf{P}(Z\gt1) = 2e^{-1}-e^{-2} = 0.600424$, $\;\mathbb{E}[Z]=1.5$, $\;\operatorname{var}(Z)=1.25$.

Trap. Reaching for the Erlang formula $f_Z(z) = \lambda^2 z e^{-\lambda z}$, which would give $f_Z(1) = e^{-1} = 0.367879$ against the true $0.465088$. That formula is only valid when the rates are equal; with $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2$ you must convolve, and the correct density degenerates to the Erlang only in the limit $\lambda_2 \to \lambda_1$.

Tool: G3 §4.6 (continuous convolution), §1.5 (exponential) · B&T §4.1

Q72. ★★★ · Censoring creates an atom · G3 §1.4–1.5

A test is run on a component whose lifetime $X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ (in years), but the test is stopped at $2$ years, so the recorded value is $Y = \min\{X, 2\}$. (a) Find $F_Y$ and classify $Y$.   (b) Compute $\mathbb{E}[Y]$ from first principles and verify it against the identity $\mathbb{E}[\min\{X,c\}] = \frac{1-e^{-\lambda c}}{\lambda}$.   (c) Compute $\operatorname{var}(Y)$ and compare with $\operatorname{var}(X)=1$.

Solution

(a) The CDF. For $0 \le y \lt 2$, the event $\{Y \le y\}$ is just $\{X \le y\}$ (capping at $2$ cannot pull a value below $2$), so $$F_Y(y) = 1 - e^{-y}, \qquad 0 \le y \lt 2 ,$$ and $F_Y(y) = 1$ for $y \ge 2$, $F_Y(y)=0$ for $y \lt 0$. Since $\lim_{y\uparrow2} F_Y(y) = 1-e^{-2} = 1-0.135335 = 0.864665$, the CDF jumps by $e^{-2} = 0.135335$ at $y=2$. So $Y$ is a mixed random variable: a continuous part with density $f(y)=e^{-y}$ on $(0,2)$ carrying mass $0.864665$, plus a point mass $\mathbf{P}(Y=2) = \mathbf{P}(X \gt 2) = e^{-2} = 0.135335$ — that atom is precisely the probability the component outlived the test. Sample: $F_Y(1) = 1-e^{-1} = 0.632121$.

(b) The mean. Split into the density part and the atom: $$\mathbb{E}[Y] = \int_0^{2} y\,e^{-y}\,dy \;+\; 2\cdot\mathbf{P}(Y=2) .$$ Integrating by parts, $\int_0^2 y e^{-y}dy = \big[-(y+1)e^{-y}\big]_0^2 = 1 - 3e^{-2} = 1 - 0.406006 = 0.593994$. The atom contributes $2 \times 0.135335 = 0.270671$. Hence $$\mathbb{E}[Y] = 0.593994 + 0.270671 = 0.864665 \text{ years}.$$ Identity check with $\lambda=1$, $c=2$: $\frac{1-e^{-2}}{1} = 0.864665$ ✓ — and indeed the tail formula $\mathbb{E}[Y] = \int_0^\infty \mathbf{P}(Y \gt y)dy = \int_0^2 e^{-y}dy = 1-e^{-2}$ gives it in one line, which is the elegant route. Simulation: $0.864566$ ✓.

(c) The variance. Same split for the second moment, with $\int_0^2 y^2e^{-y}dy = \big[-(y^2+2y+2)e^{-y}\big]_0^2 = 2 - 10e^{-2} = 2-1.353353 = 0.646647$: $$\mathbb{E}[Y^2] = 0.646647 + 2^2(0.135335) = 0.646647 + 0.541341 = 1.187988 ,$$ $$\operatorname{var}(Y) = 1.187988 - (0.864665)^2 = 1.187988 - 0.747645 = 0.440343 .$$ Simulation: $0.440286$ ✓. This is well under $\operatorname{var}(X) = 1$: censoring truncates the long right tail, which is exactly where an exponential's variance comes from. The general lesson — capped measurements always look less variable and shorter-lived than the truth — is the reason survival analysis treats censored observations specially rather than as ordinary data points.

Answer. $\;F_Y(y) = 1-e^{-y}$ on $[0,2)$ with a jump of $e^{-2}=0.135335$ at $y=2$ ($Y$ is mixed); $\;\mathbb{E}[Y] = 1-e^{-2} = 0.864665$; $\;\operatorname{var}(Y) = 0.440343 \lt 1$.

Trap. Reporting $\mathbb{E}[Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] = 1$ because "the exponential has mean $1$ and $2$ is a long way out". The cap costs $13.5\%$ of the probability its entire tail, worth $0.135$ years of mean. Equally wrong: treating $Y$ as continuous on $[0,2]$ with density $e^{-y}$ renormalized — that would give $\mathbb{E}[Y] = 0.593994/0.864665 = 0.687$, discarding the atom instead of placing it at $2$.

Tool: G3 §1.4 (CDFs and mixed distributions), §1.5 (exponential tail) · B&T §3.2–3.3
