§5   Synthesis: the discrete toolbox

Sources: L05 slides 2–8 · L06 slides 2–8 · L07 slides 2–8 · rec05 P1–P4 · rec06 P1–P3 · rec07 P1–P3 · B&T §2.1–2.7. Every number on this page is recomputed in computes/g2_s5.py (closed forms are checked against brute-force summation over the PMF).

Three lectures replaced outcomes by numbers. The replacement bought two things that G1 could not express: a single object, the PMF, that carries a whole table of probabilities at once, and two summary numbers, the mean and the variance, that compress that table into a location and a spread. Everything else in G2 — conditional PMFs, joint PMFs, independence, indicators, total expectation — is machinery for computing those two numbers without ever writing the full table down. This section puts the whole toolbox on one page, lists the traps that generate most of the wrong answers, and says precisely what breaks when the values stop being discrete.

5.1 How the pieces fit together

Read the map below as a dependency graph: an arrow means "the target is defined in terms of, or derived from, the source". Three chains descend from the PMF. The left chain is conditioning — a conditional PMF is just a PMF on a smaller universe, and summing conditional expectations against the weights of a partition is the total expectation theorem. The middle chain is averaging — expectation, then the expected-value rule that lets you average any function of $X$ without finding its PMF, then variance as the special case $g(x)=(x-\mathbb{E}[X])^2$. The right chain is multiplicity — joint PMFs, the independence test, and the linearity/indicator machinery that makes sums tractable.

Fig. 5.1 — Dependency map of the G2 toolbox (L05 slides 2–8; L06 slides 5, 7, 8; L07 slides 3–7). Solid arrows: definition or derivation. The bottom box is the G3 preview of §5.5.
Interpretation — there are only three moves. The first is partition and add: $p_X(x)$ itself is built by collecting all outcomes with $X(\omega)=x$ and adding their probabilities (L05 slide 4), marginalization $p_X(x)=\sum_y p_{X,Y}(x,y)$ adds over a partition by the value of $Y$ (L06 slide 8), and total expectation adds conditional means over a partition of $\Omega$ (L06 slide 7). The second is reweight without rebuilding: the expected-value rule $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]=\sum_x g(x)p_X(x)$ says you may leave the PMF of $X$ alone and change only the numbers you multiply it by (L05 slide 7) — that single trick delivers second moments, variances, and every "average cost/time/profit" question in the recitations. The third is decompose into indicators: write $X=\sum_i X_i$ with each $X_i\in\{0,1\}$ and use linearity (L07 slides 6–7). If a G2 problem feels hard, you are almost certainly missing one of these three moves.
Practice 5.1 (name the move). For each task, say which of the three moves — partition and add, reweight without rebuilding, decompose into indicators — does the work, and write the one identity you would invoke.
  1. You have $p_X$ for a die-like $X$ and want $\mathbb{E}[3X^2-2X+1]$.
  2. $400$ students have independent uniform birthdays; you want the expected number of days of the year on which at least one birthday falls.
  3. A fair coin decides whether $X$ is uniform on $\{1,\ldots,4\}$ or geometric($1/5$); you want $\mathbb{E}[X]$.
  4. You have $p_{X,Y}$ and want $p_Y$.
Solution

1. Reweight without rebuilding. The expected-value rule $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]=\sum_x g(x)p_X(x)$ with $g(x)=3x^2-2x+1$ (L05 slide 7). You never need the PMF of $3X^2-2X+1$. Linearity then lets you split it further as $3\mathbb{E}[X^2]-2\mathbb{E}[X]+1$ if you prefer.

2. Decompose into indicators. Let $D_d=1$ if day $d$ has at least one birthday. Then the count is $\sum_{d=1}^{365}D_d$ and $\mathbb{E}[\text{count}]=\sum_d\mathbf{P}(D_d=1)$ (L07 slides 6–7). The $D_d$ are dependent — there are only $400$ birthdays to go around — and linearity does not care.

3. Partition and add. Total expectation over the partition $\{$heads, tails$\}$: $\mathbb{E}[X]=\tfrac12\mathbb{E}[X\mid\text{heads}]+\tfrac12\mathbb{E}[X\mid\text{tails}]$ (L06 slide 7).

4. Partition and add. Marginalization $p_Y(y)=\sum_x p_{X,Y}(x,y)$ partitions the event $\{Y=y\}$ by the value of $X$ (L06 slide 8).

Practice 5.2 (the middle chain, end to end). $X$ is the number of heads in three tosses of a fair coin. Using only the expected-value rule and the computational form of the variance, find $\mathbb{E}[X]$, $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)$, then check the last against the binomial formula in §5.2.
Solution

$X\sim$ binomial($3,\tfrac12$), so $p_X(0)=p_X(3)=\tfrac18$ and $p_X(1)=p_X(2)=\tfrac38$ (these sum to $1$). The definition gives $$\mathbb{E}[X]=0\cdot\tfrac18+1\cdot\tfrac38+2\cdot\tfrac38+3\cdot\tfrac18=\tfrac{0+3+6+3}{8}=\tfrac{12}{8}=\tfrac32 .$$ The expected-value rule with $g(x)=x^2$ reuses that same PMF — no new PMF is built: $$\mathbb{E}[X^2]=0\cdot\tfrac18+1\cdot\tfrac38+4\cdot\tfrac38+9\cdot\tfrac18=\tfrac{0+3+12+9}{8}=\tfrac{24}{8}=3 .$$ Then $\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}[X])^2=3-\tfrac94=\tfrac34=0.75$, and the table's $np(1-p)=3\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac34$ agrees. (Compute key practice.p5_1b.)

Four families cover almost every discrete model in this course. In the table, $p$ is the success probability of a single trial, $n$ is the number of trials, and $a,b$ are the smallest and largest values of the uniform range (all integers between them are included). The "mechanism" column is the sentence in the problem statement that should make you reach for that row.

Interpretation — the zoo is two mechanisms, not four. Bernoulli is the atom. Binomial and geometric are the two different questions you can ask about a stream of Bernoulli trials: fix the number of trials and count the successes (binomial), or fix the number of successes at one and count the trials (geometric). That is why their means point in opposite directions: more trials raises $np$, whereas a larger success probability lowers $1/p$. The uniform is the odd one out — it encodes ignorance rather than a trial mechanism, and it is the only one of the four whose variance does not involve $p$ at all.
Interpretation — where the variances come from. $p(1-p)$ is maximized at $p=1/2$ (value $0.25$) and vanishes at $p=0$ and $p=1$: a coin you already know the answer to has no spread. The binomial variance is $n$ copies of that, $np(1-p)$, because a binomial random variable is a sum of $n$ independent indicators and variances of independent random variables add (L07 slide 5). The geometric variance $(1-p)/p^2$ blows up as $p\to 0$ far faster than its mean $1/p$ does — for $p=0.2$ the mean is $5$ but the standard deviation is $\sqrt{20}\approx 4.47$, i.e. almost as large as the mean itself. Rare-event waiting times are wildly unpredictable, not merely long.
Recipe — picking the tool.
  1. Is the quantity a number attached to each outcome? If not, it is an event and G1 already handles it. If yes, name it $X$ and say in words what $X(\omega)$ is.
  2. Does it come from one of the four mechanisms? one trial $\to$ Bernoulli; nothing prefers one value $\to$ uniform; count successes in $n$ fixed trials $\to$ binomial; wait for the first success $\to$ geometric. Then read the mean and variance off the table and stop.
  3. If no family fits, build the PMF (L05 slide 4): collect the outcomes with $X(\omega)=x$, add their probabilities, repeat for every $x$, then check $\sum_x p_X(x)=1$.
  4. Then answer the actual question. A mean of a function of $X$ $\to$ the expected-value rule, never a new PMF. A mean of a sum $\to$ linearity and indicators, and note that independence is not required. A spread $\to$ $\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}[X])^2$. A mean you can only compute inside each scenario $\to$ total expectation.

Practice 5.3 (identify the family). For each verbal setup, name the family and its parameters, then read off $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)$ from the table above.
  1. A machine stamps out parts; each is defective with probability $0.02$, independently of the others. $X$ = number of defectives in a batch of $50$.
  2. You half-remember a phone number and try a guess; each attempt is correct with probability $1/4$, independently. $X$ = number of attempts up to and including the first correct one.
  3. A fair $12$-sided die is rolled once and $X$ is the face shown.
  4. One student is picked at random from a class of $30$ that contains $12$ women; $X=1$ if the student picked is a woman and $X=0$ otherwise.
Solution

1. Binomial($50,0.02$) — the mechanism is "count the successes in a fixed number of independent identical trials". $\mathbb{E}[X]=np=50(0.02)=1$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)=np(1-p)=50(0.02)(0.98)=0.98$.

2. Geometric($1/4$) — "wait for the first success", with the number of trials unbounded. $\mathbb{E}[X]=1/p=4$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)=(1-p)/p^2=(3/4)/(1/16)=12$. Note the standard deviation $\sqrt{12}\approx 3.46$ is nearly as big as the mean, as always for a geometric.

3. Uniform on $\{1,\ldots,12\}$ — "fair die", nothing prefers one value. With $a=1,b=12$, $\mathbb{E}[X]=(a+b)/2=13/2=6.5$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)=(b-a)(b-a+2)/12=(11)(13)/12=143/12\approx 11.916667$.

4. Bernoulli($0.4$) — one trial with two outcomes; $X$ is the indicator of the event "a woman is picked", whose probability is $12/30=0.4$. So $\mathbb{E}[X]=p=0.4$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)=p(1-p)=0.4(0.6)=0.24$. (All four in compute key practice.p5_2a.)

Practice 5.4 (when no family fits). Five cards are dealt from a well-shuffled $52$-card deck, without replacement. Let $X$ be the number of aces among them. Is $X$ binomial? What is $\mathbb{E}[X]$?
Solution

Not binomial. Step 2 of the recipe fails: the five draws are trials with two outcomes, but they are not independent — drawing an ace first lowers the chance of an ace second, from $4/52$ to $3/51$. The true PMF is hypergeometric.

The mean is still easy, because linearity needs no independence. Let $X_i=1$ if the $i$th card dealt is an ace. By symmetry each single card is equally likely to be any of the $52$, so $\mathbf{P}(X_i=1)=4/52=1/13$ for every $i$ — including the fifth. Then $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{i=1}^{5}\mathbf{P}(X_i=1)=5\cdot\frac{1}{13}=\frac{5}{13}\approx 0.384615,$$ exactly what binomial($5,1/13$) would have said. The variance is where the difference shows up: the true value is $940/2873\approx 0.327184$, smaller than the binomial answer $5\cdot\frac1{13}\cdot\frac{12}{13}\approx 0.355030$, because sampling without replacement makes the indicators negatively dependent. This is the §5.3 fault line in miniature: means transfer, variances of sums do not. (Compute key practice.p5_2b, checked against the exact hypergeometric PMF.)

Interpretation — the fault line runs through the middle of the table. Notice that every expectation identity except the product rule holds without any independence assumption, while every variance-of-a-sum identity needs it. That asymmetry is the single most exploitable fact in the subject. It is why the hat problem (L07 slides 7–8) can compute $\mathbb{E}[X]=1$ in one line by linearity even though the indicators $X_i$ are strongly dependent, and it is why computing $\operatorname{var}(X)=1$ in that same problem took a whole extra slide of work with the cross terms $\mathbb{E}[X_iX_j]$ (worked in full in Example 5.1 below). Whenever a problem asks for a mean of a sum, look for indicators before you look for a PMF.
Example 5.1 (L07 slides 7–8 — the hat problem, worked in full). $n\ge 2$ people throw their hats into a box, the hats are mixed, and each person then draws one hat at random — formally, the assignment of hats to people is a uniformly random permutation of the $n$ hats. Let $X$ be the number of people who get their own hat back. Find $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)$. Both slides leave every step blank; the derivation below fills them, and every value is recomputed in computes/g2_s5.py (keys hat, hat_ingredients) at $n=3,5,10,100$.

Step 1 — decompose into indicators. Writing the PMF of $X$ directly is a derangement count and is genuinely hard; the indicator method avoids it entirely. For $i=1,\ldots,n$ set $$X_i=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if person }i\text{ draws his own hat},\\ 0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases} \qquad\text{so that}\qquad X=X_1+X_2+\cdots+X_n,$$ because adding a $1$ for each person who succeeds is exactly counting the successes.

Step 2 — the single-indicator probability. Person $i$ is equally likely to end up with any one of the $n$ hats (symmetry: the permutation is uniform, so every hat is equally likely to land on person $i$). Exactly one of those $n$ hats is his own, so $$\mathbf{P}(X_i=1)=\frac{1}{n},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad \mathbb{E}[X_i]=1\cdot\frac1n+0\cdot\Big(1-\frac1n\Big)=\frac1n,$$ using the definition of expectation on a two-value PMF. (At $n=3$ this is $1/3$; at $n=100$ it is $1/100$.)

Step 3 — the mean, by linearity alone. The $X_i$ are not independent: given that person $1$ took his own hat, person $2$ now draws from $n-1$ hats, so $\mathbf{P}(X_2=1\mid X_1=1)=1/(n-1)\neq 1/n$. Linearity does not care, since it holds for any random variables on the same sample space (L07 slide 4): $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_i]=n\cdot\frac1n=1.$$ On average exactly one person gets his own hat, whatever $n$ is.

Step 4 — expand the square. Use the computational form $\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}[X])^2=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-1$ (L05 slide 8, with $\mathbb{E}[X]=1$ from step 3). Multiplying out the sum and splitting the $n^2$ products into the $n$ with $i=j$ and the rest, $$X^2=\Big(\sum_i X_i\Big)^2=\sum_{i}X_i^2+\sum_{i\neq j}X_iX_j .$$

Step 5 — the diagonal terms. Each $X_i$ takes only the values $0$ and $1$, and $0^2=0$, $1^2=1$, so $X_i^2=X_i$ identically. Hence $\mathbb{E}[X_i^2]=\mathbb{E}[X_i]=1/n$, and the $n$ diagonal terms contribute $$\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[X_i^2]=n\cdot\frac1n=1 .$$

Step 6 — the cross terms. For $i\neq j$ the product $X_iX_j$ is again a $0$–$1$ variable: it equals $1$ exactly when persons $i$ and $j$ both get their own hats. So its expectation is that event's probability, and the multiplication rule of G1 gives $$\mathbb{E}[X_iX_j]=\mathbf{P}(X_i=1,\,X_j=1)=\mathbf{P}(X_i=1)\,\mathbf{P}(X_j=1\mid X_i=1) =\frac1n\cdot\frac{1}{n-1}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)},$$ the conditional factor being $1/(n-1)$ because once person $i$ has removed his own hat, person $j$ is equally likely to hold any of the remaining $n-1$ hats, one of which is his. Note this is larger than $\frac1n\cdot\frac1n$, the value independence would have predicted.

Step 7 — count the cross terms and add. The index pairs $(i,j)$ with $i\neq j$ are ordered: $n$ choices for $i$ and $n-1$ remaining choices for $j$, so there are $n(n-1)$ of them. Every one has the same expectation, so by linearity $$\mathbb{E}[X^2]=\underbrace{n\cdot\frac1n}_{\text{diagonal}} +\underbrace{n(n-1)\cdot\frac{1}{n(n-1)}}_{\text{cross terms}}=1+1=2 .$$

Step 8 — the variance. $$\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}[X])^2=2-1^2=1 .$$ Both the mean and the variance are $1$ for every $n\ge 2$ — the count of matches neither grows nor spreads as the crowd gets larger. Each factor of $n$ in a count is cancelled by a factor of $1/n$ in a probability, twice over in step 7.

Why this needed a whole extra slide. Step 3 was one line because linearity is free. Steps 4–7 were long because $\operatorname{var}(X)=\sum_i\operatorname{var}(X_i)$ is illegal here: the $X_i$ are dependent, and that rule would have given $n\cdot\frac1n(1-\frac1n)=1-\frac1n$, which is wrong for every finite $n$ (it says $2/3$ at $n=3$). The cross terms are exactly the correction that the illegal shortcut throws away.

Practice 5.5 (what can you compute?). You are told only that $\mathbb{E}[X]=2$, $\operatorname{var}(X)=3$, $\mathbb{E}[Y]=-1$, $\operatorname{var}(Y)=5$, and that $X$ and $Y$ are dependent in an unspecified way. Which of the following can you evaluate, and which need information you do not have? (a) $\mathbb{E}[X+Y]$; (b) $\mathbb{E}[3X-2Y+4]$; (c) $\operatorname{var}(X+Y)$; (d) $\mathbb{E}[XY]$; (e) $\operatorname{var}(2X+1)$; (f) $\sigma_X$.
Solution

(a) Computable: $1$. Linearity of expectation is in the "no" column — $\mathbb{E}[X+Y]=\mathbb{E}[X]+\mathbb{E}[Y]=2+(-1)=1$.

(b) Computable: $12$. Same row, plus the constant and scaling rows: $3(2)-2(-1)+4=6+2+4=12$.

(c) Not computable. The variance-of-a-sum row is in the "yes" column. In general $\operatorname{var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{var}(X)+\operatorname{var}(Y)+2\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)$, and you were given no covariance. All you can say is that it lies somewhere in $[(\sqrt5-\sqrt3)^2,(\sqrt5+\sqrt3)^2]$; it equals $8$ only if the covariance happens to vanish.

(d) Not computable. $\mathbb{E}[XY]=\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]=-2$ requires independence, which is explicitly denied.

(e) Computable: $12$. $\operatorname{var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{var}(X)=4(3)=12$; the $+1$ is invisible to the variance.

(f) Computable: $\sqrt3\approx 1.732051$. The standard deviation is a function of $\operatorname{var}(X)$ alone.

The pattern: everything involving one variable at a time, or a sum inside an expectation, is free; everything involving a product or a variance of a sum needs the joint law. (Compute key practice.p5_3a.)

Practice 5.6 (memorylessness plus an identity). $X$ is geometric with $p=1/5$. Find $\mathbb{E}[X\mid X>3]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X\mid X>3)$ without summing a series.
Solution

By the memorylessness row of the table, conditioned on $\{X>3\}$ the variable $X-3$ is again geometric($1/5$): the three failed trials are wasted and you are back where you started (L06 slide 6). Hence $\mathbb{E}[X-3\mid X>3]=1/p=5$, and by the shifting rule for expectations $$\mathbb{E}[X\mid X>3]=3+\mathbb{E}[X-3\mid X>3]=3+5=8 .$$ For the variance, the shift-invariance row gives $$\operatorname{var}(X\mid X>3)=\operatorname{var}(X-3\mid X>3)=\frac{1-p}{p^2}=\frac{4/5}{1/25}=20,$$ unchanged from the unconditional $20$. Conditioning on a delay moves the mean but not the spread. (Both checked by brute-force summation over the conditional PMF in compute key practice.p5_3b.)

5.4 Top gotchas of G2

These six errors account for most of the lost points on discrete random variables. Each is a place where two objects that read the same in English are different in the formalism.

Gotcha 1 — $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]\neq g(\mathbb{E}[X])$. The expectation operator passes through linear functions and nothing else (L05 slide 7). The lecture's own counterexample is the 200-mile trip at random speed $V$ with $\mathbf{P}(V=1)=\mathbf{P}(V=200)=1/2$ (L06 slides 3–4). The travel time is $T=200/V$, so $$\mathbb{E}[T]=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{200}{1}+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{200}{200}=\tfrac12(200)+\tfrac12(1)=100.5\ \text{hours},$$ whereas $200/\mathbb{E}[V]=200/100.5=1.990050$ hours — the two answers differ by a factor of about $50$. Half the time you crawl for 200 hours, and that half alone contributes $100$ to the average. The same failure with $g(x)=x^2$ is exactly the variance: for $X$ uniform on $\{1,2,3,4\}$, $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=7.5$ while $(\mathbb{E}[X])^2=6.25$, and the gap $1.25$ is $\operatorname{var}(X)$. Since $\operatorname{var}(X)\ge 0$ always, $\mathbb{E}[X^2]\ge(\mathbb{E}[X])^2$ with equality only when $X$ is a constant — the gap has a sign, and you can use it as a check.
Gotcha 2 — $X$ is not $x$, and neither is $\mathbb{E}[X]$. $X$ is a function on the sample space (L05 slide 2), $x$ is a number it might equal, and $p_X(x)$ is the probability of the event $\{\omega:X(\omega)=x\}$. Consequences: $p_X(X)$ and $\mathbf{P}(X)$ are meaningless; the summation index in $\sum_x x\,p_X(x)$ is the lower-case $x$ and the sum is over values, not outcomes; and $\mathbb{E}[X]$ is a number, not a random variable — which is why $\mathbb{E}\big[X-\mathbb{E}[X]\big]=\mathbb{E}[X]-\mathbb{E}[X]=0$ is legal (L06 slide 2) and why $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X]\big]=\mathbb{E}[X]$. Also note that $\mathbb{E}[X]$ need not be a value $X$ can take: for the speed PMF, $\mathbb{E}[V]=100.5$ but $V$ is only ever $1$ or $200$.
Gotcha 3 — variance is not linear, and does not care about shifts. The correct rules are $\operatorname{var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{var}(X)$ (L05 slide 8, L07 slide 5) — note the square, the absolute value of $a$, and the total disappearance of $b$. For binomial($10,0.3$) with $\operatorname{var}(X)=2.1$: $\operatorname{var}(-3X+7)=9\cdot 2.1=18.9$, not $-3\cdot 2.1$ and not $18.9+7$. Variance is a squared distance from the mean, so shifting the whole PMF moves the mean with it and changes nothing, while stretching by $a$ stretches every deviation by $|a|$ and every squared deviation by $a^2$. The additive rule $\operatorname{var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{var}(X)+\operatorname{var}(Y)$ requires independence and fails spectacularly otherwise (L07 slide 5): if $Y=X$ then $\operatorname{var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{var}(2X)=4\operatorname{var}(X)$, not $2\operatorname{var}(X)$; if $Y=-X$ then $X+Y\equiv 0$ and $\operatorname{var}(X+Y)=0$. On the L06 slide 8 joint PMF the dependence is mild but real: $\operatorname{var}(X)+\operatorname{var}(Y)=0.8475+0.69=1.5375$ while $\operatorname{var}(X+Y)=1.2275$, the difference $-0.31$ being twice the covariance $\mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]=6.95-7.105=-0.155$.
Gotcha 4 — independence of random variables is stronger than it looks. The definition demands $p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y)$ for every pair $(x,y)$ — the qualifier is printed in red on L07 slide 3 for a reason. One violated cell kills it, and a single zero in the joint table is usually enough: on the L06 slide 8 / L07 slide 3 table, $p_{X,Y}(1,1)=0$ while $p_X(1)=3/20>0$ and $p_Y(1)=1/20>0$, so the product is positive and independence fails immediately. As with events in G1, pairwise independence of $X,Y,Z$ does not give independence: the definition requires $p_{X,Y,Z}(x,y,z)=p_X(x)p_Y(y)p_Z(z)$ for all triples. And independence is not preserved by conditioning — restricting the table to $X\le 2$ and $Y\ge 3$ changes which cells exist and can create or destroy the factorization. Finally, matching means is not independence: $\mathbb{E}[XY]=\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$ is implied by independence but does not imply it.
Gotcha 5 — size-biased sampling: who you ask changes the answer. Four buses carry $40,33,25,50$ students, total $148$ (rec05 P3). Ask a randomly chosen driver for his bus size and you sample buses uniformly: $\mathbb{E}[Y]=\tfrac14(40+33+25+50)=37$. Ask a randomly chosen student and each bus is selected in proportion to its own size, $p_X(x)=x/148$, so $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{40^2+33^2+25^2+50^2}{148}=\frac{1600+1089+625+2500}{148}=\frac{5814}{148}=39.283784\approx 39.28.$$ The inflation $39.283784-37=2.283784$ is exactly $\operatorname{var}(Y)/\mathbb{E}[Y]$, so it vanishes only when all buses are the same size. The lesson generalizes: sampling by "pick a random member" over-represents large groups (class sizes, queue lengths, family sizes), and the two questions "how big is a typical bus" and "how big is the bus a typical student rode" have genuinely different answers. Always ask what the uniform draw is uniform over.
Gotcha 6 — expectations can be infinite, or undefined. $\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_x x\,p_X(x)$ is an infinite series when the range of $X$ is infinite, and it need not converge. In the St. Petersburg game (rec05 P4 = B&T Problem 2.21, p. 123) you toss a fair coin until the first tail; if that happens on toss $k$ you receive $2^k$ dollars. The first tail occurs on toss $k$ exactly when the first $k-1$ tosses are heads and the $k$th is a tail, so by independence $p_X(2^k)=(1/2)^{k-1}(1/2)=2^{-k}$, and $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2^k\cdot 2^{-k}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}1=\infty,$$ each term contributing exactly one dollar: the partial sum through $k=100$ is $\$100$, through $k=1000$ it is $\$1000$, and it never stops growing. So "expected gain" is not a finite number and cannot be the price you would pay; the recitation's simulations averaged only $\$19.20$ over $20$ plays and $\$11.16$ over $200$ plays, because the enormous payoffs that carry the mean occur too rarely to show up. A finite mean is a property of a PMF that you must verify, not assume — and the same warning applies to $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$, so a random variable can have a finite mean and an infinite variance.
Interpretation — a three-line self-check. Before writing a final number, ask: (i) does the PMF sum to $1$? (ii) is $\mathbb{E}[X]$ inside the range of values of $X$, and is $\operatorname{var}(X)\ge 0$ with $\mathbb{E}[X^2]\ge(\mathbb{E}[X])^2$? (iii) did I use a product or a variance-of-a-sum rule anywhere, and if so did I actually verify independence? Gotchas 1, 3 and 4 all fail one of these three checks, and each check costs a line.
Practice 5.7 (spot the error — gotchas 1–3). Each claim below is wrong. Name the gotcha, say precisely which step is illegal, and give the correct answer.
  1. "$V$ equals $1$ or $200$ mph with probability $1/2$ each and $T=200/V$. Since $\mathbb{E}[V]=100.5$, the expected travel time is $\mathbb{E}[T]=200/100.5\approx 1.990050$ hours."
  2. "$X$ is the face of a fair die and $Y=7-X$. Then $\operatorname{var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{var}(X)+\operatorname{var}(Y)=2\cdot\tfrac{35}{12}\approx 5.833333$."
  3. "For $X\sim$ binomial($10,0.3$) with $\operatorname{var}(X)=2.1$, we get $\operatorname{var}(2X-5)=2(2.1)-5=-0.8$."
Solution

1. Gotcha 1. The illegal step is pushing the expectation through the nonlinear $g(v)=200/v$. Expectation commutes with linear functions only. Correct: $\mathbb{E}[T]=\tfrac12(200/1)+\tfrac12(200/200)=100+0.5=100.5$ hours — a factor of about $50$ off the claimed answer.

2. Gotcha 3. The additive rule was applied to dependent variables; here $Y$ is a deterministic function of $X$, about as dependent as it gets. Correct: $X+Y=X+(7-X)=7$ is a constant, so $\operatorname{var}(X+Y)=0$. The claimed $5.833333$ is not merely inaccurate, it is maximally wrong. (Note $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\operatorname{var}(-X+7)=(-1)^2\operatorname{var}(X)=35/12$ is itself correct.)

3. Gotcha 3 again. Two errors: the constant $b=-5$ was carried through (variance ignores shifts) and $a=2$ was not squared. Correct: $\operatorname{var}(2X-5)=2^2\operatorname{var}(X)=4(2.1)=8.4$. A negative variance is impossible, so check (ii) of the self-check would have caught it. (Compute key practice.p5_4a.)

Practice 5.8 (spot the error — gotchas 4–6). Same instructions.
  1. "On the L06 slide 8 joint table I checked one cell, $p_{X,Y}(2,3)=p_X(2)p_Y(3)$. So $X$ and $Y$ are independent."
  2. "Four buses carry $40,33,25,50$ students. Pick a student at random; the expected size of the bus he rode is $(40+33+25+50)/4=37$."
  3. "Every PMF has a mean, so the fair price of the St. Petersburg game is $\mathbb{E}[X]$, whatever that number turns out to be."
Solution

1. Gotcha 4. Independence requires the factorization at every pair $(x,y)$, not one. One counterexample cell kills it, and this table has an easy one: $p_{X,Y}(1,1)=0$ while $p_X(1)=3/20>0$ and $p_Y(1)=1/20>0$, so the product is positive and the equality fails. A single verified cell proves nothing.

2. Gotcha 5. That computation samples buses uniformly, which is the driver's question, not the student's. A random student lands on a bus with probability proportional to its size, $p_X(x)=x/148$, giving $\mathbb{E}[X]=(40^2+33^2+25^2+50^2)/148=5814/148\approx 39.283784$. The claimed $37$ is $\mathbb{E}[Y]$, and the gap $2.283784=\operatorname{var}(Y)/\mathbb{E}[Y]$ is the size bias.

3. Gotcha 6. The premise is false: $\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_x x\,p_X(x)$ is an infinite series when the range is infinite and need not converge. Here every term is exactly $1$ dollar ($2^k\cdot 2^{-k}$), so the sum diverges and there is no finite $\mathbb{E}[X]$ to quote as a price. Finiteness of a mean is something you verify, not assume.

5.5 The bridge to G3: what breaks when the values are continuous

Everything in this section assumed $X$ takes values in a finite or countable set, so that the events $\{X=x\}$ partition $\Omega$ into pieces that can be listed and added. Suppose instead $X$ is the exact time a bus arrives, or a voltage, or a point chosen uniformly in $[0,1]$. Two things break at once.

  1. The PMF collapses to zero. By the continuous uniform law of G1 (L01 slide 10), $\mathbf{P}(X=x)=0$ for every single $x$ in $[0,1]$: a point has zero length. So $p_X(x)\equiv 0$, and it carries no information at all — yet $X$ is certainly somewhere. The object $p_X$ has to be replaced.
  2. The sum has nothing to run over. $\sum_x x\,p_X(x)$ is a sum over the values with positive mass, and there are none. Uncountably many terms cannot be added.

The repair is the same one calculus always makes: replace mass by density and sums by integrals. Chop $[0,1]$ into $m$ equal bins of width $1/m$ and put mass $1/m$ at each bin's midpoint. The mass per bin $\to 0$, but the mass per unit length stays at $\frac{1/m}{1/m}=1$ for every $m$ — that ratio is the density, and it survives the limit while the mass does not. The moments of the discretized version converge to the continuous answers (computed in computes/g2_s5.py):

Definition (preview, G3). A random variable $X$ is continuous if there is a nonnegative function $f_X$, its probability density function (PDF), with $$\mathbf{P}(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx\quad\text{for all }a\le b,\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(x)\,dx=1,$$ and then $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,f_X(x)\,dx,\qquad \mathbb{E}[g(X)]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x)\,f_X(x)\,dx,\qquad \operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}[X])^2.$$ Every entry of the table in §5.3 survives with $\sum_x\rightarrow\int dx$ and $p_X\rightarrow f_X$.
Gotcha — the density is not a probability. $p_X(x)$ was a probability, so $0\le p_X(x)\le 1$. A density $f_X(x)$ is a probability per unit length and may exceed $1$: the uniform density on $[0,\tfrac12]$ equals $2$ everywhere on that interval, and $\int_0^{1/2}2\,dx=1$ is still fine. Only the integral is a probability. Two further discrete habits that do not transfer: $\mathbf{P}(X=x)=0$ in the continuous case, so $\le$ and $<$ may be interchanged freely inside probabilities (they may not for discrete $X$); and $\mathbf{P}(X=x\mid Y=y)$ cannot be defined by the G1 formula because the conditioning event has probability zero, which is why G3 has to redefine conditioning from scratch.
Interpretation — what carries over unchanged. All the structural results of G2 are statements about expectations, not about PMFs, and expectations survive the translation verbatim: linearity, $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]\neq g(\mathbb{E}[X])$, the variance identities, independence implying $\mathbb{E}[XY]=\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$ and additive variances, total expectation over a partition, and the indicator method — indicators are still discrete even when $X$ is not, which is exactly why the trick keeps working. What changes is only the bookkeeping used to evaluate them. That is the reason this section is organized around the identity table rather than the zoo: the zoo is discrete-specific, the identities are forever.
Practice 5.9 (read the limit table). In the discretization of the uniform on $[0,1]$ into $m$ bins, say what happens as $m\to\infty$ to (i) the mass per bin, (ii) the density, (iii) $\mathbb{E}[X]$, (iv) $\operatorname{var}(X)$, (v) $\mathbf{P}(X=0.5)$. Then explain why the variance column approaches $1/12$ from below rather than oscillating.
Solution

(i) The mass per bin is $1/m\to 0$: this is the collapse that makes the PMF useless. (ii) The density is $\frac{1/m}{1/m}=1$ for every $m$ and stays $1$ in the limit — it is the object that survives. (iii) $\mathbb{E}[X]=0.5$ exactly at every $m$, by the symmetry of the midpoints about $1/2$. (iv) $\operatorname{var}(X)\to 1/12=0.0833\ldots$. (v) $\mathbf{P}(X=0.5)$ is $1/m$ while $0.5$ is a bin midpoint and $\to 0$; in the limit every single point has probability zero.

Why from below. The discretized variance has the closed form $$\operatorname{var}_m(X)=\frac{1}{12}\Big(1-\frac{1}{m^2}\Big)=\frac1{12}-\frac{1}{12m^2},$$ verified against brute-force summation at $m=4,10,100,1000$ (compute key practice.p5_5a): $5/64=0.078125$, $33/400=0.0825$, $0.083325$, $0.08333325$. The correction $-1/(12m^2)$ is negative for every finite $m$, so the approach is monotone from below and never overshoots. Intuitively, collapsing each bin onto its midpoint removes the spread within bins — a uniform on a bin of width $1/m$ has variance $1/(12m^2)$ — and keeps only the spread between bins.

Practice 5.10 (what survives?). For each fact, say whether it holds verbatim for continuous $X$, holds after a notational change, or fails outright. (a) $\mathbb{E}[aX+bY]=a\mathbb{E}[X]+b\mathbb{E}[Y]$; (b) $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]=\sum_x g(x)p_X(x)$; (c) $\operatorname{var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{var}(X)$; (d) $0\le p_X(x)\le 1$; (e) the indicator method; (f) $\mathbf{P}(X\le c)=\mathbf{P}(X<c)$.
Solution

(a) Verbatim. Linearity is a statement about expectations, and expectations survive the translation untouched — this is the whole point of §5.5.

(b) Notational change. The rule itself survives; only the bookkeeping changes: $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)f_X(x)\,dx$, with $\sum_x\to\int dx$ and $p_X\to f_X$.

(c) Verbatim. It is derived from linearity and the definition of variance, both of which are expectation statements.

(d) Fails. The replacement object is a density, a probability per unit length, and $f_X(x)>1$ is perfectly legal — the uniform density on $[0,\tfrac12]$ equals $2$. Only $f_X\ge 0$ and $\int f_X=1$ survive.

(e) Verbatim. An indicator is a $0$–$1$ random variable no matter what $X$ is, so it stays discrete and $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=\mathbf{P}(X_i=1)$ still holds. That is exactly why the trick keeps working in G3.

(f) Holds for continuous $X$, fails for discrete $X$. This one runs the other way: $\mathbf{P}(X=c)=0$ in the continuous case makes $\le$ and $<$ interchangeable, whereas for a discrete $X$ with an atom at $c$ the two differ by $p_X(c)$.
