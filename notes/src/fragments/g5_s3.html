§3   Steady-state probabilities, balance equations, birth–death chains

Sources: L17 slides 5–8 (raster p02) · L18 slides 2–4 (raster p01) · Bertsekas–Tsitsiklis §7.3 (steady-state behavior, pp. 352–362: Examples 7.5, 7.8, 7.9 and its Figure 7.14) and B&T Problems 7.20, 7.24, 7.25. Every number on this page is recomputed in computes/g5_s3.py; every figure is generated by computes/g5_s3_figs.py.

§1 built the machinery for the short run: given the one-step transition probabilities $p_{ij}$, the $n$-step probabilities $r_{ij}(n)=\mathbf{P}(X_n=j\mid X_0=i)$ follow from the Chapman–Kolmogorov recursion. §2 sorted the states into recurrent classes and transient states and asked whether $r_{ij}(n)$ settles down at all. This section answers the settling-down question quantitatively. When the limit exists it is a number $\pi_j$ that does not remember where the chain started, and — this is the payoff — you never have to multiply matrices to find it. The $\pi_j$ are the solution of a small system of linear equations, the balance equations, which you can usually solve by hand in a few lines (L17 slide 5; B&T §7.3).

Four things happen below. First we take the limit inside the Chapman–Kolmogorov recursion and land on the balance equations, being careful about the step the slide waves through: why the balance equations alone are not enough and the normalization $\sum_j\pi_j=1$ has to be adjoined. Second we solve chains — the two-state chain of L17 slide 7, its cousin from B&T Example 7.5, and a three-state chain that exercises the general linear solve. Third we read $\pi_j$ as a long-run frequency, which is what makes the balance equations obvious rather than merely true, and which lets us answer the two probabilities left blank on L18 slide 3. Fourth we specialize to birth–death chains, where transitions move only to neighboring states: there a cut argument collapses the whole linear system into a chain of two-term equations, and the answer comes out in closed form. That closed form is the queueing formula $\pi_i=(1-\rho)\rho^{\,i}$ with load factor $\rho=p/q$, and the phone-company/Erlang-B formula of L18 slide 4.

Interpretation. The whole section is one sentence repeated in four dialects: in the long run, the rate at which probability flows into a state equals the rate at which it flows out. Written for one state at a time this is the balance equation $\pi_j=\sum_k\pi_k p_{kj}$. Written for a whole block of states at once it is the cut equation, and in a birth–death chain the cut equation has just two terms, which is why those chains are solvable in closed form.

3.1 From the $n$-step recursion to the balance equations

The recursion we are taking a limit of

L17 slide 2 restates the key recursion without re-deriving it (it was proved in L16), so we re-derive it here in full, because every step of the limiting argument leans on it. Fix a starting state $i$ and a target state $j$, and let $m$ be the number of states, labeled $1,\dots,m$. Recall $r_{ij}(n)=\mathbf{P}(X_n=j\mid X_0=i)$, the probability of sitting in $j$ exactly $n$ transitions after starting in $i$.

$$ \begin{aligned} r_{ij}(n) &=\mathbf{P}(X_n=j\mid X_0=i)\\[2pt] &=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbf{P}(X_{n-1}=k\mid X_0=i)\;\mathbf{P}(X_n=j\mid X_{n-1}=k,\,X_0=i) &&\text{total probability, conditioning on the state at time }n-1\\[2pt] &=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbf{P}(X_{n-1}=k\mid X_0=i)\;\mathbf{P}(X_n=j\mid X_{n-1}=k) &&\text{Markov property: given }X_{n-1},\text{ the past }X_0\text{ is irrelevant}\\[2pt] &=\sum_{k=1}^{m} r_{ik}(n-1)\,p_{kj} &&\text{definitions, plus time-homogeneity }\mathbf{P}(X_n=j\mid X_{n-1}=k)=p_{kj}. \end{aligned} $$

Here $k$ ranges over every state the chain could occupy one step before the end; the events $\{X_{n-1}=k\}$, $k=1,\dots,m$, are disjoint and their union is the whole sample space, which is exactly what the total probability theorem needs. (This fills the [DERIVATION GAP] flagged on L17 slide 2.)

When does $r_{ij}(n)$ converge?

Steady-State Convergence Theorem (L17 slide 5, L18 slide 2; B&T §7.3, p. 352). Consider a Markov chain whose recurrent states form a single recurrent class that is aperiodic (transient states are allowed). Then there are numbers $\pi_1,\dots,\pi_m$ such that
  1. $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{ij}(n)=\pi_j$ for every $j$, and the limit is the same for every starting state $i$;
  2. the $\pi_j$ are the unique solution of the system $$\pi_j=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\pi_k p_{kj}\ \ (j=1,\dots,m),\qquad \sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_j=1;$$
  3. $\pi_j=0$ for every transient state $j$, and $\pi_j>0$ for every recurrent state $j$.
The $m$ equations $\pi_j=\sum_k\pi_kp_{kj}$ are the balance equations; the extra equation $\sum_j\pi_j=1$ is the normalization equation. The vector $(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_m)$ is called the stationary distribution.

Both hypotheses are needed, and it is worth seeing exactly what each one rules out (B&T §7.3, p. 352; classification itself is §2's business).

  • Two or more recurrent classes. Then whether you can be in $j$ far in the future depends on which class you started in or drifted into, so $\lim_n r_{ij}(n)$ must depend on $i$. Nothing is lost: once the chain enters a recurrent class it never leaves, so you first compute the probability of ending up in each class (that is §4's absorption calculation) and then run the steady-state analysis inside each class separately.
  • A periodic recurrent class. Take the two-state chain with $p_{12}=p_{21}=1$. Starting in state 1 you are in state 1 after every even number of transitions and in state 2 after every odd number, so $$r_{11}(n)=\begin{cases}1,&n\text{ even},\\ 0,&n\text{ odd},\end{cases}$$ which has no limit. The balance equations $\pi_1=\pi_2$, $\pi_2=\pi_1$ plus $\pi_1+\pi_2=1$ still have the unique solution $\pi_1=\pi_2=1/2$ — it just is no longer a limit of $r_{ij}(n)$. It is still the long-run fraction of time in each state (§3.3), which is why periodic chains are not useless, only more delicate.

Taking the limit

Assume the two hypotheses hold, so part (a) of the theorem is available. Fix $j$ and let $n\to\infty$ in the recursion:

$$ \begin{aligned} \pi_j &=\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{ij}(n) &&\text{part (a) of the theorem}\\[2pt] &=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{m}r_{ik}(n-1)\,p_{kj} &&\text{the recursion just derived, valid for every }n\ge1\\[2pt] &=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Big(\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{ik}(n-1)\Big)p_{kj} &&\textbf{finite}\text{ sum of }m\text{ terms: the limit passes through}\\[2pt] &=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\pi_k\,p_{kj} &&\text{part (a) again, applied to each }k\ (\,n-1\to\infty\text{ too}\,). \end{aligned} $$

That is the balance equation for state $j$. Doing this for each $j=1,\dots,m$ gives all $m$ of them. Two remarks on the third line, which L17 slide 5 does silently (the [DERIVATION GAP] flagged there).

Gotcha (why "finite" is in bold). Exchanging a limit with a sum is free only when the sum has finitely many terms — each term converges, and a finite sum of convergent sequences converges to the sum of the limits. With infinitely many states the exchange can fail, and worse, the limits $\pi_j$ can all be $0$ so that no stationary distribution exists at all. §3.5 shows the canonical example: an unbounded queue with arrival rate above service rate. B&T flag this on p. 362: "in such chains, even if there is a single and aperiodic recurrent class, the chain may never reach steady state."
Gotcha (the argument is not a proof of convergence). The display above assumes $r_{ij}(n)\to\pi_j$ and then identifies what the limit must satisfy. It does not prove that the limit exists — that is the hard content of the convergence theorem, proved via the Perron–Frobenius theorem or a coupling argument (B&T outline it in the chapter problems, and note that the balance equations always have a nonnegative solution for any chain; single-class + aperiodic is what makes that solution unique and equal to the limit).

Why normalization has to be adjoined

The balance equations by themselves cannot determine $\pi$: they are homogeneous (if $\pi$ solves them so does $c\pi$ for any constant $c$), and moreover one of them is redundant. Here is the redundancy, spelled out (this is B&T Problem 7.24; it fills the second half of the L17 slide-5 [DERIVATION GAP]). Add all $m$ balance equations together:

$$ \begin{aligned} \sum_{j=1}^{m}\pi_j &=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\pi_k p_{kj} &&\text{summing the }m\text{ balance equations over }j\\ &=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\pi_k\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{kj} &&\text{swap the order of the two finite sums}\\ &=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\pi_k\cdot 1 &&\text{each row of the transition matrix sums to }1\\ &=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\pi_k . \end{aligned} $$

The result is the identity $\sum_j\pi_j=\sum_k\pi_k$, i.e. $0=0$. So the $m$ balance equations are linearly dependent: the last one is the negative of the sum of the other $m-1$, hence carries no new information. They pin $\pi$ down only up to a scale factor, and the normalization equation $\sum_j\pi_j=1$ supplies exactly the one missing piece.

Numerically, for the three-state chain of Example 3.3 below, the matrix $\mathbf{P}^{\mathsf T}-I$ of the balance system has rank $2$, not $3$, and its rows sum to the zero vector to machine precision ($\max$ entry $2.8\times10^{-17}$) — confirmed in computes/g5_s3.py section C.

Gotcha (rows versus columns). The balance equation $\pi_j=\sum_k\pi_kp_{kj}$ sums down the $j$-th column of the transition matrix, weighting each $p_{kj}$ by the probability $\pi_k$ of the state you came from. In matrix form it is $\pi=\pi P$ with $\pi$ a row vector — $\pi$ is a left eigenvector of $P$ for eigenvalue 1, not a right one. The right eigenvector for eigenvalue 1 is the all-ones column vector (rows sum to one), which tells you nothing. Solving $P\pi^{\mathsf T}=\pi^{\mathsf T}$ by mistake is the single most common arithmetic error in this material.
Property (doubly stochastic chains; authored here, straight from the balance equations). Call a chain doubly stochastic if every column of the transition matrix sums to 1 as well as every row, i.e. $\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{ij}=1$ for every $j$. Then the uniform distribution $\pi_j=1/m$ satisfies the balance equations, so it is stationary — and when the chain is a single aperiodic recurrent class, uniqueness in the convergence theorem makes it the steady state. The verification is one line: for each $j$, $$\sum_{k=1}^{m}\pi_kp_{kj}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{m}p_{kj} =\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}p_{kj}=\frac{1}{m}\cdot1=\pi_j,$$ using the column sum in the last step; and normalization holds because $\sum_{j}1/m=1$.

A symmetric transition matrix ($p_{ij}=p_{ji}$) is automatically doubly stochastic, since its columns are its rows. Take the lazy random walk on a 4-cycle: states $1,2,3,4$ sit on a circle, and each step stays put with probability $1/2$ or moves to either neighbor with probability $1/4$,

$$P=\begin{pmatrix} 0.5&0.25&0&0.25\\ 0.25&0.5&0.25&0\\ 0&0.25&0.5&0.25\\ 0.25&0&0.25&0.5\end{pmatrix}.$$

Every row and every column sums to $1$; all four states communicate around the circle, and the self-loops make the class aperiodic. So $\pi=(1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4)$ with no algebra at all — the linear solve in computes/g5_s3.py section G returns $0.25$ in every coordinate, with balance residual $0$. The moral: check the column sums before setting up the linear system; symmetric shuffles, symmetric random walks and doubly stochastic models of "no preferred state" are solved the moment you notice.

Practice 3.1. A chain on states $\{1,2,3\}$ has $p_{11}=1$ (state 1 absorbing), $p_{22}=p_{23}=1/2$, $p_{33}=p_{31}=1/2$. Does the steady-state convergence theorem apply? What is $\pi$?
Solution

From state 2 you can reach 3, from 3 you can reach 1, and from 1 you can never leave. So state 1 is the only recurrent state; $\{1\}$ is a single recurrent class, and it is aperiodic because $p_{11}=1>0$ means the chain can return to 1 in one step (a self-loop always kills periodicity). States 2 and 3 are transient. The theorem applies.

By part (c), $\pi_2=\pi_3=0$ and $\pi_1>0$; normalization then forces $\pi=(1,0,0)$. Check it against the balance equations: $\pi_1=\pi_1p_{11}+\pi_2p_{21}+\pi_3p_{31}=1\cdot1+0+0=1$ ✓; $\pi_2=\pi_2p_{22}=0$ ✓; $\pi_3=\pi_2p_{23}+\pi_3p_{33}=0$ ✓. The moral: with a unique absorbing state you do not need to solve anything — the steady state is "sit in the absorbing state".

Practice 3.2. Somebody hands you a solution $\pi=(0.6,0.9)$ of the balance equations for a two-state chain and asks whether the chain is broken. What do you tell them?
Solution

Nothing is broken. The balance equations are homogeneous and dependent, so the set of solutions is a whole line $\{c\,v: c\in\mathbb{R}\}$ through the origin; $(0.6,0.9)$ is one point on it. To get the steady-state probabilities, rescale so the entries sum to $1$: divide by $0.6+0.9=1.5$, giving $\pi=(0.4,0.6)$. Any solver that returns an unnormalized vector (an eigenvector routine, for instance) has to be followed by this division.

3.2 Solving the balance equations

Example 3.1 (L17 slide 7 and L18 slide 3 — the two-state chain; solved in full). The slide prints only the diagram: $p_{11}=0.5$, $p_{12}=0.5$, $p_{21}=0.2$, $p_{22}=0.8$. Find $\pi_1,\pi_2$. (The slide leaves the entire solution to the board; L18 slide 3 later quotes the answer $\pi_1=2/7$, $\pi_2=5/7$ with no derivation — the [UNSOLVED EXAMPLE] and [DERIVATION GAP] we now close.)
Fig. 3.1 — The two-state chain of L17 slide 7 (redrawn; the handout prints the circles empty). Row sums check: $0.5+0.5=1$ and $0.2+0.8=1$. The steady-state values derived below are printed under the states.

Step 1 — write the balance equations. There are two, one per state. For state 1, sum over every state $k$ that can feed into 1, weighting $p_{k1}$ by $\pi_k$:

$$\pi_1=\pi_1p_{11}+\pi_2p_{21}=0.5\,\pi_1+0.2\,\pi_2,$$ $$\pi_2=\pi_1p_{12}+\pi_2p_{22}=0.5\,\pi_1+0.8\,\pi_2 .$$

Step 2 — simplify, and watch them collapse into one. Subtract $0.5\pi_1$ from the first:

$$0.5\,\pi_1=0.2\,\pi_2\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \pi_1=\tfrac{0.2}{0.5}\,\pi_2=0.4\,\pi_2 .$$

Subtract $0.8\pi_2$ from the second: $0.2\pi_2=0.5\pi_1$ — the same equation. This is the dependence proved in §3.1, visible in miniature.

Step 3 — normalize. Substitute $\pi_1=0.4\pi_2$ into $\pi_1+\pi_2=1$:

$$0.4\,\pi_2+\pi_2=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1.4\,\pi_2=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi_2=\frac{1}{1.4}=\frac{10}{14}=\frac{5}{7}\approx0.714286,$$ $$\pi_1=1-\pi_2=\frac{2}{7}\approx0.285714 .$$

This matches the values printed on L18 slide 3. Verified two ways in computes/g5_s3.py section A: a numpy solve of the $2\times2$ system returns $(0.2857142857142857,\,0.7142857142857143)$, agreeing with $(2/7,5/7)$ to $0$ error, and the residual $\max_j|(\pi P)_j-\pi_j|$ is $1.1\times10^{-16}$.

Example 3.2 (B&T Example 7.5, p. 353 — the sibling chain). Same shape, different numbers: $p_{11}=0.8$, $p_{12}=0.2$, $p_{21}=0.6$, $p_{22}=0.4$. Find $\pi$.
Solution

Balance for state 1: $\pi_1=0.8\pi_1+0.6\pi_2$, i.e. $0.2\pi_1=0.6\pi_2$, i.e. $\pi_1=3\pi_2$. (Balance for state 2 gives $\pi_2=0.2\pi_1+0.4\pi_2\Rightarrow0.6\pi_2=0.2\pi_1$ — the same equation again.) Normalization: $3\pi_2+\pi_2=1$, so $\pi_2=1/4=0.25$ and $\pi_1=3/4=0.75$.

Confirmed by numpy in computes/g5_s3.py section B: $\pi=(0.75,\,0.25)$ with balance residual $2.8\times10^{-17}$ and $\pi_1/\pi_2=3.0$. Compare with Example 3.1: making the chain stickier in state 1 ($p_{11}$ up from $0.5$ to $0.8$) and quicker to leave state 2 ($p_{21}$ up from $0.2$ to $0.6$) flips the steady state from mostly-state-2 to mostly-state-1, exactly as intuition demands.

Example 3.3 (three states — the general linear solve; authored here, method of B&T §7.3). A server is up (state 1), degraded (state 2), or down (state 3), observed once an hour, with $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.7&0.2&0.1\\0.3&0.4&0.3\\0&0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}.$$ What fraction of hours is the server down in the long run?

Step 0 — check the model. Each row sums to $1$ ($0.7+0.2+0.1$, $0.3+0.4+0.3$, $0+0.5+0.5$), so $P$ is a legitimate transition matrix. All three states communicate (from 3 you reach 2, from 2 you reach 1 and 3, from 1 you reach 2 and 3), so there is a single recurrent class; it is aperiodic because $p_{11}=0.7>0$ is a self-loop. The theorem applies.

Step 1 — write the three balance equations, each summing down a column of $P$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \pi_1&=0.7\,\pi_1+0.3\,\pi_2+0\cdot\pi_3 &&\text{(column 1: }p_{11},p_{21},p_{31})\\ \pi_2&=0.2\,\pi_1+0.4\,\pi_2+0.5\,\pi_3 &&\text{(column 2)}\\ \pi_3&=0.1\,\pi_1+0.3\,\pi_2+0.5\,\pi_3 &&\text{(column 3)} \end{aligned} $$

Step 2 — drop one equation (any one) and solve the rest. We keep the first and third. From the first, $0.3\pi_1=0.3\pi_2$, hence

$$\pi_1=\pi_2 .$$

From the third, $0.5\pi_3=0.1\pi_1+0.3\pi_2$; substituting $\pi_2=\pi_1$ gives $0.5\pi_3=0.1\pi_1+0.3\pi_1=0.4\pi_1$, hence

$$\pi_3=\frac{0.4}{0.5}\,\pi_1=0.8\,\pi_1 .$$

Step 3 — normalize. $\pi_1+\pi_2+\pi_3=\pi_1+\pi_1+0.8\pi_1=2.8\pi_1=1$, so

$$\pi_1=\frac{1}{2.8}=\frac{10}{28}=\frac{5}{14},\qquad \pi_2=\frac{5}{14},\qquad \pi_3=0.8\cdot\frac{5}{14}=\frac{4}{14}=\frac{2}{7}.$$

Numerically $\pi=(0.357143,\,0.357143,\,0.285714)$; the numpy solve in computes/g5_s3.py section C agrees to $5.6\times10^{-17}$, and the balance residual is $5.6\times10^{-17}$.

Step 4 — sanity-check the dropped equation. Column 2: $0.2(5/14)+0.4(5/14)+0.5(4/14)=(1+2+2)/14=5/14=\pi_2$ ✓. The server is down $\pi_3=2/7\approx28.6\%$ of hours, about $6.86$ hours a day.

Matrix powers confirm the limit and show how fast it arrives: the second-largest eigenvalue modulus is $0.5646$, and by $n=20$ every row of $P^{20}$ already agrees with $\pi$ to four decimals whichever row you started in (row 1 is $(0.357149,\,0.357140,\,0.285711)$, row 3 is $(0.357136,\,0.357146,\,0.285718)$, both from C_r_n20_rows); by $n=40$ the agreement is to eight decimals (C_r_n40_row1 $=(0.35714286,\,0.35714286,\,0.28571429)$).

Recipe — finding steady-state probabilities.
  1. Check the row sums. Every row of $P$ must sum to 1. A typo here poisons everything after.
  2. Classify (§2). One recurrent class? Aperiodic? If there are two or more recurrent classes, do §4's absorption analysis first, then run this recipe inside each class. If the single class is periodic, the $\pi_j$ below are still the long-run frequencies but not limits of $r_{ij}(n)$.
  3. Is it a birth–death chain (transitions only to $i-1$, $i$, $i+1$)? If yes, jump to the local-balance shortcut of §3.4 — it is far less work.
  4. Otherwise write the balance equations column by column: $\pi_j=\sum_k\pi_kp_{kj}$. The coefficient of $\pi_k$ is the probability of the transition $k\to j$.
  5. Discard one balance equation (they are dependent) and adjoin $\sum_j\pi_j=1$. Solve the resulting $m\times m$ system.
  6. Verify with the equation you dropped, and check $\pi_j\ge0$ and $\sum_j\pi_j=1$.

Practice 3.3. Weather is sunny (1), cloudy (2), or rainy (3), with $$P=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&0.3&0.1\\0.2&0.5&0.3\\0.1&0.4&0.5\end{pmatrix}.$$ Find $\pi$ exactly, and say how many rainy days to expect in a 100-day stretch.
Solution

Row sums are $1$; all states communicate and $p_{11}>0$, so the theorem applies. Substitute into $(\ast\ast)$: $5(4\pi_1-2\pi_2)=\pi_1+3\pi_2$, i.e. $20\pi_1-10\pi_2=\pi_1+3\pi_2$, i.e. $19\pi_1=13\pi_2$, so $\pi_2=\frac{19}{13}\pi_1$. Then $\pi_3=4\pi_1-2\cdot\frac{19}{13}\pi_1=\frac{52-38}{13}\pi_1=\frac{14}{13}\pi_1$. Normalize: $$\pi_1\Big(1+\frac{19}{13}+\frac{14}{13}\Big)=\pi_1\cdot\frac{13+19+14}{13}=\frac{46}{13}\pi_1=1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi_1=\frac{13}{46}.$$ Hence $\pi=\left(\frac{13}{46},\frac{19}{46},\frac{14}{46}\right)=(0.282609,\,0.413043,\,0.304348)$, verified by numpy (residual $5.6\times10^{-17}$). Expected rainy days in 100: $100\pi_3=100\cdot\frac{14}{46}=30.43$ days (using the frequency interpretation of §3.3).

3.3 The frequency interpretation: what $\pi_j$ actually counts

L17 slide 6 gives the balance equation a second reading, and it is the reading worth memorizing.

Property (steady-state probabilities as expected state frequencies; L17 slide 6, B&T §7.3, p. 357). For a chain with a single aperiodic recurrent class, let $v_{ij}(n)$ be the expected number of visits to state $j$ during the first $n$ transitions, starting from $i$. Then $$\pi_j=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{v_{ij}(n)}{n}.$$ Likewise, if $q_{ijk}(n)$ is the expected number of transitions from $j$ to $k$ within the first $n$ transitions, then $$\pi_j p_{jk}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{q_{ijk}(n)}{n}.$$ In words: $\pi_j$ is the long-run fraction of time spent in $j$, and $\pi_jp_{jk}$ is the long-run frequency of the transition $j\to k$.

Read this way, the balance equation stops being an algebraic accident. Every time the chain lands in $j$, it must have arrived from somewhere. In a long run of $n$ transitions, about $n\pi_kp_{kj}$ of them are $k\to j$ moves; summing over all possible predecessors $k$ counts every arrival into $j$ exactly once, and the total must equal the number of times the chain occupies $j$, about $n\pi_j$. Dividing by $n$:

$$\underbrace{\pi_j}_{\text{frequency of being in }j} =\underbrace{\sum_k\pi_kp_{kj}}_{\text{frequency of transitions into }j}.$$

(The self-transition $j\to j$ is included in the sum, as it must be: "arriving into $j$" counts staying put.) Symmetrically, since $\sum_\ell p_{j\ell}=1$ we can also write $\pi_j=\pi_j\sum_\ell p_{j\ell}=\sum_\ell\pi_jp_{j\ell}$, the total frequency of transitions out of $j$. So the balance equation says: flow in = flow out.

Interpretation (the cost formula). The frequency reading is what makes steady state useful in engineering. If being in state $j$ costs $c_j$ per period, the long-run average cost per period is $\sum_j\pi_jc_j$; if each transition $j\to k$ costs $d_{jk}$, the long-run average is $\sum_{j,k}\pi_jp_{jk}d_{jk}$. B&T's motivating example (p. 356) is a machine that costs \$1 to repair each time it breaks: the long-run repair cost per day is just the steady-state probability of the broken state.
Interpretation (link forward to §4). $$ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{P}(X_1=1,\,X_{100}=1\mid X_0=1) &=\mathbf{P}(X_1=1\mid X_0=1)\cdot\mathbf{P}(X_{100}=1\mid X_1=1,\,X_0=1) &&\text{multiplication rule}\\ &=\mathbf{P}(X_1=1\mid X_0=1)\cdot\mathbf{P}(X_{100}=1\mid X_1=1) &&\text{Markov property}\\ &=p_{11}\cdot r_{11}(99) &&\text{time-homogeneity: 99 steps from time 1 to time 100}\\ &=0.5\times 0.285714\ldots\\ &\approx 0.5\times\tfrac{2}{7}=\tfrac{1}{7}\approx 0.142857 . \end{aligned} $$

(b) Same idea, but now the conditioning runs the other way — condition on the earlier time:

Numerically $0.1428571428571433$ again. Notice what (b) is: $\pi_1p_{12}$, the long-run frequency of the transition $1\to2$ from the property above. Gotcha. $\mathbf{P}(X_{100}=1,\,X_{101}=2)$ is not $\pi_1\cdot\pi_2=\frac{2}{7}\cdot\frac{5}{7}=\frac{10}{49}\approx0.204$. Practice 3.6. A call center is idle or busy, minute by minute, with $p_{\text{idle,busy}}=0.4$ and $p_{\text{busy,idle}}=0.1$. Each minute of busy time earns \$3; each transition from idle to busy costs \$1 in setup. What is the long-run profit per minute?

3.4 Birth–death chains and the cut (local balance) equations

Definition (birth–death chain; L17 slide 8, B&T §7.3 p. 358 and its Figure 7.14). A chain on states $\{0,1,\dots,m\}$ is a birth–death chain if in one step it can only move to an adjacent state or stay put: $$p_{i,i+1}=p_i\ \ (\text{a "birth"}),\qquad p_{i,i-1}=q_i\ \ (\text{a "death"}),\qquad p_{ii}=1-p_i-q_i,$$ with the boundary conventions $q_0=0$ and $p_m=0$ (so $p_{00}=1-p_0$ and $p_{mm}=1-q_m$), and all other $p_{ij}=0$. The state $i$ is typically a population size, a queue length, or a number of busy servers, so $p_i$ is the chance it goes up by one this period and $q_i$ the chance it goes down.
Fig. 3.5 — The birth–death chain and the cut argument (redrawn from L17 slide 8, which draws the same dashed cut; compare B&T §7.3, its Figure 7.14). Every path from the left block to the right block must use the single arc $i\to i+1$, and every path back must use $i+1\to i$ — which is what forces the two frequencies to be equal.

The cut equation, derived

L17 slide 8 asserts the cut equation from the "frequencies must balance" picture. That picture is correct and worth keeping (see below), but the equation also follows from the ordinary balance equations by a short induction, which is what the slide's [DERIVATION GAP] asks for. Here it is.

Claim. For a birth–death chain, the steady-state probabilities satisfy the local balance (or cut) equations $$\pi_i\,p_i=\pi_{i+1}\,q_{i+1},\qquad i=0,1,\dots,m-1 .$$

Base case $i=0$. The balance equation for state 0 collects the ways of arriving in 0: from 0 itself (probability $p_{00}=1-p_0$) and from 1 (probability $q_1$). No other state can reach 0 in one step. So

$$\pi_0=\pi_0(1-p_0)+\pi_1q_1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi_0-\pi_0+\pi_0p_0=\pi_1q_1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi_0p_0=\pi_1q_1 . \checkmark$$

Inductive step. Suppose $\pi_{i-1}p_{i-1}=\pi_iq_i$ holds for some $i\ge1$; we prove $\pi_ip_i=\pi_{i+1}q_{i+1}$. The balance equation for state $i$ has exactly three incoming terms — from $i-1$ by a birth, from $i$ by staying, from $i+1$ by a death:

$$\pi_i=\pi_{i-1}p_{i-1}+\pi_i\,(1-p_i-q_i)+\pi_{i+1}q_{i+1}.$$

Expand the middle term and cancel $\pi_i$ from both sides:

$$ \begin{aligned} \pi_i&=\pi_{i-1}p_{i-1}+\pi_i-\pi_ip_i-\pi_iq_i+\pi_{i+1}q_{i+1}\\ 0&=\pi_{i-1}p_{i-1}-\pi_iq_i-\pi_ip_i+\pi_{i+1}q_{i+1} &&\text{subtract }\pi_i\text{ from both sides}\\ 0&=\underbrace{(\pi_{i-1}p_{i-1}-\pi_iq_i)}_{=\,0\ \text{by the inductive hypothesis}} \;-\;\pi_ip_i+\pi_{i+1}q_{i+1}\\ \pi_ip_i&=\pi_{i+1}q_{i+1}. \checkmark \end{aligned} $$

By induction the cut equation holds for every $i$ from $0$ to $m-1$. (The balance equation for the top state $m$ is then automatically satisfied — it is the redundant one of §3.1.)

Interpretation (why the picture is the proof). Draw a vertical line between states $i$ and $i+1$, splitting the state space into the left block $\{0,\dots,i\}$ and the right block $\{i+1,\dots,m\}$ (Fig. 3.5). Because the chain moves only between neighbors, the only way to get from left to right is the single transition $i\to i+1$, whose long-run frequency is $\pi_ip_i$; the only way back is $i+1\to i$, with frequency $\pi_{i+1}q_{i+1}$. In any long stretch of $n$ steps these crossings must alternate — you cannot cross left-to-right twice in a row without crossing back in between — so the two counts differ by at most 1, and after dividing by $n$ and letting $n\to\infty$ the frequencies are equal. This is the argument on L17 slide 8; the induction above is the same statement in algebra.
Property (detailed balance; reversible chains — the general form of the cut equation, cf. B&T Problem 7.25). Suppose a distribution $\pi$ with $\pi_j\ge0$ and $\sum_j\pi_j=1$ satisfies the detailed balance equations $$\pi_i\,p_{ij}=\pi_j\,p_{ji}\qquad\text{for every pair of states }i,j.$$ Then $\pi$ is stationary. The proof is one line — sum over $i$: $$\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_ip_{ij}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\pi_jp_{ji}=\pi_j\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{ji}=\pi_j,$$ using detailed balance term by term, then the fact that row $j$ of the transition matrix sums to 1. The left-hand side is the balance equation for state $j$, so all $m$ of them hold. A chain possessing such a $\pi$ is called reversible: in steady state the joint probability $\mathbf{P}(X_n=i,\,X_{n+1}=j)=\pi_ip_{ij}$ is symmetric in $i$ and $j$, so the sequence run backwards is the same Markov chain.
Interpretation (what detailed balance buys, and where it lives in this course). Balance says that the flow into a state equals the flow out of it, netted over all the state's neighbors; detailed balance says the flow balances pair by pair, which is much stronger and much easier to solve — guess a $\pi$ satisfying it and you have solved the chain without ever writing the $m$-equation linear system. Birth–death chains are the course's standard example: for any pair that is not a neighbor pair, $p_{ij}=p_{ji}=0$ and detailed balance holds trivially, while for a neighbor pair it reads $\pi_ip_i=\pi_{i+1}q_{i+1}$ — exactly the cut equation proved above. That is why the queue of §3.5 and the phone exchange of §3.6 come out in closed form. The converse fails: being stationary does not make $\pi$ reversible, as the next Gotcha shows.
Gotcha. Local balance is not a general fact about Markov chains. It is a special feature of chains whose transition graph is a line (or, more generally, a tree, or any chain that is "reversible"). B&T Problem 7.25 asks for a chain with a single aperiodic recurrent class whose $\pi$ does not satisfy $\pi_ip_{ij}=\pi_jp_{ji}$ — a three-state cycle $1\to2\to3\to1$ with a little back-flow does the job: probability circulates around the loop, so individual pairs are not in balance even though every state is. If your chain has a $1\to2\to3\to1$ shortcut, you must solve the full linear system.

Telescoping to a closed form

The cut equations solve themselves. Rearranged, $\pi_{i+1}=\pi_i\,p_i/q_{i+1}$, so starting at $\pi_0$ and stepping up:

$$ \pi_1=\pi_0\frac{p_0}{q_1},\qquad \pi_2=\pi_1\frac{p_1}{q_2}=\pi_0\frac{p_0p_1}{q_1q_2},\qquad\dots,\qquad \boxed{\ \pi_i=\pi_0\prod_{k=1}^{i}\frac{p_{k-1}}{q_k}\ },\quad i=0,1,\dots,m, $$

with the empty product for $i=0$ read as $1$. Then $\pi_0$ is fixed by normalization:

$$\pi_0\left[1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\prod_{k=1}^{i}\frac{p_{k-1}}{q_k}\right]=1 .$$

An $m+1$-dimensional linear system has become a product and one division. That is the entire reason birth–death chains are a named special case.

3.5 The queue: constant $p$ and $q$, the load factor $\rho$, and what happens as $\rho\to1$

Specialize to the case L17 slide 8 highlights and B&T Example 7.9 models: a buffer holding at most $m$ packets, where in each period at most one thing happens — an arrival with probability $p$, a departure with probability $q$, nothing with the remaining probability. Then $p_i=p$ for $i<m$ and $q_i=q$ for $i>0$, and every ratio in the telescoping product is the same number.

Definition (load factor). $\displaystyle\rho=\frac{p}{q}$, the ratio of the arrival probability to the departure probability. It is dimensionless: the average number of arrivals per departure. $\rho<1$ means work drains faster than it arrives; $\rho>1$ means the opposite.

With $p_{k-1}/q_k=p/q=\rho$ for every $k$, the product collapses:

$$\pi_{i+1}=\pi_i\frac{p}{q}=\pi_i\rho \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \pi_i=\pi_0\,\rho^{\,i},\qquad i=0,1,\dots,m,$$

which is the formula printed on L17 slide 8. Normalization needs the finite geometric sum:

$$1=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\pi_i=\pi_0\sum_{i=0}^{m}\rho^{\,i} =\begin{cases}\pi_0\dfrac{1-\rho^{\,m+1}}{1-\rho},&\rho\neq1,\\[8pt] \pi_0\,(m+1),&\rho=1,\end{cases}$$

using $\sum_{i=0}^{m}x^i=(1-x^{m+1})/(1-x)$ for $x\neq1$ (and $m+1$ equal terms when $x=1$). Therefore

$$\boxed{\ \pi_0=\frac{1-\rho}{1-\rho^{\,m+1}}\ (\rho\neq1),\qquad \pi_0=\frac{1}{m+1}\ (\rho=1), \qquad \pi_i=\pi_0\rho^{\,i}.\ }$$

The infinite buffer, $\rho<1$

L17 slide 8 then takes $p<q$ (so $\rho<1$) and $m\approx\infty$ and asserts $\pi_0=1-\rho$ and $\mathbb{E}[X_n]=\rho/(1-\rho)$, with both derivations left off the slide. Here they are.

The normalization. With $\rho<1$ we have $\rho^{\,m+1}\to0$ as $m\to\infty$, so

$$\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\pi_i=\pi_0\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\rho^{\,i}=\frac{\pi_0}{1-\rho}=1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \pi_0=1-\rho,$$

using the infinite geometric series $\sum_{i\ge0}\rho^i=1/(1-\rho)$, which converges precisely because $\rho<1$. Hence

$$\pi_i=(1-\rho)\rho^{\,i},\qquad i=0,1,2,\dots$$

The mean. Two routes to the same answer; take whichever you find faster.

Route 1 (recognize the PMF). $\pi_i=(1-\rho)\rho^i$ for $i=0,1,2,\dots$ is the PMF of a shifted geometric random variable: if $Y$ is geometric with parameter $1-\rho$ on $\{1,2,\dots\}$, i.e. $p_Y(y)=(1-\rho)\rho^{\,y-1}$, then $X=Y-1$ has exactly this PMF. From G2, $\mathbb{E}[Y]=1/(1-\rho)$, so

$$\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[Y]-1=\frac{1}{1-\rho}-1=\frac{1-(1-\rho)}{1-\rho}=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}.$$

Route 2 (sum directly). Start from $\sum_{i\ge0}\rho^i=\frac{1}{1-\rho}$ and differentiate both sides with respect to $\rho$ (legal inside the radius of convergence):

$$\sum_{i\ge1}i\,\rho^{\,i-1}=\frac{1}{(1-\rho)^2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sum_{i\ge0}i\,\rho^{\,i}=\frac{\rho}{(1-\rho)^2}\ \ \text{(multiply by }\rho).$$

Therefore

$$\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{i\ge0}i\,\pi_i=(1-\rho)\sum_{i\ge0}i\rho^{\,i} =(1-\rho)\cdot\frac{\rho}{(1-\rho)^2}=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}. \checkmark$$

Solve $\rho/(1-\rho)=4$: $\rho=4-4\rho$, so $5\rho=4$ and $\rho=0.8$. That is the largest tolerable value, since $\rho/(1-\rho)$ is increasing in $\rho$ (its derivative $1/(1-\rho)^2$ is positive).

Empty fraction: $\pi_0=1-\rho=0.2$, i.e. 20% of periods. Tail: since $\pi_i=(1-\rho)\rho^i$, $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge5)=\sum_{i\ge5}(1-\rho)\rho^i=(1-\rho)\rho^5\sum_{j\ge0}\rho^j =(1-\rho)\rho^5\cdot\frac{1}{1-\rho}=\rho^5=0.8^5=0.32768 .$$ (The general fact worth remembering: $\mathbf{P}(X\ge k)=\rho^k$, a clean geometric tail.) At $\rho=0.9$ the same tail would be $0.9^5=0.59049$ — computed in computes/g5_s3.py section F — so nudging the load up by an eighth nearly doubles the chance of a deep backlog.

3.6 The phone company: an inhomogeneous birth–death chain (L18 slide 4)

Example 3.5 (L18 slide 4 — probability of blocked phone calls; derived in full). Calls originate as a Poisson process of rate $\lambda$. Each call's duration is exponential with parameter $\mu$, independently of everything else. The exchange has $B$ lines; a call that arrives when all $B$ are busy is blocked (lost, not queued). What fraction of calls is blocked?

Step 1 — discretize time. Chop time into slots of small length $\delta$ and let $X_n$ be the number of calls in progress at the end of slot $n$; the state space is $\{0,1,\dots,B\}$. From G4 §3, the defining small-interval property of a Poisson process gives

$$\mathbf{P}(\text{one arrival in a slot})=\lambda\delta+o(\delta),\qquad \mathbf{P}(\text{two or more arrivals})=o(\delta).$$

For departures: a call in progress has an exponential duration, which is memoryless (G3), so regardless of how long it has already been running it ends during the next $\delta$ with probability $\mu\delta+o(\delta)$. If $i$ calls are in progress, they are independent, so the probability that exactly one of them ends is $i\mu\delta+o(\delta)$ (the $i$ ways of choosing which one, each of probability $\mu\delta$, minus overlaps of order $\delta^2$). Dropping all $o(\delta)$ terms — legitimate in the limit $\delta\to0$ — we get a birth–death chain with

$$p_i=\lambda\delta\ \ (0\le i<B),\qquad q_i=i\mu\delta\ \ (1\le i\le B),\qquad p_{ii}=1-\lambda\delta-i\mu\delta .$$

This closes the first half of the L18 slide-4 [DERIVATION GAP]. Note the structure: the birth rate is constant (arrivals do not care how busy you are), while the death rate is proportional to the state (the more calls in progress, the more of them can finish).

Fig. 3.8 — The discretized phone-exchange chain (redrawn from L18 slide 4). Births happen at the constant rate $\lambda\delta$; deaths from state $i$ at rate $i\mu\delta$, because any one of the $i$ calls in progress may end.

Step 2 — local balance. The chain is birth–death, so §3.4's cut equation applies across the cut between $i-1$ and $i$:

$$\pi_{i-1}\,(\lambda\delta)=\pi_i\,(i\mu\delta) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \lambda\,\pi_{i-1}=i\mu\,\pi_i,\qquad i=1,\dots,B,$$

which is the equation printed on the slide. The slot length $\delta$ appears on both sides and cancels — the answer does not depend on how finely we chopped time, which is exactly what we needed for the discretization to be an honest model of the continuous-time system.

Step 3 — telescope. Rearranging, $\pi_i=\pi_{i-1}\lambda/(i\mu)$. Applying this repeatedly from $\pi_0$:

$$ \pi_1=\pi_0\frac{\lambda}{1\mu},\qquad \pi_2=\pi_1\frac{\lambda}{2\mu}=\pi_0\frac{\lambda^2}{2\mu^2},\qquad \pi_3=\pi_2\frac{\lambda}{3\mu}=\pi_0\frac{\lambda^3}{3\cdot2\,\mu^3},\ \dots $$ $$\pi_i=\pi_0\prod_{k=1}^{i}\frac{\lambda}{k\mu} =\pi_0\frac{\lambda^i}{\mu^i}\cdot\frac{1}{1\cdot2\cdots i} =\pi_0\frac{\lambda^i}{\mu^i\,i!},\qquad i=0,1,\dots,B,$$

the formula on the slide (second half of the [DERIVATION GAP]: the product $\prod_k 1/k$ is what produces the factorial).

Step 4 — normalize.

$$1=\sum_{i=0}^{B}\pi_i=\pi_0\sum_{i=0}^{B}\frac{\lambda^i}{\mu^i\,i!} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \pi_0=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{B}\frac{\lambda^i}{\mu^i\,i!}\right)^{-1}.$$
Interpretation (a truncated Poisson). Write $a=\lambda/\mu$, the offered load, measured in erlangs: it is the mean number of calls that would be in progress if there were unlimited lines (arrivals per unit time $\times$ mean duration). Then $\pi_i\propto a^i/i!$, which is the Poisson PMF with parameter $a$ up to the normalizing constant — the same distribution, restricted to $\{0,\dots,B\}$ and renormalized. If there were unlimited lines the normalizing constant would be $e^{-a}$ and the number of calls in progress would be exactly Poisson with parameter $a$ — the continuous-time echo of G4's Poisson results. The finite $B$ chops off the tail, and the chopped-off mass is what turns into blocking.

Step 5 — read off the blocking probability. A call is blocked exactly when it arrives to find the system in state $B$. Because arrivals come from a Poisson process, they are equally likely to fall at any instant and are independent of the current state, so the fraction of arriving calls that see state $B$ equals the fraction of time the system spends in state $B$ — which is $\pi_B$ by §3.3. Hence

$$\boxed{\ \mathbf{P}(\text{call blocked})=\pi_B =\frac{a^B/B!}{\sum_{i=0}^{B}a^i/i!},\qquad a=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}.\ }$$

This is the Erlang-B formula, the oldest formula in telephone engineering (Erlang, 1917). L18 slide 4 stops before naming it; the interpretation is the missing last line of the slide.

A worked instance. Take $\lambda=30$ calls per minute and a mean call duration of $1/\mu=0.1$ minutes ($\mu=10$ per minute), with $B=4$ lines. Then $a=\lambda/\mu=3$ erlangs, and the unnormalized weights are

$$1,\quad 3,\quad \frac{3^2}{2}=4.5,\quad \frac{3^3}{6}=4.5,\quad \frac{3^4}{24}=3.375, \qquad\text{sum }=16.375 .$$

Dividing, and clearing to a common denominator of $131$ (multiply every weight by $8$):

$$\pi=\left(\tfrac{8}{131},\,\tfrac{24}{131},\,\tfrac{36}{131},\,\tfrac{36}{131},\,\tfrac{27}{131}\right) =(0.061069,\,0.183206,\,0.274809,\,0.274809,\,0.206107).$$

So $\mathbf{P}(\text{blocked})=\pi_4=27/131=0.206107$: more than one call in five is lost. Two independent checks, both in computes/g5_s3.py section E. (i) Building the discretized $5\times5$ transition matrix with $\delta=10^{-4}$ and solving $\pi=\pi P$ numerically reproduces these five numbers to $9.2\times10^{-16}$ — confirming that $\delta$ really does cancel. (ii) The mean number of busy lines is $\sum_i i\pi_i=2.381679$, which must also equal $a\,(1-\pi_B)=3(1-0.206107)=2.381679$ (offered load minus blocked load); the two agree to $4.4\times10^{-16}$.

Gotcha. Erlang-B assumes blocked calls vanish — they are lost, not queued and not retried. That is why the chain has no state above $B$ and no waiting room. A system where blocked customers wait in line is a different chain (states $0,1,2,\dots$ unbounded, death rate $B\mu\delta$ once all servers are busy) with a different answer (the Erlang-C formula). Reading the birth and death rates off the physical description, state by state, is the modeling step that does all the work; after that the local-balance machinery is identical.

§3 cheatsheet.
  • Convergence (L17 slide 5, L18 slide 2). Single recurrent class + aperiodic $\Rightarrow r_{ij}(n)\to\pi_j$ for every $i$. Two classes $\Rightarrow$ the limit remembers $X_0$; periodic $\Rightarrow$ no limit (but the $\pi_j$ still are the long-run frequencies).
  • Balance equations. $\displaystyle\pi_j=\sum_k\pi_kp_{kj}$ for each $j$, obtained by letting $n\to\infty$ in $r_{ij}(n)=\sum_kr_{ik}(n-1)p_{kj}$ (finite sums only). They are dependent — they sum to $0=0$ — so drop one and adjoin $\displaystyle\sum_j\pi_j=1$. In matrix form $\pi=\pi P$ with $\pi$ a row vector.
  • Doubly stochastic. If every column of $P$ sums to 1 too (in particular if $P$ is symmetric), then $\pi_j=1/m$ solves the balance equations — the uniform distribution is stationary, and is the steady state when the class is single and aperiodic. Check the column sums before solving anything.
  • Frequency reading (L17 slide 6). $\pi_j$ = long-run fraction of time in $j$; $\pi_jp_{jk}$ = long-run frequency of the transition $j\to k$; balance = flow in equals flow out. Long-run average cost $=\sum_j\pi_jc_j$ (state costs) or $\sum_{j,k}\pi_jp_{jk}d_{jk}$ (transition costs). Also $t_j^*=1/\pi_j$ (§4).
  • Two-state chain (L17 slide 7 / L18 slide 3). $p_{11}=0.5,p_{12}=0.5,p_{21}=0.2,p_{22}=0.8 \Rightarrow \pi=(2/7,\,5/7)$, and both L18 slide-3 answers equal $1/7\approx0.142857$.
  • Birth–death (L17 slide 8). Local balance across each cut: $\pi_ip_i=\pi_{i+1}q_{i+1}$, hence $\displaystyle\pi_i=\pi_0\prod_{k=1}^{i}\frac{p_{k-1}}{q_k}$, with $\pi_0$ from normalization. Valid because the chain is a line — not a general fact.
  • Detailed balance / reversibility. If $\pi_ip_{ij}=\pi_jp_{ji}$ for all $i,j$ then $\pi$ is stationary (sum over $i$ and use $\sum_ip_{ji}=1$), and the chain is called reversible. Birth–death chains are the standard example — there detailed balance is the cut equation. The converse fails: stationary does not imply reversible.
  • Constant $p,q$. $\rho=p/q$; $\pi_i=\pi_0\rho^{\,i}$; $\pi_0=(1-\rho)/(1-\rho^{m+1})$ for finite $m$, $\pi_0=1/(m+1)$ when $\rho=1$. Infinite buffer with $\rho<1$: $\pi_i=(1-\rho)\rho^{\,i}$, $\mathbf{P}(X\ge k)=\rho^k$, $\mathbb{E}[X]=\rho/(1-\rho)$ — which diverges as $\rho\to1$ ($\rho=0.9\Rightarrow9$; $\rho=0.99\Rightarrow99$). No stationary distribution at all if $\rho\ge1$ and the buffer is infinite.
  • Erlang-B (L18 slide 4). Constant birth rate $\lambda\delta$, state-proportional death rate $i\mu\delta$ $\Rightarrow \lambda\pi_{i-1}=i\mu\pi_i \Rightarrow \pi_i=\pi_0a^i/i!$ with $a=\lambda/\mu$ erlangs; blocking $=\pi_B=\dfrac{a^B/B!}{\sum_{i=0}^Ba^i/i!}$ (truncated Poisson). For $a=3$, $B=4$: $\pi_B=27/131=0.2061$; $B=8$ brings it to $0.00813$.
