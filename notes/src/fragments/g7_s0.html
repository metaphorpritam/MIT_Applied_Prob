§0   Orientation: the classical turn

Sources: L23 slides 1–4, 6–7 · L24 slides 1–2 · L25 slides 1–2 · rec23 P1–P3 · rec24 P1–P2 · Bertsekas–Tsitsiklis (cited throughout as B&T) Chapter 9 preamble and §9.1 · numbers from computes/g7_s0.py. A bare "§N.k" always means this note's own subsection; every reference to the textbook carries the "B&T" prefix.

Note G6 ended by making the unknown a random variable. You wrote a prior $f_\Theta(\theta)$, you conditioned on the data, and the posterior $f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)$ was the complete answer; MAP and LMS were two ways of squeezing a number out of it. Everything worked because the Bayesian move restores a single, fully specified probabilistic model — a joint distribution of $(\Theta,X)$ — so that G1's conditioning machinery applies verbatim.

Note G7 refuses that move. The classical statistician says: the mass of the electron is not random, the fraction of voters who prefer candidate A on this particular Tuesday is not random, the true bias of this particular coin is not random. Each is a fixed number that merely happens to be unknown, and inventing a probability distribution for it is inventing information you do not have. B&T state the position in one sentence in the Chapter 9 preamble: "Classical statistics treats unknown parameters as constants to be determined. A separate probabilistic model is assumed for each possible value of the unknown parameter."

That refusal costs you the posterior, and it forces a different vocabulary — estimators, bias, consistency, confidence intervals, likelihood ratios, rejection regions. This section sets up the framework and the notation the rest of G7 runs on: what $\theta$ is, what $\hat\Theta_n$ is, what a classical probability statement is a statement about, and when you would choose this philosophy over G6's.

0.1 One parameter, many models

Definition (classical model; L23 slide 2, B&T §9.1). The observation $X$ (a single random variable, or a vector $X=(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$ of $n$ data points) has distribution $$p_X(x;\theta)\quad\text{if }X\text{ is discrete},\qquad f_X(x;\theta)\quad\text{if }X\text{ is continuous},$$ where $\theta$ is an unknown constant — the parameter — ranging over a known set of possible values. For a vector observation and a vector of $m$ parameters the model is written $p_{X_1,\ldots,X_n}(x_1,\ldots,x_n;\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_m)$ (L23 slide 2). The semicolon separates the argument $x$, which the distribution is a distribution over, from the parameter $\theta$, which merely indexes which distribution you are looking at.

Read the definition slowly, because the entire note hangs on it. There is not one probabilistic model here; there is a whole family of them, one per candidate value of $\theta$, and exactly one member of the family is the true one. B&T's picture of this — its Figure 9.1 — is a stack of distributions labelled $\theta_1,\theta_2,\ldots$, with the observed value $x$ drawn from whichever one is real. Your job is to look at $x$ and say something defensible about which member of the stack you are in.

Gotcha — the semicolon is not a conditioning bar. L23 slide 2 prints, in red, "These are NOT conditional probabilities; $\theta$ is NOT random." In G6 you wrote $f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$ and the bar was a genuine conditioning bar: it referred to a joint distribution of $(\Theta,X)$, and $\mathbf{P}(\Theta\in A\mid X=x)$ was a legitimate quantity. In G7 you write $p_X(x;\theta)$ and there is no joint distribution, because $\theta$ has no distribution. Consequently the symbols $\mathbf{P}(\theta\le 0.6)$, $f_\Theta(\theta)$, $f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)$, $\mathbb{E}[\theta]$ are all meaningless in this note — not false, but ungrammatical. If you catch yourself writing one, you have slipped back into G6.

The notation extends to everything derived from the model. B&T's convention, which this note follows: probabilities, expectations and variances computed under the model indexed by $\theta$ carry $\theta$ as a subscript or after a semicolon, as in $\mathbf{P}_\theta(A)$ or $\mathbf{P}(A;\theta)$, and $\mathbb{E}_\theta[h(X)]$ or $\mathbb{E}[h(X);\theta]$. The book adds the warning that this "only indicates a functional dependence, not [conditioning] in the probabilistic sense" (B&T §9.1). So $\mathbb{E}[h(X);\theta]$ is an ordinary expectation — a number — that happens to be a function of $\theta$.

Example 0.1 (the same data, seen classically; L23 slide 3, B&T §9.1). A coin with unknown bias $\theta\in[0,1]$ — the probability of heads on one toss — is tossed $n=10$ times and $x=7$ heads come up. The classical model is the family of binomial PMFs $$p_X(x;\theta)=\binom{10}{x}\theta^{x}(1-\theta)^{10-x},\qquad x=0,1,\ldots,10,$$ one PMF for each $\theta$. Evaluating the family at the observed $x=7$, with $\binom{10}{7}=120$: $$p_X(7;\theta)=120\,\theta^{7}(1-\theta)^{3}.$$

Four members of the family, from computes/g7_s0.py:

$\theta$$p_X(7;\theta)=120\,\theta^7(1-\theta)^3$reading
$0.3$$0.009002$this model almost never produces 7 heads
$0.5$$0.117187$the fair coin: possible, not typical
$0.7$$0.266828$the best explanation available
$0.9$$0.057396$too biased; 9 heads would be typical here

The ratios say the same thing more sharply: $0.266828/0.117187=2.2769$ and $0.266828/0.009002=29.6420$. The data are $2.2769$ times more probable under $\theta=0.7$ than under a fair coin, and $29.6420$ times more probable than under $\theta=0.3$. Choosing the $\theta$ that maximizes this number is maximum likelihood; here it is $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=x/n=0.7000$, derived properly in §2.

Gotcha — a likelihood is not a distribution over $\theta$. The column of numbers $0.009002,\,0.117187,\,0.266828,\,0.057396$ tempts you to normalize it and call the result "the probability that $\theta=0.7$". Do not. Each entry is a probability of the data under a different model; they are computed in four different probability spaces and there is nothing to add them up over. The function $\theta\mapsto p_X(7;\theta)$ does not integrate to $1$ over $\theta$ and is not a density in $\theta$. Normalizing it is legal in G6, where it amounts to Bayes' rule with a flat prior — and that is exactly the assumption a classical statistician declines to make.

0.2 The estimator is random; the estimate is a number

If $\theta$ carries all the certainty, where does the randomness live? In the data, and therefore in anything computed from the data.

Definition (estimator vs. estimate; L23 slide 2, footnote 2 of the L23 transcript). An estimator is a random variable $$\hat\Theta_n=g(X_1,\ldots,X_n),$$ a fixed function $g$ of the observations, chosen before the data arrive. Because $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ are random, $\hat\Theta_n$ is random and has a distribution — one distribution for each $\theta$, since the $X_i$ do. An estimate is the number $\hat\theta_n=g(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ you get by feeding one realized data set into $g$. Capital $\hat\Theta_n$ for the rule, lowercase $\hat\theta_n$ for the output; the subscript $n$ records the sample size. The estimation error is $\hat\Theta_n-\theta$, and the entire design problem of §1–§3 is to make it small (L23 slide 2).
Example 0.2 (two data sets, one $\theta$; L23 slide 3 exponential model). Let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be i.i.d. exponential with parameter $\theta$, so $f_{X_i}(x;\theta)=\theta e^{-\theta x}$ for $x\ge 0$ — think of the $X_i$ as service times whose unknown rate is $\theta$. §2 derives the ML estimator $$\hat\Theta_n=\frac{n}{X_1+\cdots+X_n}.$$ Take the true rate to be $\theta=0.5$ (unknown to the statistician, known to us because we are simulating) and $n=20$. Two independent data sets, generated in computes/g7_s0.py, give $$\sum_i x_i = 42.2254\ \Rightarrow\ \hat\theta_{20}=\frac{20}{42.2254}=0.4736, \qquad \sum_i x_i = 27.2869\ \Rightarrow\ \hat\theta_{20}=\frac{20}{27.2869}=0.7330 .$$ Same rule, same true $\theta$, two answers $0.2593$ apart. Nothing went wrong: the spread is the estimator's distribution, and classical statistics is the study of that distribution.

How good is the rule? Since $\hat\Theta_n$ is a random variable, ask for its mean. The sum $S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n$ of $n$ i.i.d. exponential($\theta$) variables is Erlang (Gamma) of order $n$ — G4 §3 — with density $$f_{S_n}(s;\theta)=\frac{\theta^{n}s^{n-1}e^{-\theta s}}{(n-1)!},\qquad s\ge 0 .$$ Then, for $n\ge 2$, $$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{S_n}\right] =\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{s}\cdot\frac{\theta^{n}s^{n-1}e^{-\theta s}}{(n-1)!}\,ds =\int_0^\infty \frac{\theta^{n}s^{n-2}e^{-\theta s}}{(n-1)!}\,ds =\frac{\theta}{n-1}\int_0^\infty \frac{\theta^{n-1}s^{n-2}e^{-\theta s}}{(n-2)!}\,ds =\frac{\theta}{n-1},$$ where the third equality just pulls out $\theta/(n-1)$ using $(n-1)!=(n-1)\cdot(n-2)!$, and the last uses that the remaining integrand is exactly the Erlang density of order $n-1$, which integrates to $1$ (normalization, G3 §1). Multiplying by $n$, $$\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]=n\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac1{S_n}\right]=\frac{n\theta}{n-1}\neq\theta .$$ At $\theta=0.5$ this gives $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_2]=1.0$, $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_5]=0.625$, $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_{20}]=0.526316$, $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_{100}]=0.505051$ — overshoots of $100.0000\%$, $25.0000\%$, $5.2632\%$ and $1.0101\%$ of $\theta$. For $n=1$ the integral diverges and $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_1]=\mathbb{E}[1/X_1]=\infty$, the case L23 slide 4 flags. A Monte Carlo run of $400{,}000$ replicates at $n=20$ returns a sample average of $0.52618$ against the exact $0.526316$, with an estimator standard deviation of $0.12384$ (all from computes/g7_s0.py).

Interpretation — three words you now need. That one calculation already contains the whole vocabulary of §1. The gap $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]-\theta=\theta/(n-1)$ is the estimator's bias: on average the rule overshoots. The bias vanishes as $n\to\infty$, and in fact $\hat\Theta_n\to\theta$ in probability, which is consistency. And the spread $0.12384$ around the mean is what feeds the mean squared error $\mathbb{E}[(\hat\Theta_n-\theta)^2]=\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_n)+(\text{bias})^2$ of L23 slide 4. §1 defines all three carefully and insists — L23 slide 4 says it with three exclamation marks — that they must hold for all $\theta$, since you never know which member of the family you are in.

0.3 Every classical question is a probability question about the data

Here is the rule that makes the classical framework mechanical. You may never write a probability about $\theta$; you may always write a probability about $X$, computed under a fixed $\theta$. So every classical claim has the shape $$\mathbf{P}\big(\text{some event described in terms of the data}\,;\,\theta\big)=\text{something},\qquad\text{for every }\theta,$$ and the two great classical constructions are exactly two choices of the event.

Hypothesis testing (§4, L25 slide 2) picks a rejection region $R$ — a set of data values — and rejects $H_0$ when $x\in R$. Its quality measures are $\mathbf{P}(X\in R;H_0)$, the probability of false rejection, and $\mathbf{P}(X\notin R;H_1)$, the probability of false acceptance: both are probabilities about the data. Continuing Example 0.1, the tail probabilities are $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 7;\theta=0.5)=0.171875$ and $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 7;\theta=0.7)=0.649611$. Seven or more heads in ten tosses happens about one time in six for a fair coin, so it is not remotely enough to convict the coin — a conclusion §4 makes precise with a threshold.

Confidence intervals (§1, L23 slide 6) pick a random interval $[\hat\Theta_n^-,\hat\Theta_n^+]$, both endpoints being functions of the data, and demand $$\mathbf{P}\big(\hat\Theta_n^-\le\theta\le\hat\Theta_n^+\big)\ge 1-\alpha\quad\text{for all }\theta .$$ The event inside is again an event about the data — the two endpoints are the random things — and the "for all $\theta$" is not decoration. L23 slide 6 warns in as many words that the "interpretation is subtle"; the next box is that subtlety.

Example 0.3 (what "95% confident" actually promises; L23 slides 6–7). Poll $n=100$ voters, $X_i=1$ if voter $i$ says yes, so $X_i$ is Bernoulli($\theta$) and $\sigma^2=\theta(1-\theta)$. The textbook interval is $\hat\Theta_n\pm z\sigma/\sqrt n$ with $\hat\Theta_n=M_n=(X_1+\cdots+X_n)/n$ and $z$ chosen from $\Phi(z)=1-\alpha/2$; for $\alpha=0.05$, $z=1.959964$ and $\Phi(1.96)=0.975002$. Since $\sigma$ is unknown too, L23 slide 7 offers the bound $\sigma\le 1/2$ — because $\max_{0\le\theta\le1}\theta(1-\theta)=0.250000$ at $\theta=1/2$, so $\sigma\le\sqrt{0.25}=0.500000$ — or the ad hoc plug-in $\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\hat\Theta_n(1-\hat\Theta_n)}$.

Take the plug-in version and compute, by exact enumeration over all $101$ possible values of $X_1+\cdots+X_{100}$, the true probability that the interval covers $\theta$ (computes/g7_s0.py):

true $\theta$$0.5$$0.3$$0.1$$0.05$$0.02$
exact coverage at $n=100$$0.943112$$0.950180$$0.932416$$0.877463$$0.866449$

Over a grid of $981$ values of $\theta$ in $[0.01,0.99]$ the coverage ranges from a maximum of $0.956317$ down to a minimum of $0.633433$, attained at $\theta=0.9900$. So the nominal $95\%$ is a CLT approximation that is decent in the middle of the range and can fail badly near the edges, where the normal approximation of G6 §2 has not kicked in at $n=100$. §1 shows the fixes.

Gotcha — the confidence is in the procedure, not in the interval. Suppose the poll returns $58$ yes votes. With the known-$\sigma$ interval at $\theta=1/2$ the half-width is $1.959964\times 0.5/\sqrt{100}=0.097998$, so you report $[0.4820,\,0.6780]$. It is wrong to say "there is a $95\%$ probability that $\theta$ lies in $[0.4820,0.6780]$." Both endpoints are now numbers and $\theta$ is a number: either $\theta$ is in there or it isn't, and no probability of any kind attaches to that statement in the classical framework. What the $95\%$ describes is the rule: if you repeated the whole poll many times, about $95\%$ of the intervals produced would contain $\theta$ — $94.3112\%$ of them at $\theta=1/2$ and $n=100$, to be exact. G6's posterior interval is the object that does support the forbidden sentence, and it supports it only because a prior was assumed.

0.4 Bayesian or classical?

The two philosophies answer the same practical questions with different machinery. The table lines them up; the recipe says how to choose.

QuestionBayesian (G6 §3–§4)Classical (G7, this note)
Status of the unknown A random variable $\Theta$ with a distribution. A fixed constant $\theta$; no distribution exists (L23 slide 2).
Number of models One: the joint law of $(\Theta,X)$. A family $p_X(x;\theta)$, one per $\theta$ (B&T Chapter 9 preamble).
Input you must supply The prior $p_\Theta$ or $f_\Theta$ — and you must defend it. Nothing beyond the model family — but no prior information can be used, even when you have some.
Notation $f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$ — a conditioning bar. $f_X(x;\theta)$ — a semicolon; a label, not a condition.
The output The posterior $f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)$, a complete distribution; MAP or LMS summarizes it. A single estimator $\hat\Theta_n$ (a random variable), or a decision "reject / do not reject".
What is random in the answer $\Theta$, given the observed data. The estimator/interval/decision, given a fixed $\theta$.
Measure of quality Posterior spread; $\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-\hat\Theta)^2]$ averaged over the prior. Bias, MSE, coverage, error probabilities — required to be good for every $\theta$ (L23 slide 4).
The 7-heads-in-10 coin Uniform prior $\Rightarrow$ posterior Beta$(8,4)$; MAP $=0.700000$, LMS $=0.666667$, $p_X(7)=0.0909090909$. $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=0.7000$; no posterior, no marginal, no probability statement about $\theta$.
Where it appears in G7 Referenced for contrast throughout; MAP is ML with a prior (§2). §1 estimators & CIs, §2 ML, §3 regression, §4 testing.
Interpretation — the numbers agree more than the philosophies do. For the coin, ML gives $0.700000$ and Bayesian MAP under a uniform prior gives $0.700000$: identical, because a flat prior makes "maximize the posterior" the same problem as "maximize the likelihood" (§2 proves this). LMS differs, at $0.666667$, only because it answers a different question — the mean of the posterior rather than its peak. So the split is rarely about arithmetic on a single data set. It is about what you are entitled to say afterwards, and about whether external information may be folded in.
Recipe — classical or Bayesian?
  1. Is there a genuine, defensible prior? A frequency actually observed in past data, a manufacturing tolerance, a physical constraint — if yes, use it: G6's posterior is the complete answer and throwing the prior away is throwing away data.
  2. Would a reader object that the prior was invented? If the only prior you can write is "uniform, because I have no idea," classical methods make that neutrality explicit instead of disguising it as a distribution. Regulatory, legal, and publication settings usually demand this.
  3. Is the unknown physically non-random? The mass of a particle, the speed of light in a fixed medium, the bias of one specific coin: the classical reading is the honest one. Reach for ML (§2) or a confidence interval (§1).
  4. Do you need a guarantee that holds whatever the truth is? "For all $\theta$" is a classical clause. Bayesian guarantees are averages over the prior and say nothing about a specific bad $\theta$. Sample-size and error-rate specifications belong to §1 and §4.
  5. Is the question "which of two stories is it?" Then it is hypothesis testing. Bayesian if the two hypotheses have meaningful prior probabilities (G6 §3 MAP); classical likelihood ratio test if they do not (§4) — the two rules have the same algebraic shape, differing only in the threshold.
  6. Is the question "fit a line/curve to these pairs"? Go straight to §3; classical regression needs no prior at all and reduces to a least-squares formula in the data.

Fig. 0.1 — The map of note G7. Top band: the classical setup of L23 slide 2 (B&T §9.1, its Figure 9.1) — $\theta$ enters as a knob, not as a random variable, and the only random object downstream is the data $X$ and whatever is computed from it. The split below is the one B&T draw in the Chapter 9 preamble: a continuum of $\theta$ values means estimation, a short list of them means testing.
  • §1 — Estimators, bias, consistency, confidence intervals (L23 slides 4–7, rec23 P3, B&T §9.1). $\hat\Theta_n$ as a random variable; unbiasedness $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]=\theta$; consistency $\hat\Theta_n\to\theta$ in probability; the decomposition $\mathbb{E}[(\hat\Theta_n-\theta)^2]=\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_n)+(\text{bias})^2$; the sample mean $M_n$ and its $\sigma^2/n$ error; confidence intervals via the CLT, and the three ways of coping with an unknown $\sigma$ including the sample variance $\hat S_n^2$ with its $n-1$ denominator. Hook: G6 §1–§2 supplied the WLLN and the CLT; §1 is where they finally get used for something.
  • §2 — Maximum likelihood (L23 slide 3, L24 slide 2, rec23 P1–P2, rec24 P1–P2, B&T §9.1). Maximizing $p_X(x;\theta)$ over $\theta$; why you maximize the logarithm instead; i.i.d. products becoming sums; the exponential, normal, uniform and Poisson-type examples; the relation to MAP. Hook: one recipe that applies to every model in the course, and it is the estimator you will actually reach for.
  • §3 — Linear regression (L24 slides 3–6, rec24 P2, B&T §9.2). Fitting $y\approx\theta_0+\theta_1 x$ to data pairs by minimizing the sum of squared residuals; the closed-form solution; why least squares is ML under Gaussian noise; multiple and nonlinear regression; and the standard traps of reading a regression. Hook: the one classical method you have certainly seen before, now derived rather than assumed.
  • §4 — Binary hypothesis testing and the likelihood ratio test (L24 slides 7–8, L25 slides 2–4, B&T §9.3). $H_0$ versus $H_1$; rejection regions; the two error types; the likelihood ratio test $L(x)=p_X(x;H_1)/p_X(x;H_0)>\xi$ and the choice of $\xi$ to fix the false-rejection probability $\alpha$; worked normal-mean and normal-variance tests. Hook: the same ratio that G6 §3 compared against a prior odds is here compared against a threshold you design.
  • §5 — Synthesis and the course finale (L23–L25, rec23–rec24, B&T Chapter 9). Which estimator, which interval, which test; the classical/Bayesian decision revisited with the full toolkit; and the whole G1 $\to$ G7 arc from a sample space to a defended conclusion. Hook: the last section of the last note — it is where all seven notes are meant to click together.
