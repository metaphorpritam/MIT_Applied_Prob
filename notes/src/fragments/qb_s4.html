Q73. ★★ · $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ as a random variable · G4 §1.1–1.3

A machine picks a rod length $Y$ at random from $\{1,2,4\}$ metres, each with probability $1/3$. Given $Y=y$, it then cuts the rod at a point $X$ that is uniform on $[0,y]$.

(a) Compute $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=2]$. Is it a number or a random variable?   (b) Write $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ explicitly and give its PMF.   (c) Use the law of iterated expectations to get $\mathbb{E}[X]$.   (d) Use the law of total variance to get $\operatorname{var}(X)$, and verify it directly.

Solution

Setup. Conditioned on $Y=y$ the cut point is $U(0,y)$, so $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]=y/2$ and $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)=y^2/12$ (the uniform mean and variance, G4 §1.1).

(a) A number. Substituting $y=2$: $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=2]=2/2=1$. Once you have fixed the conditioning value, the averaging is done and what is left is a constant, $1$ metre.

(b) A random variable. Do not fix $y$: the map $y\mapsto y/2$ evaluated at the random $Y$ is the random variable $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=Y/2$. It is a function of $Y$, so it inherits $Y$'s randomness: $$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=\begin{cases}0.5 & \text{w.p. } 1/3\ (Y=1),\\ 1 & \text{w.p. } 1/3\ (Y=2),\\ 2 & \text{w.p. } 1/3\ (Y=4).\end{cases}$$

(c) Iterated expectations. $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]$ — average the random variable from (b) over $Y$: $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\tfrac13(0.5)+\tfrac13(1)+\tfrac13(2)=\frac{3.5}{3}=1.166667=\tfrac76 \text{ m}.$$

(d) Total variance. Within-group first: $$\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]=\tfrac13\Big(\tfrac{1}{12}+\tfrac{4}{12}+\tfrac{16}{12}\Big)=\tfrac13(0.083333+0.333333+1.333333)=0.583333=\tfrac{21}{36}.$$ Between-group next. $\mathbb{E}\big[(Y/2)^2\big]=\tfrac13(0.25+1+4)=\tfrac{5.25}{3}=1.75$, so $$\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)=1.75-(1.166667)^2=1.75-1.361111=0.388889 .$$ Adding, $\operatorname{var}(X)=0.583333+0.388889=0.972222$. Direct check: $\mathbb{E}[X^2\mid Y=y]=y^2/3$, so $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=\tfrac13\big(\tfrac13+\tfrac43+\tfrac{16}{3}\big)=\tfrac{21}{9}=2.333333$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)=2.333333-1.361111=0.972222$ — identical. A $2\times10^6$-draw simulation gives $\mathbb{E}[X]\approx1.166325$, $\operatorname{var}(X)\approx0.972754$.

Answer. $\;$(a) $1$, a number. (b) $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=Y/2$, taking $0.5,1,2$ each w.p. $1/3$. (c) $\mathbb{E}[X]=7/6\approx1.166667$. (d) $\operatorname{var}(X)=0.583333+0.388889=0.972222$.

Trap. Writing "$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=7/6$". That is $\mathbb{E}[X]$, the number you get after averaging away $Y$; $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ still varies with $Y$ and has variance $0.388889$. A quantity with positive variance is not a constant.

Q74. ★ · Law of iterated expectations · G4 §1.2

Every incoming support ticket is routed to exactly one of three tiers: tier 1 with probability $0.50$, tier 2 with probability $0.35$, tier 3 with probability $0.15$. Mean handling time is $6$ minutes in tier 1, $18$ minutes in tier 2, and $47$ minutes in tier 3. Let $T$ be the handling time of a ticket.

(a) Find $\mathbb{E}[T]$.   (b) Agent time is billed at $\$1.20$ per minute; find the expected cost per ticket.   (c) What fraction of all agent minutes is consumed by tier 3? Comment on the number.

Solution

Setup. Let $S\in\{1,2,3\}$ be the tier. The problem hands you $\mathbb{E}[T\mid S=s]$ for each $s$ and the PMF of $S$ — exactly the two ingredients of the law of iterated expectations, $\mathbb{E}[T]=\sum_s \mathbf{P}(S=s)\,\mathbb{E}[T\mid S=s]$ (G4 §1.2).

(a) Weighted average. Term by term: $$0.50\times6=3.0,\qquad 0.35\times18=6.3,\qquad 0.15\times47=7.05,$$ so $\mathbb{E}[T]=3.0+6.3+7.05=16.35$ minutes.

(b) Linearity. Cost $C=1.20\,T$, so $\mathbb{E}[C]=1.20\times16.35=\$19.62$. (Linearity applies to any random variable, no independence needed.)

(c) Reading the terms. Each product $\mathbf{P}(S=s)\mathbb{E}[T\mid S=s]$ is the mean minutes per ticket contributed by tier $s$. Tier 3's share is $$\frac{7.05}{16.35}=0.431193, \text{ i.e. } 43.119266\%,$$ while tier 1's is $3.0/16.35=0.183486$. So the tier that receives $15\%$ of tickets eats $43\%$ of the labour, and the tier receiving half the tickets eats $18\%$. Iterated expectations is not just a formula: its summands are a workload budget.

Answer. $\;\mathbb{E}[T]=16.35$ min, $\;\mathbb{E}[C]=\$19.62$, $\;$tier 3 consumes $43.12\%$ of agent minutes.

Trap. Averaging the three tier means unweighted, $(6+18+47)/3=23.666667$. That answers a different question — the mean over a randomly chosen tier, not over a randomly chosen ticket.

Q75. ★★ · Total variance: between and within · G4 §1.3

A plant runs three shifts. A randomly chosen production hour is a day shift with probability $0.5$, an evening shift with probability $0.3$, a night shift with probability $0.2$. Let $X$ be the number of defective units produced in that hour. Conditional on the shift, $X$ has mean and variance $$\text{day: } (40,\,25),\qquad \text{evening: } (52,\,64),\qquad \text{night: } (70,\,144).$$ (a) Find $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)$.   (b) Split $\operatorname{var}(X)$ into its within-shift and between-shift parts and say in words what each measures.   (c) Management can either (i) retrain so that all three shifts have the same mean $49.6$, keeping the conditional variances, or (ii) do nothing. What does the standard deviation of $X$ become under (i)?

Solution

Setup. Let $S$ be the shift. The law of total variance (G4 §1.3) reads $$\operatorname{var}(X)=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid S)\big]}_{\text{within}}+\underbrace{\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid S]\big)}_{\text{between}} .$$

(a1) Overall mean. $\mathbb{E}[X]=0.5(40)+0.3(52)+0.2(70)=20+15.6+14=49.6$ defectives.

(a2) Within. Average the conditional variances: $$\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid S)\big]=0.5(25)+0.3(64)+0.2(144)=12.5+19.2+28.8=60.5 .$$

(a3) Between. The random variable $\mathbb{E}[X\mid S]$ takes values $40,52,70$ with probabilities $0.5,0.3,0.2$. Its second moment is $$0.5(1600)+0.3(2704)+0.2(4900)=800+811.2+980=2591.2,$$ so $\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid S]\big)=2591.2-(49.6)^2=2591.2-2460.16=131.04$.

(a4) Total. $\operatorname{var}(X)=60.5+131.04=191.54$, i.e. $\sigma_X=\sqrt{191.54}=13.839798$.

(b) Interpretation. The within term $60.5$ is the average scatter you would still see if you were told the shift — irreducible hour-to-hour noise inside a shift. The between term $131.04$ is the scatter of the shift averages themselves — how much of the spread is explained by simply knowing which shift it is. Here $131.04/191.54=0.684139$, so $68.4\%$ of the variance is between-shift: shift identity is the dominant driver.

(c) Equalizing the means. Making all conditional means equal to $49.6$ makes $\mathbb{E}[X\mid S]$ a constant, so the between term collapses to $0$ and only the within term survives: $$\operatorname{var}(X)=60.5,\qquad \sigma_X=\sqrt{60.5}=7.778175 .$$ The standard deviation falls from $13.84$ to $7.78$, a $44\%$ reduction — and no hour ever became individually more predictable.

Answer. $\;\mathbb{E}[X]=49.6$; $\;\operatorname{var}(X)=60.5+131.04=191.54$ ($\sigma=13.839798$); within $=60.5$, between $=131.04$ ($68.41\%$); after retraining $\sigma=7.778175$.

Trap. Averaging the conditional variances alone and reporting $\operatorname{var}(X)=60.5$. That is the variance you would have if the shift were known; pooling three differently centred populations adds the between term $131.04$ on top.

Q76. ★★ · True or false, with reason · G4 §1.3

Decide true or false and justify each. $X,Y$ are random variables with finite variance.

(a) $\operatorname{var}(X)\ \ge\ \mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]$ always.   (b) If $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ is the same constant for every value of $Y$, then $X$ and $Y$ are independent.   (c) If $\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]=0$, then $X$ is (with probability 1) a function of $Y$.

Solution

(a) True. Total variance gives $\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)]+\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y])$. The second term is a variance, hence $\ge0$, so the first term alone can never exceed $\operatorname{var}(X)$. In words: conditioning on extra information can only reduce the average remaining variance. Equality holds exactly when $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ is constant.

(b) False. A constant conditional mean kills only the between term; $Y$ can still control the conditional spread. Counterexample: $Y$ is $1$ or $2$ with probability $1/2$ each; given $Y=1$, $X=\pm1$ equally likely; given $Y=2$, $X=\pm2$ equally likely. Then $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=0$ for both values, so $\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y])=0$, yet $$\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=1)=1,\quad \operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=2)=4,\quad \operatorname{var}(X)=0.5(1)+0.5(4)+0=2.5 .$$ Dependence is visible in the PMF: $\mathbf{P}(X=2)=0.5\times0.5=0.25$ but $\mathbf{P}(X=2\mid Y=1)=0$. Since $0\ne0.25$, $X$ and $Y$ are dependent. (What does follow from a constant $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ is $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=0$ — uncorrelated, not independent.)

(c) True. $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)\ge0$ for every $y$, so an average of zero forces $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)=0$ for every $y$ that occurs with positive probability. A random variable with zero variance is a constant, so given $Y=y$ the value of $X$ is pinned to the single number $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]=g(y)$. Hence $X=g(Y)$ almost surely, and then $\operatorname{var}(X)=\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y])$: all of the variance is between-group.

Answer. $\;$(a) True. (b) False — constant conditional mean gives $\operatorname{cov}=0$, not independence; the counterexample has $\operatorname{var}(X)=2.5$ with all of it within-group. (c) True.

Trap. Answering (b) "True" on the ground that a constant $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ means $Y$ tells you nothing about $X$. It tells you nothing about the mean of $X$ — which is exactly why $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=0$ — but in the counterexample $\mathbf{P}(X=2)=0.25$ while $\mathbf{P}(X=2\mid Y=1)=0$, so $Y$ still controls the spread. Uncorrelated is the most you may conclude; independence is the wrong answer.

Q77. ★ · Sum of a random number of terms · G4 §2.2

A food truck serves $N$ customers in a day, where $\mathbb{E}[N]=120$ and $\operatorname{var}(N)=200$. The $i$th customer spends $X_i$ dollars; the $X_i$ are i.i.d. with $\mathbb{E}[X]=14$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)=36$, and are independent of $N$. Let $Y=X_1+\dots+X_N$ be the day's revenue.

(a) Find $\mathbb{E}[Y]$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y)$.   (b) Which source of randomness — how many customers, or how much each spends — contributes more to $\operatorname{var}(Y)$, and by how much?

Solution

Setup. This is the random-sum formula (G4 §2.2), which needs only the four moments given — no distributional assumption about $N$ or $X$: $$\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[N]\,\mathbb{E}[X],\qquad \operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)+\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2\operatorname{var}(N).$$

(a1) Mean. $\mathbb{E}[Y]=120\times14=\$1680$.

(a2) Variance. Substituting term by term: $$\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)}_{120\times36\;=\;4320}+\underbrace{(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N)}_{14^2\times200\;=\;196\times200\;=\;39\,200}=43\,520\ \text{dollars}^2,$$ so $\sigma_Y=\sqrt{43\,520}=208.614477$ dollars.

(b) Which term wins. The count term is $39\,200/43\,520=0.900735$, i.e. $90.07\%$ of the variance. Uncertainty in how many customers show up dominates uncertainty in what each one spends, because the per-customer noise averages out across $120$ customers while the count noise is multiplied by the full mean ticket $\mathbb{E}[X]=14$. A $60\,000$-day simulation gives $\mathbb{E}[Y]\approx1681.35$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y)\approx43\,389$.

Answer. $\;\mathbb{E}[Y]=\$1680$, $\;\operatorname{var}(Y)=4320+39\,200=43\,520$ ($\sigma_Y=\$208.61$); the customer count supplies $90.07\%$ of the variance.

Trap. Multiplying the two variances, $\operatorname{var}(N)\operatorname{var}(X)=200\times36=7200$, or keeping only the first term, $\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)=4320$. Neither is $43\,520$: the count term carries $(\mathbb{E}[X])^2=196$, not $\operatorname{var}(X)$, and dropping it discards the $90.07\%$ of the variance that part (b) is about.

Q78. ★★ · Spot the error · G4 §2.2

A student writes: "$Y=X_1+\dots+X_N$ is a sum of $N$ i.i.d. terms, so conditioning on $N$ gives $\operatorname{var}(Y\mid N)=N\operatorname{var}(X)$, and therefore $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)$."

Test the claim on this case: $N$ is uniform on $\{0,1,2,3\}$ and every $X_i$ equals exactly $5$ (so $\operatorname{var}(X)=0$). Identify the missing step, state the correct formula, and give the correct $\operatorname{var}(Y)$.

Solution

Step 1: what the test case says. With $X_i\equiv5$ we have $Y=5N$ exactly. Since $N$ is uniform on $\{0,1,2,3\}$, $\mathbb{E}[N]=1.5$ and $\operatorname{var}(N)=\frac{4^2-1}{12}=1.25$. Then $$\mathbb{E}[Y]=5(1.5)=7.5,\qquad \operatorname{var}(Y)=\operatorname{var}(5N)=25\operatorname{var}(N)=25(1.25)=31.25 .$$ The student's formula gives $\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)=1.5\times0=0$. A random variable taking the four distinct values $0,5,10,15$ cannot have zero variance, so the formula is wrong.

Step 2: the missing step. The student computed only $\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(Y\mid N)\big]$ and stopped. The law of total variance has a second term: $$\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(Y\mid N)\big]+\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid N]\big).$$ Here $\mathbb{E}[Y\mid N]=N\mathbb{E}[X]$ is not constant — it moves with $N$ — so its variance is $(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N)$, which the student silently set to zero.

Step 3: the correct formula, applied. $$\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)+\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2\operatorname{var}(N)=1.5(0)+25(1.25)=0+31.25=31.25,$$ matching the exact computation. A $2\times10^6$-run simulation gives $31.242124$.

Step 4: when is the student right? Only when $\operatorname{var}(N)=0$, i.e. $N$ is a deterministic constant $n_0$ — then $\operatorname{var}(Y)=n_0\operatorname{var}(X)$, the ordinary i.i.d. formula. The random-sum formula degenerates to the student's whenever the count stops being random.

Answer. $\;$The step $\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[Y\mid N]\big)=(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N)$ is missing; correctly $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)+(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N)=31.25$, not $0$.

Tool: G4 §2.2 (random sums via total variance) · G4 §1.3 · B&T §4.4

Q79. ★★ · Compound Poisson: derivation plus numbers · G4 §2.2, §3.2

An insurer receives $N$ claims per day, $N\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda)$ with $\lambda=8$. Claim sizes $X_i$ are i.i.d. with mean $\mu=\$2500$ and standard deviation $\sigma=\$4000$, independent of $N$. Let $Y$ be the day's total payout.

(a) Show in general that $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[X^2]$.   (b) Compute $\mathbb{E}[Y]$, $\operatorname{var}(Y)$ and $\sigma_Y$.   (c) Report the coefficient of variation $\sigma_Y/\mathbb{E}[Y]$ and interpret it.

Solution

(a) Derivation. Start from the random-sum variance formula (G4 §2.2): $$\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)+\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2\operatorname{var}(N).$$ For a Poisson count the mean and variance coincide, $\mathbb{E}[N]=\operatorname{var}(N)=\lambda$ (G4 §3.2). Substituting both: $$\operatorname{var}(Y)=\lambda\operatorname{var}(X)+\lambda\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2=\lambda\Big(\operatorname{var}(X)+(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\Big)=\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[X^2],$$ the last equality being the definition of variance rearranged. Note the second moment, not the variance, is what matters: a claim distribution with mean $0$ risk but huge spread costs exactly as much variance as its spread.

(b) Numbers. $\mathbb{E}[Y]=\lambda\mu=8\times2500=\$20\,000$ per day. The second moment is $$\mathbb{E}[X^2]=\sigma^2+\mu^2=4000^2+2500^2=16\,000\,000+6\,250\,000=22\,250\,000 .$$ Hence $$\operatorname{var}(Y)=8\times22\,250\,000=178\,000\,000\ \text{dollars}^2,\qquad \sigma_Y=\sqrt{178\,000\,000}=\$13\,341.66 .$$ Split into the two original terms as a check: sizes contribute $8\times16{,}000{,}000=128$ million, the random count contributes $2500^2\times8=50$ million, total $178$ million. A $60\,000$-day simulation with gamma-distributed claim sizes gives $\mathbb{E}[Y]\approx19\,953$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y)\approx1.7773\times10^8$.

(c) Interpretation. $\sigma_Y/\mathbb{E}[Y]=13\,341.66/20\,000=0.667083$. Daily payout has a standard deviation two-thirds the size of its mean — the insurer cannot budget on the mean alone. Here $128/178=71.9\%$ of the variance comes from the heavy claim sizes, the reverse of Q77, because $\sigma=4000$ far exceeds $\mu=2500$.

Answer. $\;\operatorname{var}(Y)=\lambda\mathbb{E}[X^2]$; numerically $\mathbb{E}[Y]=\$20\,000$, $\operatorname{var}(Y)=1.78\times10^8$, $\sigma_Y=\$13\,341.66$, $\;\sigma_Y/\mathbb{E}[Y]=0.667083$.

Trap. Writing $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\lambda\operatorname{var}(X)=8\times16{,}000{,}000=1.28\times10^8$. That drops the $\mu^2\lambda=5\times10^7$ contributed by the random number of claims and understates $\sigma_Y$ by about $\$2000$ a day.

Q80. ★ · Bernoulli process: the $k$th success · G4 §2.3, §2.5

An inspector tests items one at a time, independently; each is defective with probability $p=0.15$. Let $T_1$ be the trial index of the first defective and $T_3$ that of the third.

(a) Find $\mathbf{P}(T_3=7)$.   (b) Find $\mathbb{E}[T_3]$ and $\operatorname{var}(T_3)$.   (c) Find $\mathbf{P}(T_1\le5)$.

Solution

Setup. Independent trials with a common success probability form a Bernoulli process (G4 §2.3). The $k$th success time has the Pascal PMF $$\mathbf{P}(T_k=t)=\binom{t-1}{k-1}p^k(1-p)^{t-k},\qquad t=k,k+1,\dots$$ and $T_1$ is geometric.

(a) Pascal at $k=3$, $t=7$. The event is "exactly $2$ defectives among the first $6$ trials, and a defective on trial $7$", so $$\mathbf{P}(T_3=7)=\binom{6}{2}p^3(1-p)^4=15\times(0.15)^3\times(0.85)^4=15\times0.003375\times0.522006 .$$ Multiplying: $0.003375\times0.522006=0.001762$, and $15\times0.001762=0.026427$.

(b) Moments. $T_3$ is the sum of three i.i.d. geometric interarrival times, each with mean $1/p$ and variance $(1-p)/p^2$ (G4 §2.4–2.5). By linearity and independence, $$\mathbb{E}[T_3]=\frac{3}{0.15}=20\ \text{trials},\qquad \operatorname{var}(T_3)=\frac{3(1-0.15)}{0.15^2}=\frac{3\times0.85}{0.0225}=\frac{2.55}{0.0225}=113.333333,$$ so $\sigma_{T_3}=10.645813$ trials — a very wide distribution around $20$.

(c) Geometric tail. $\mathbf{P}(T_1>5)=(1-p)^5=(0.85)^5=0.443705$, hence $\mathbf{P}(T_1\le5)=1-0.443705=0.556295$.

Answer. $\;\mathbf{P}(T_3=7)=0.026427$, $\;\mathbb{E}[T_3]=20$, $\;\operatorname{var}(T_3)=113.333333$, $\;\mathbf{P}(T_1\le5)=0.556295$.

Trap. Answering (a) with the binomial $\binom{7}{3}p^3(1-p)^4=0.061662$. That is $\mathbf{P}(\text{exactly 3 defectives in 7 trials})$, which allows the seventh trial to be clean; $T_3=7$ requires trial $7$ itself to be the third defective, which is why the coefficient is $\binom{6}{2}=15$, not $\binom{7}{3}=35$.

Q81. ★★ · Memorylessness and fresh start · G4 §2.4

A monitoring system polls a sensor once per second; each poll independently raises an alarm with probability $p=0.02$.

(a) Given that the first $100$ polls raised no alarm, what is the expected number of additional polls until the first alarm?   (b) Given the same information, find $\mathbf{P}(\text{no alarm in polls }101\text{ through }150)$.   (c) Suppose instead that the first $100$ polls raised exactly $1$ alarm. A colleague reasons: "alarms average $2$ per $100$ polls; we saw only $1$ in the first $100$, so we should expect about $3$ in the next $100$ to make up the shortfall." Evaluate.   (d) From time $0$, find the mean and variance of the poll index at which the second alarm occurs.

Solution

Setup. Polls are i.i.d. Bernoulli($0.02$) trials, so this is a Bernoulli process (G4 §2.3). Its fresh-start property (G4 §2.4): for any fixed time $n$, the trials after $n$ form a Bernoulli process with the same $p$, independent of everything up to $n$.

(a) Memorylessness. Conditioned on no alarm in the first $100$ polls, the remaining wait $T-100$ has the same geometric distribution as $T$ itself. Hence the expected additional wait is $$\mathbb{E}[T-100\mid T>100]=\frac1p=\frac{1}{0.02}=50\ \text{polls}.$$ The $100$ wasted polls buy nothing: the process has no memory of them.

(b) A clean 50-trial block. By fresh start, polls $101,\dots,150$ are $50$ fresh independent trials, so $$\mathbf{P}(\text{no alarm in those }50)=(1-p)^{50}=(0.98)^{50}=0.364170 .$$ (Unconditionally, $\mathbf{P}(\text{no alarm in first }100)=(0.98)^{100}=0.132620$ — but that number plays no role here, because conditioning on it changes nothing about the future.)

(c) The gambler's fallacy. False. Counts over disjoint blocks of trials are independent, so the number of alarms in polls $101$–$200$ is Binomial($100,0.02$) whatever happened before, with mean $100\times0.02=2$, not $3$. Coins and sensors do not "make up" shortfalls; only an estimate of $p$ would be revised by past data, and here $p$ is known.

(d) Second alarm. The time of the second alarm is the sum of two i.i.d. geometric interarrivals, so $$\mathbb{E}[T_2]=\frac{2}{p}=\frac{2}{0.02}=100\ \text{polls},\qquad \operatorname{var}(T_2)=\frac{2(1-p)}{p^2}=\frac{2\times0.98}{0.0004}=4900,$$ i.e. a standard deviation of $70$ polls.

Q82. ★★ · Merging Bernoulli processes · G4 §2.6

Two independent sensors report in each one-second slot: sensor A reports with probability $p_A=0.10$, sensor B with probability $p_B=0.25$, independently of each other and across slots. The control room records a "report slot" whenever at least one sensor reports.

(a) Show that the record is a Bernoulli process and find its parameter.   (b) Find the expected number of slots until the first report slot.   (c) Given that a slot is a report slot, find the probabilities that it came from A alone, B alone, and both.   (d) Find the expected number of slots until the first slot in which both sensors report.

Solution

(a) The merged parameter. Slots are independent (both sources are, and across slots too), and each slot independently is a report slot with the same probability, so the merged record is a Bernoulli process (G4 §2.6). Its parameter is $$p=\mathbf{P}(A\cup B)=p_A+p_B-p_Ap_B=0.10+0.25-0.025=0.325,$$ where $p_Ap_B=0.10\times0.25=0.025$ is subtracted because a slot with both reports would otherwise be counted twice. Equivalently $p=1-(1-0.10)(1-0.25)=1-0.9\times0.75=1-0.675=0.325$.

(b) Geometric wait. The first arrival of a Bernoulli($0.325$) process is geometric, so $$\mathbb{E}[T_1]=\frac{1}{0.325}=3.076923\ \text{slots}.$$

(c) Attribution, by conditional probability. Divide each disjoint case by $p=0.325$: $$\mathbf{P}(\text{A only}\mid\text{report})=\frac{0.10\times0.75}{0.325}=\frac{0.075}{0.325}=0.230769,$$ $$\mathbf{P}(\text{B only}\mid\text{report})=\frac{0.25\times0.90}{0.325}=\frac{0.225}{0.325}=0.692308,\qquad \mathbf{P}(\text{both}\mid\text{report})=\frac{0.025}{0.325}=0.076923 .$$ The three sum to $1.000000$, as they must.

(d) A rarer merged process. "Both report" is itself a Bernoulli process with parameter $p_Ap_B=0.025$, so the wait for the first such slot is geometric with mean $1/0.025=40$ slots.

Answer. $\;$(a) Bernoulli with $p=0.325$. (b) $3.076923$ slots. (c) A only $0.230769$, B only $0.692308$, both $0.076923$. (d) $40$ slots.

Trap. Taking the merged parameter to be $p_A+p_B=0.35$ (which would give $\mathbb{E}[T_1]=2.857143$). Bernoulli merging is not additive, because two sensors can report in the same slot; the overlap $0.025$ must come off. Poisson merging is additive precisely because simultaneous arrivals have probability zero in continuous time (Q88).

Q83. ★★★ · A random number of geometric waits · G4 §2.2, §2.4

A game works as follows. First a fair spinner picks $K$ uniformly from $\{1,2,3,4\}$. The player then tosses a biased coin repeatedly, with $\mathbf{P}(\text{head})=p=0.4$ per toss independently, and stops at the $K$th head. Let $T$ be the total number of tosses.

(a) Find $\mathbb{E}[T]$ and $\operatorname{var}(T)$.   (b) Find $\mathbf{P}(T=2)$ exactly.   (c) Explain why you cannot get $\operatorname{var}(T)$ by writing "$T$ is Pascal with $k=\mathbb{E}[K]=2.5$".

Solution

Setup. $T=X_1+\dots+X_K$ where the $X_i$ are i.i.d. geometric($0.4$) interarrival times of the Bernoulli process (G4 §2.4) and $K$ is independent of them. That is exactly the random-sum structure of G4 §2.2 with the count $K$.

(a1) The four moments. For $K$ uniform on $\{1,2,3,4\}$: $\mathbb{E}[K]=2.5$ and $\operatorname{var}(K)=\frac{4^2-1}{12}=\frac{15}{12}=1.25$. For a geometric with $p=0.4$: $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac1p=2.5,\qquad \operatorname{var}(X)=\frac{1-p}{p^2}=\frac{0.6}{0.16}=3.75 .$$

(a2) Substitute. $$\mathbb{E}[T]=\mathbb{E}[K]\mathbb{E}[X]=2.5\times2.5=6.25\ \text{tosses},$$ $$\operatorname{var}(T)=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[K]\operatorname{var}(X)}_{2.5\times3.75\,=\,9.375}+\underbrace{(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(K)}_{6.25\times1.25\,=\,7.8125}=17.1875,$$ so $\sigma_T=\sqrt{17.1875}=4.145781$ tosses. A $2\times10^5$-game simulation gives $\mathbb{E}[T]\approx6.2364$ and $\operatorname{var}(T)\approx16.99$.

(b) The PMF at its smallest interesting value. Condition on $K$ and use total probability. $T=2$ needs $K\le2$, since $T\ge K$ always.
• $K=1$: the first head must land on toss $2$, so $\mathbf{P}=(1-p)p=0.6\times0.4=0.24$; weighted, $0.25\times0.24=0.06$.
• $K=2$: both tosses must be heads, $\mathbf{P}=p^2=0.16$; weighted, $0.25\times0.16=0.04$.
• $K=3,4$: impossible, contributing $0$.
Total $\mathbf{P}(T=2)=0.06+0.04=0.10$. The simulation gives $0.100055$.

(c) Why the shortcut fails. Replacing the random $K$ by its mean destroys exactly the term $(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(K)=7.8125$. A Pascal variable with a fixed $k=2.5$ heads would have variance $2.5\times3.75=9.375$, understating the true $17.1875$ by $45\%$ — and it is not even a legal distribution, since $k$ must be a positive integer. More generally, plugging a mean into a nonlinear place is the error $\mathbb{E}[g(K)]\ne g(\mathbb{E}[K])$; here the variance is linear in $k$ but the randomness of $k$ contributes separately.

Answer. $\;\mathbb{E}[T]=6.25$, $\;\operatorname{var}(T)=9.375+7.8125=17.1875$ ($\sigma_T=4.145781$), $\;\mathbf{P}(T=2)=0.10$.

Trap. In (b), adding the two conditional probabilities unweighted: $0.24+0.16=0.400000$. Those two numbers are $\mathbf{P}(T=2\mid K=1)$ and $\mathbf{P}(T=2\mid K=2)$; total probability multiplies each by $\mathbf{P}(K=k)=0.25$ before adding, which is what turns $0.40$ into the correct $0.10$. A conditional probability is not the probability of the intersection.

Q84. ★ · Poisson counts over intervals · G4 §3.1–3.2

Patients arrive at a rural clinic as a Poisson process with rate $\lambda=3$ per hour.

(a) Find the probability of exactly $2$ arrivals in a given $30$-minute window.   (b) Find the probability of at least one arrival in a $2$-hour window.   (c) Find the probability of exactly $2$ arrivals in the first half-hour and none in the second half-hour.   (d) Given $2$ arrivals in the first half-hour, what is the probability of none in the second?

Solution

Setup. For a Poisson process of rate $\lambda$, the count $N_\tau$ over an interval of length $\tau$ is Poisson with parameter $\lambda\tau$, and counts over disjoint intervals are independent (G4 §3.1): $$\mathbf{P}(N_\tau=k)=e^{-\lambda\tau}\frac{(\lambda\tau)^k}{k!} .$$

(a) Half an hour. Here $\tau=0.5$ h, so $\lambda\tau=3\times0.5=1.5$ expected arrivals and $$\mathbf{P}(N=2)=e^{-1.5}\frac{1.5^2}{2!}=0.223130\times\frac{2.25}{2}=0.223130\times1.125=0.251021 .$$ A $2\times10^6$-sample check gives $0.250866$.

(b) Two hours, complement. $\lambda\tau=3\times2=6$, so $$\mathbf{P}(N\ge1)=1-\mathbf{P}(N=0)=1-e^{-6}=1-0.002479=0.997521 .$$

(c) Two disjoint windows. The half-hours are disjoint, so the counts are independent and the probabilities multiply: $$\mathbf{P}=\underbrace{0.251021}_{\text{2 in first}}\times\underbrace{e^{-1.5}}_{=\,0.223130\ \text{(0 in second)}}=0.056010 .$$

(d) Conditioning changes nothing. By the same independence, $\mathbf{P}(N_{\text{2nd}}=0\mid N_{\text{1st}}=2)=\mathbf{P}(N_{\text{2nd}}=0)=e^{-1.5}=0.223130$. Consistency check: $0.056010/0.251021=0.223130$. ✓

Answer. $\;$(a) $0.251021$, (b) $0.997521$, (c) $0.056010$, (d) $0.223130$.

Trap. Using $\lambda\tau=3$ in (a) because the rate is $3$. The Poisson parameter is rate $\times$ length; over half an hour it is $1.5$, and using $3$ would give $e^{-3}3^2/2=0.224042$ — close enough to look plausible, which is what makes it dangerous.

Q85. ★ · Exponential interarrivals, memorylessness · G4 §3.3

Emergency calls reach a dispatcher as a Poisson process with rate $\lambda=5$ per hour. Let $T$ be the time until the next call, measured in minutes from now.

(a) State the distribution of $T$ and its mean.   (b) Find $\mathbf{P}(T>20)$.   (c) Eight minutes pass with no call. Find the probability of a further wait exceeding $20$ minutes, and the expected further wait.   (d) Find $\mathbf{P}(5<T<15)$.

Solution

Setup. Interarrival times of a Poisson process are exponential with the same rate (G4 §3.3). Converting units: $\lambda=5$/hour $=5/60=0.083333$ per minute, so $T\sim\text{Exp}(1/12)$ with $\mathbb{E}[T]=1/\lambda=12$ minutes and $\mathbf{P}(T>t)=e^{-t/12}$.

(a) $T$ is exponential with rate $0.083333$ per minute; $\mathbb{E}[T]=12$ minutes.

(b) Tail. $\mathbf{P}(T>20)=e^{-20/12}=e^{-1.666667}=0.188876$.

(c) Memorylessness. The exponential is memoryless: for $s,t\ge0$, $\mathbf{P}(T>s+t\mid T>s)=\mathbf{P}(T>t)$. Hence $$\mathbf{P}(T>28\mid T>8)=\mathbf{P}(T>20)=e^{-20/12}=0.188876,$$ identical to (b), and the expected further wait is again $1/\lambda=12$ minutes (so the expected total wait, counted from the original start, is $8+12=20$ minutes). The eight elapsed minutes carry no information because a Poisson process restarts afresh at every fixed time.

(d) An interval probability. Use the two tails: $$\mathbf{P}(5<T<15)=\mathbf{P}(T>5)-\mathbf{P}(T>15)=e^{-5/12}-e^{-15/12}=0.659241-0.286505=0.372736 .$$

Answer. $\;$(a) Exponential, mean $12$ min. (b) $0.188876$. (c) $0.188876$ and $12$ more minutes. (d) $0.372736$.

Trap. In (c), "subtracting the elapsed time" to get $\mathbf{P}(T>20-8)=e^{-12/12}=0.367879$. That treats the wait as a fixed budget being drawn down. Memorylessness says the opposite: after any wait, the remaining time has the original distribution, so the answer is $0.188876$, not $0.367879$.

Q86. ★★ · Erlang, and which tool to use · G4 §3.4

Same dispatcher as Q85: calls are Poisson with $\lambda=5$ per hour. Let $Y_5$ be the time (in hours from now) of the fifth call.

(a) Name the distribution of $Y_5$, write its PDF, and evaluate the PDF at $t=1$ hour.   (b) Find $\mathbb{E}[Y_5]$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y_5)$.   (c) Find $\mathbf{P}(Y_5>1.5)$ — and explain why the count representation is the right tool rather than integrating the PDF.

Solution

(a) Erlang of order 5. $Y_5=X_1+\dots+X_5$ is a sum of five i.i.d. Exp($\lambda$) interarrivals, which is Erlang of order $5$ (G4 §3.4): $$f_{Y_5}(t)=\frac{\lambda^5 t^4 e^{-\lambda t}}{4!},\qquad t\ge0 .$$ At $t=1$ with $\lambda=5$: $\lambda^5=3125$, $e^{-5}=0.006738$, $4!=24$, so $$f_{Y_5}(1)=\frac{3125\times1\times0.006738}{24}=\frac{21.056}{24}=0.877337\ \text{per hour}.$$

(b) Moments by linearity. Each $X_i$ has mean $1/\lambda=0.2$ h and variance $1/\lambda^2=0.04$ h$^2$, and they are independent, so $$\mathbb{E}[Y_5]=\frac{5}{5}=1\ \text{hour},\qquad \operatorname{var}(Y_5)=\frac{5}{25}=0.2\ \text{h}^2,$$ giving $\sigma=\sqrt{0.2}=0.447214$ h $=26.832816$ minutes.

(c) Counts beat integration. The key equivalence (G4 §3.4) is $$\{Y_5>1.5\}=\{\text{fewer than 5 arrivals by time }1.5\}=\{N_{1.5}\le4\},$$ and $N_{1.5}$ is Poisson with parameter $\lambda\tau=5\times1.5=7.5$. So the answer is a finite sum instead of an integral of $t^4e^{-5t}$ (which needs four integrations by parts). With $e^{-7.5}=0.000553$: $$\mathbf{P}(N_{1.5}\le4)=e^{-7.5}\Big(1+7.5+\frac{7.5^2}{2}+\frac{7.5^3}{6}+\frac{7.5^4}{24}\Big)$$ $$=0.000553+0.004148+0.015555+0.038889+0.072916=0.132062 .$$ A gamma-survival-function evaluation gives $0.132062$ and a $2\times10^6$-draw simulation gives $0.131954$. So a $1.5$-hour wait for the fifth call, though its mean is only $1$ hour, still happens about $13\%$ of the time.

Answer. $\;$(a) Erlang order $5$; $f_{Y_5}(1)=0.877337$/h. (b) $\mathbb{E}[Y_5]=1$ h, $\operatorname{var}(Y_5)=0.2$ h$^2$. (c) $\mathbf{P}(Y_5>1.5)=0.132062$.

Trap. Writing $\{Y_5>1.5\}=\{N_{1.5}<5\}$ but then computing $\mathbf{P}(N_{1.5}\le5)=0.241436$ by including $k=5$. Five arrivals by time $1.5$ means the fifth has already happened, so $k=5$ belongs to the complementary event.

Q87. ★★ · What the small-interval definition actually says · G4 §3.1–3.2

The Poisson process of rate $\lambda$ is often defined by: over an interval of length $\delta$, $\mathbf{P}(0\text{ arrivals})\approx1-\lambda\delta$, $\mathbf{P}(1\text{ arrival})\approx\lambda\delta$, and $\mathbf{P}(2\text{ or more})$ is negligible — all as $\delta\to0$.

(a) With $\lambda=2$ per minute and $\delta=0.001$ minute, compute the exact $\mathbf{P}(\ge1\text{ arrival})$ and compare it to $\lambda\delta$.   (b) Compute the exact $\mathbf{P}(\ge2)$ and the ratio $\mathbf{P}(\ge2)/\delta$; then recompute the ratio at $\delta/2$. What does the comparison establish?   (c) Splitting one minute into $1000$ slots of length $\delta$ and calling each slot a Bernoulli trial with $p=\lambda\delta=0.002$, compare $\mathbf{P}(3\text{ arrivals in the minute})$ under the binomial and the Poisson.

Solution

(a) The linear term. Exactly, $\mathbf{P}(\ge1)=1-e^{-\lambda\delta}$ with $\lambda\delta=2\times0.001=0.002$: $$1-e^{-0.002}=1-0.998002=0.001998 \quad\text{versus}\quad \lambda\delta=0.002 .$$ The relative error is $100\times|0.001998-0.002|/0.001998=0.100033\%$ — the approximation is the first term of $1-e^{-x}=x-x^2/2+\dots$, and the discarded $x^2/2$ is what you see.

(b) "Negligible" means $o(\delta)$, not "small". Exactly, $$\mathbf{P}(\ge2)=1-e^{-\lambda\delta}(1+\lambda\delta)=1-0.998002\times1.002=1.997335\times10^{-6}.$$ The ratio to $\delta$ is $1.997335\times10^{-6}/0.001=0.001997$. Halving to $\delta=0.0005$ gives ratio $0.000999$ — also halved. So $\mathbf{P}(\ge2)/\delta\to0$: multiple arrivals vanish faster than linearly in $\delta$, which is exactly the technical meaning of "negligible" in the definition. The single-arrival probability, by contrast, has ratio $\mathbf{P}(1)/\delta\to\lambda=2$, a nonzero constant. That difference in order is the whole content of the small-interval definition.

(c) Binomial to Poisson. Binomial($1000,0.002$) at $k=3$ gives $0.180628$; Poisson($\lambda\tau=2$) at $k=3$ gives $e^{-2}2^3/3!=0.180447$. They differ by $0.000181$, about one part in a thousand — because $n=1000$ is large and $p=0.002$ is small with $np=2$ fixed, which is precisely the limit that turns the slotted Bernoulli process into the Poisson process (G4 §3.2).

Answer. $\;$(a) $0.001998$ versus $\lambda\delta=0.002$, a $0.10\%$ relative error. (b) $\mathbf{P}(\ge2)=1.9973\times10^{-6}$; the ratio to $\delta$ halves when $\delta$ halves, so $\mathbf{P}(\ge2)=o(\delta)$. (c) $0.180628$ versus $0.180447$.

Trap. Reading "negligible" as "zero" and concluding that a Poisson process can never have two arrivals in a short interval. It can — probability $2\times10^{-6}$ here, not $0$. What is asserted is only that this probability shrinks faster than $\delta$, so it contributes nothing in the limit that builds the process.

Q88. ★★ · Competing exponentials · G4 §4.2

A turbine can fail in three independent ways. Time to a bearing failure is exponential with rate $\lambda_1=0.5$/year, to a blade failure exponential with $\lambda_2=0.3$/year, to a controller failure exponential with $\lambda_3=0.2$/year, all independent. The turbine stops at the first of these, at time $T=\min(T_1,T_2,T_3)$.

(a) Find the distribution of $T$ and $\mathbb{E}[T]$.   (b) Find the probability that the blade is the cause.   (c) Find $\mathbf{P}(T>2\text{ years and the controller is the cause})$.   (d) Find $\mathbb{E}[T\mid \text{controller is the cause}]$.

Solution

Setup. Each failure mode is the first arrival of an independent Poisson process; merging them (G4 §4.2) gives a Poisson process of rate $\lambda=\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3$, and each merged arrival independently carries a label $i$ with probability $\lambda_i/\lambda$.

(a) Minimum of exponentials. $\mathbf{P}(T>t)=\prod_i\mathbf{P}(T_i>t)=e^{-\lambda_1t}e^{-\lambda_2t}e^{-\lambda_3t}=e^{-\lambda t}$, so $T$ is exponential with $$\lambda=0.5+0.3+0.2=1.0\ \text{per year},\qquad \mathbb{E}[T]=\frac{1}{1.0}=1\ \text{year}.$$ Simulation over $2\times10^6$ turbines gives $\mathbb{E}[T]\approx1.000461$.

(b) Label probability. $\mathbf{P}(\text{blade first})=\lambda_2/\lambda=0.3/1.0=0.3$. Likewise controller $=0.2/1.0=0.2$ (simulated $0.199761$).

(c) The surprise: time and cause are independent. Q89. ★★★ · Split streams really are independent · G4 §4.3

Messages arrive at a mail server as a Poisson process of rate $\lambda=12$ per hour. Independently of everything else, each message is spam with probability $p=0.25$ and legitimate otherwise. Over a fixed $2$-hour window let $N_S$ and $N_L$ be the counts of spam and legitimate messages, and $N=N_S+N_L$.

(a) Derive the joint PMF of $(N_S,N_L)$ and show that they are independent Poisson variables.   (b) Compute $\mathbf{P}(N_S=5,\ N_L=20)$.   (c) A student objects: "$N_S+N_L=N$, so a large $N_S$ forces a small $N_L$ — they must be negatively correlated." Resolve the objection.

Solution

Setup. Over $\tau=2$ hours the total count is Poisson with parameter $\lambda\tau=12\times2=24$. Given $N=n$, the $n$ messages are labelled independently, so $N_S\mid N=n$ is Binomial($n,0.25$).

(a) Derivation. Fix integers $s,\ell\ge0$ and put $n=s+\ell$. Condition on the total: $$\mathbf{P}(N_S=s,N_L=\ell)=\mathbf{P}(N=n)\,\mathbf{P}(N_S=s\mid N=n)=e^{-\lambda\tau}\frac{(\lambda\tau)^{n}}{n!}\cdot\binom{n}{s}p^s(1-p)^{\ell}.$$ Expand $\binom{n}{s}=\frac{n!}{s!\,\ell!}$; the $n!$ cancels, and splitting $(\lambda\tau)^n=(\lambda\tau)^s(\lambda\tau)^\ell$ and $e^{-\lambda\tau}=e^{-\lambda\tau p}e^{-\lambda\tau(1-p)}$ gives $$\mathbf{P}(N_S=s,N_L=\ell)=\underbrace{e^{-\lambda\tau p}\frac{(\lambda\tau p)^{s}}{s!}}_{\text{Poisson}(\lambda\tau p)}\times\underbrace{e^{-\lambda\tau(1-p)}\frac{\big(\lambda\tau(1-p)\big)^{\ell}}{\ell!}}_{\text{Poisson}(\lambda\tau(1-p))} .$$ The joint PMF factors into a function of $s$ times a function of $\ell$ for all $(s,\ell)$, which is the definition of independence; and each factor is a Poisson PMF, with parameters $$\lambda\tau p=24\times0.25=6\ \text{(spam)},\qquad \lambda\tau(1-p)=24\times0.75=18\ \text{(legitimate)}.$$

(b) Numbers. By the factorization, $$\mathbf{P}(N_S=5)=e^{-6}\frac{6^5}{5!}=0.160623,\qquad \mathbf{P}(N_L=20)=e^{-18}\frac{18^{20}}{20!}=0.079804,$$ $$\mathbf{P}(N_S=5,N_L=20)=0.160623\times0.079804=0.012818 .$$ Cross-check by the conditional route: $\mathbf{P}(N=25)\mathbf{P}(\text{Bin}(25,0.25)=5)=0.077905\times0.164538=0.012818$. ✓ A $2\times10^6$-window simulation gives $0.012845$, and the sample covariance of $(N_S,N_L)$ is $-0.002$, i.e. zero to simulation noise.

(c) Resolving the objection. The objection would be right if $N$ were fixed: given $N=25$, indeed $N_L=25-N_S$ and the correlation is $-1$. But $N$ is itself random and Poisson. A larger $N_S$ is evidence of a busier window, which raises the expected $N_L$ too; this positive effect through $N$ exactly cancels the negative "shares of a fixed pie" effect, leaving $\operatorname{cov}(N_S,N_L)=0$. The cancellation is exact only for the Poisson total — that is the special property of Poisson splitting. (Check with total variance: $\operatorname{var}(N)=24=\operatorname{var}(N_S)+\operatorname{var}(N_L)+2\operatorname{cov}=6+18+0$. ✓)

Answer. $\;$(a) $N_S\sim$ Poisson($6$) and $N_L\sim$ Poisson($18$), independent. (b) $0.012818$. (c) Conditionally on $N$ they are perfectly negatively dependent, but the randomness of the Poisson total exactly offsets it, giving $\operatorname{cov}(N_S,N_L)=0$ and full independence.

Trap. Answering (c) by computing the covariance after fixing the total: with $N=25$ held fixed, $N_L=25-N_S$ gives $\operatorname{cov}(N_S,N_L\mid N=25)=-\operatorname{var}(N_S\mid N=25)=-25(0.25)(0.75)=-4.687500$ and a correlation of exactly $-1$. That is a genuine number, but it answers a conditional question; the unconditional covariance the student was arguing about is $0$.

Q90. ★ · Splitting, then merging · G4 §4.2–4.3

(a) Find the probability of exactly $4$ urgent emails in a $3$-hour window.   (b) Given that exactly $8$ emails arrived in inbox 1 during one particular hour, find the probability that exactly $2$ of them were urgent.   (c) Considering both inboxes together, find the mean time between consecutive emails and the probability that the next email is from inbox 1.

Solution

(a) Splitting. The urgent substream is itself Poisson with rate $10\times0.20=2$ per hour (G4 §4.3). Over $\tau=3$ hours its parameter is $2\times3=6$, so $$\mathbf{P}(4\text{ urgent})=e^{-6}\frac{6^4}{4!}=0.002479\times\frac{1296}{24}=0.002479\times54=0.133853 .$$

(b) Conditioning on the total makes it binomial. Given that $8$ emails arrived, each is independently urgent with probability $0.20$, so the urgent count is Binomial($8,0.20$): $$\mathbf{P}(2)=\binom{8}{2}(0.20)^2(0.80)^6=28\times0.04\times0.262144=0.293601 .$$

(c) Merging. Two independent Poisson streams merge into a Poisson process of rate $10+4=14$ per hour (G4 §4.2). The interarrival time is exponential with mean $$\frac{1}{14}\ \text{hour}=\frac{60}{14}=4.285714\ \text{minutes},$$ and each merged arrival comes from inbox 1 with probability $\lambda_1/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)=10/14=0.714286$.

Answer. $\;$(a) $0.133853$, (b) $0.293601$, (c) $4.285714$ minutes and $0.714286$.

Trap. Answering (b) with the Poisson $\mathbf{P}(N_{\text{urg}}=2)=e^{-2}2^2/2!=0.270671$ for a one-hour window. That is the unconditional answer. Once you are told the exact total, the split count stops being Poisson and becomes binomial with that total as its $n$ — the unconditional Poisson answer $0.270671$ is not the same as $0.293601$.

Q91. ★★★ · Random incidence and length bias · G4 §4.5

Trains on a line run with i.i.d. gaps between consecutive departures: a gap is $4$ minutes with probability $0.8$ and $24$ minutes with probability $0.2$. A passenger arrives at the platform at a time unrelated to the schedule (uniform over a long horizon). Let $L$ be the length of the gap the passenger lands in and $W$ the passenger's wait for the next train.

(a) Find the mean gap between trains.   (b) Find the PMF of $L$ and $\mathbb{E}[L]$.   (c) Find $\mathbb{E}[W]$.   (d) Only $20\%$ of gaps are long. What fraction of passengers experience a long gap?

Solution

(a) The gap distribution as scheduled. $\mathbb{E}[X]=0.8(4)+0.2(24)=3.2+4.8=8$ minutes. Over a long horizon of $n$ gaps, total elapsed time is about $8n$ minutes.

(b) Length bias. A uniformly arriving passenger lands in a given gap with probability proportional to that gap's length, not to how often it occurs (G4 §4.5). Out of $n$ gaps, the $0.8n$ short ones occupy $0.8n\times4=3.2n$ minutes and the $0.2n$ long ones occupy $0.2n\times24=4.8n$ minutes. So $$\mathbf{P}(L=4)=\frac{3.2n}{8n}=0.4,\qquad \mathbf{P}(L=24)=\frac{4.8n}{8n}=0.6 .$$ Hence $$\mathbb{E}[L]=0.4(4)+0.6(24)=1.6+14.4=16\ \text{minutes}.$$ The general formula agrees: $\mathbb{E}[L]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]/\mathbb{E}[X]$ with $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=0.8(16)+0.2(576)=12.8+115.2=128$, so $\mathbb{E}[L]=128/8=16$. A simulation over $400\,000$ gaps with $2\times10^6$ uniformly placed passengers gives $\mathbb{E}[L]\approx16.033$.

(c) The wait. Given the gap the passenger lands in has length $\ell$, the arrival instant is uniform inside it, so $\mathbb{E}[W\mid L=\ell]=\ell/2$. By iterated expectations, $$\mathbb{E}[W]=\tfrac12\mathbb{E}[L]=\tfrac12(16)=8\ \text{minutes}.$$ Simulation: $8.018$ minutes.

(d) Passengers versus gaps. $\mathbf{P}(L=24)=0.6$: three passengers in five suffer a long gap, even though only one gap in five is long. The service looks three times worse from the platform than from the timetable, and both descriptions are correct.

Answer. $\;$(a) $8$ min. (b) $L=4$ w.p. $0.4$, $L=24$ w.p. $0.6$; $\mathbb{E}[L]=16$ min. (c) $\mathbb{E}[W]=8$ min. (d) $60\%$.

Trap. Answering (c) with $\mathbb{E}[X]/2=8/2=4$ minutes — "half the average gap". That halves the wrong quantity: you must halve the length-biased gap $\mathbb{E}[L]=16$, not the ordinary gap $\mathbb{E}[X]=8$. The true wait is twice the naive one here.

Q92. ★★ · Interpretation: the Poisson inspection paradox · G4 §4.4

Buses arrive at a stop as a Poisson process with rate $6$ per hour. You walk up at a time unrelated to the buses. Let $L$ be the length of the interarrival interval that contains your arrival instant.

(a) State the mean interarrival time, your expected wait for the next bus, and the expected time since the previous bus.   (b) Give $\mathbb{E}[L]$ and $\operatorname{var}(L)$.   (c) A commuter says: "$\mathbb{E}[L]=20$ minutes but buses come every $10$ minutes on average — the schedule must be lying, or the process is not Poisson." Respond.

Solution

(a) Two memoryless halves. The rate is $6$/hour, so the mean interarrival time is $60/6=10$ minutes. By memorylessness looking forward, your remaining wait is exponential with mean $10$ minutes; by the same property applied to the reversed process, the elapsed time since the previous bus is also exponential with mean $10$ minutes (G4 §4.4). The two are independent.

(b) Their sum. $L=(\text{time since last})+(\text{time to next})$, a sum of two independent Exp($\lambda$) variables, i.e. Erlang of order $2$: $$\mathbb{E}[L]=\frac{2}{\lambda}=10+10=20\ \text{minutes},\qquad \operatorname{var}(L)=\frac{2}{\lambda^2}=2\times10^2=200\ \text{min}^2,$$ so $\sigma_L=\sqrt{200}=14.142136$ minutes. A $2\times10^6$-draw simulation gives $\mathbb{E}[L]\approx20.005$.

(c) No contradiction — a different sampling scheme. Both statements are true because they average over different populations. "Buses come every $10$ minutes" averages over intervals, each counted once. "$\mathbb{E}[L]=20$" averages over arrival instants, and a long interval covers more instants, so it is more likely to be the one you land in — length bias. The ratio here is exactly $\mathbb{E}[L]/\mathbb{E}[X]=20/10=2$, the Poisson signature $\mathbb{E}[X^2]/\mathbb{E}[X]^2$ for an exponential. Nothing about the process is being misreported.

Answer. $\;$(a) mean gap $10$ min, expected forward wait $10$ min, expected backward time $10$ min. (b) $\mathbb{E}[L]=20$ min, $\operatorname{var}(L)=200$ min$^2$. (c) Both figures are correct; they average over intervals versus over time instants, and length bias makes the second twice the first.

Trap. "Since the mean gap is $10$ minutes and I arrive in the middle, my expected wait is $5$ minutes." Two errors cancel into a wrong answer: the gap you land in averages $20$, and by memorylessness the forward wait is a full $10$ minutes, not half of anything.

Q93. ★★★ · A Poisson rate that is itself random · G4 §1.3, §3.2

A website's hourly hit rate depends on whether a promotion is running: $\Lambda=20$ per hour with probability $0.7$ (ordinary hour) and $\Lambda=90$ per hour with probability $0.3$ (promoted hour). Given $\Lambda=\lambda$, the number of hits $N$ in that hour is Poisson($\lambda$).

(a) Find $\mathbb{E}[N]$ and $\operatorname{var}(N)$.   (b) Compare $\operatorname{var}(N)$ with the variance of a plain Poisson variable of the same mean, and interpret.   (c) Is $N$ Poisson? Support your answer by computing $\mathbf{P}(N=0)$.

Solution

Setup. $N$ is not Poisson with a fixed parameter; it is a mixture. Condition on $\Lambda$, where the Poisson facts $\mathbb{E}[N\mid\Lambda=\lambda]=\lambda$ and $\operatorname{var}(N\mid\Lambda=\lambda)=\lambda$ apply (G4 §3.2). Thus $\mathbb{E}[N\mid\Lambda]=\Lambda$ and $\operatorname{var}(N\mid\Lambda)=\Lambda$, both random variables.

(a1) Mean, by iterated expectations. $$\mathbb{E}[N]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[N\mid\Lambda]\big]=\mathbb{E}[\Lambda]=0.7(20)+0.3(90)=14+27=41\ \text{hits}.$$

(a2) Variance, by total variance. Within: $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(N\mid\Lambda)]=\mathbb{E}[\Lambda]=41$ — the Poisson noise itself. Between: with $\mathbb{E}[\Lambda^2]=0.7(400)+0.3(8100)=280+2430=2710$, $$\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[N\mid\Lambda]\big)=\operatorname{var}(\Lambda)=2710-41^2=2710-1681=1029 .$$ Adding, $$\operatorname{var}(N)=41+1029=1070,\qquad \sigma_N=\sqrt{1070}=32.710854 .$$ A $2\times10^6$-hour simulation gives $\mathbb{E}[N]\approx41.017$ and $\operatorname{var}(N)\approx1070.66$.

(b) Overdispersion. A plain Poisson($41$) would have $\operatorname{var}=41$ and $\sigma=6.403124$. The mixture's variance is $1070/41=26.097561$ times larger. Almost all of the excess ($1029$ of $1070$, i.e. $96\%$) is the between term: the dominant uncertainty is not "how many hits at a known rate" but "which regime am I in". Fitting a single Poisson to such data always underestimates the spread — the standard tell of an unmodelled mixture.

(c) Not Poisson. Any Poisson variable has variance equal to its mean; here $1070\ne41$, so $N$ cannot be Poisson. Concretely, $$\mathbf{P}(N=0)=0.7e^{-20}+0.3e^{-90}=0.7\times2.0612\times10^{-9}+\text{(about }10^{-40})=1.442808\times10^{-9},$$ whereas a Poisson($41$) would give $e^{-41}=1.562882\times10^{-18}$ — nine orders of magnitude smaller. The mixture is far more likely to be quiet, because it can be in the low-rate regime.

Answer. $\;\mathbb{E}[N]=41$, $\;\operatorname{var}(N)=41+1029=1070$ ($\sigma_N=32.710854$); $\;26.10\times$ the Poisson variance; $N$ is not Poisson, e.g. $\mathbf{P}(N=0)=1.442808\times10^{-9}$.

Trap. Computing the average rate $41$ and declaring $N\sim$ Poisson($41$) with $\operatorname{var}=41$. Substituting a mean for a random parameter is legitimate for the mean here (because $\mathbb{E}[N\mid\Lambda]$ is linear in $\Lambda$) but destroys the entire between term $1029$ in the variance.

Tool: G4 §1.3 (law of total variance), §3.2 (Poisson mean and variance) · B&T §4.3, §6.2

Q94. ★ · Which tool applies, and why · G4 §5.1

For each of the five situations below, name the single tool from G4 that resolves it and give a one-line reason. Do not compute anything.

(i) A courier job is handled by one of three depots with known probabilities, and each depot has a known mean delivery time; you want the overall mean.   (ii) Daily revenue is the sum of the amounts spent by a random number of customers; you want its variance.   (iii) You show up at a bus stop at a time unrelated to the timetable and want the expected length of the interval you land in.   (iv) Test scores pooled over several classes vary far more than scores within any one class; you want to quantify the split.   (v) A Poisson-arrival hotline has been silent for $10$ minutes; you want the distribution of the additional wait.

Solution

(i) Law of iterated expectations (G4 §1.2). You are given a PMF over "which depot" plus a conditional mean for each; $\mathbb{E}[T]=\sum_s\mathbf{P}(S=s)\mathbb{E}[T\mid S=s]$ is precisely that data assembled. Nothing about variances is needed.

(ii) Random-sum variance (G4 §2.2): $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)+(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N)$. Two independent sources of randomness — the number of terms and each term — and the formula keeps both. Using $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)$ would treat the customer count as fixed.

(iii) Random incidence / length bias (G4 §4.4–4.5). Sampling by time rather than by interval makes long intervals overrepresented in proportion to their length, giving $\mathbb{E}[L]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]/\mathbb{E}[X]\ge\mathbb{E}[X]$.

(iv) Law of total variance (G4 §1.3): $\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(X\mid C)]+\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid C])$, with $C$ the class. The first term is the within-class scatter, the second the spread of the class averages — exactly the split being asked for.

(v) Memorylessness / fresh start (G4 §3.3, §2.4). The additional wait of a Poisson process, given any elapsed silence, is exponential with the original rate; the silent $10$ minutes carry no information.

Answer. $\;$(i) iterated expectations; (ii) random-sum variance formula; (iii) random incidence / length bias; (iv) law of total variance; (v) memorylessness of the exponential (fresh start).

Trap. The two tempting mis-picks. In (iii), reaching for the ordinary mean gap $\mathbb{E}[X]$ — that is the average over intervals, whereas arriving at a random time gives $\mathbb{E}[L]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]/\mathbb{E}[X]$, strictly larger unless every gap is the same length. In (v), reasoning that ten silent minutes make a call "overdue" and shorten the remaining wait — memorylessness says the additional wait is exponential with the original mean $1/\lambda$, neither shorter nor longer.
