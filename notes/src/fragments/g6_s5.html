§5   Synthesis: one page of tools, five gotchas, and the bridge to classical inference

Sources: L19 slides 2, 4–6, 8 · L20 slides 2, 4, 6–7 · L21 slides 2–5 · L22 slides 1, 4–6, 8–9 (the slides the two master tables summarize) · B&T §5.1–5.4 and §8.1–8.4, with the bridge pointing at §9.1. Every number on this page is recomputed in computes/g6_s5.py (exact Fraction arithmetic where the answer is rational, scipy.stats for the exact binomial and Beta quantities, a four-million-draw Monte-Carlo cross-check on the estimation example) and the three figures by computes/g6_s5_figs.py.

G6 covered two apparently unrelated stories. §1–§2 asked what happens to a sum or an average of many independent pieces, and answered with a ladder of tools — Markov, Chebyshev, the weak law, the central limit theorem — each buying a sharper statement with a stronger hypothesis. §3–§4 asked how to turn an observation into a statement about an unknown quantity, and answered with the posterior distribution and three ways to collapse it to a single number. This section puts each story on one page, then says what the two have in common: both are machinery for converting what you know into what you may claim, and in both the fatal error is claiming more than your hypotheses bought.

5.1 The inequality-and-limit table

Fix the notation once, since all four rows share it. $X$ is a random variable with mean $\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]$ and variance $\sigma^2=\operatorname{var}(X)$, both assumed finite; $X_1,X_2,\dots$ are i.i.d. copies of $X$; $S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n$ is their sum, with $\mathbb{E}[S_n]=n\mu$ and $\operatorname{var}(S_n)=n\sigma^2$ (independence, so variances add); $M_n=S_n/n$ is the sample mean, with $\mathbb{E}[M_n]=\mu$ and $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n$; $Z_n=(S_n-n\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt n)$ is the standardized sum, with mean $0$ and variance $1$ for every $n$; $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF; $a$, $c$ and $\epsilon$ are positive constants chosen by you.

ToolStatementWhat it needsWhat it givesSource
Markov $\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)\le \dfrac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}$ for $a>0$ $X\ge 0$ and the mean. Nothing else — no variance, no shape. A bound, true for every distribution meeting the hypothesis. Usually very loose. §1; B&T §5.1 (Markov is on no L19 slide)
Chebyshev $\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge c)\le \dfrac{\sigma^2}{c^2}$; with $c=k\sigma$, $\le 1/k^2$ Mean and variance. $X$ may take either sign. A two-sided bound. Beats Markov once you are several $\sigma$ out, still loose. L19 slide 2; §1; B&T §5.1
Weak law (WLLN) $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}\to 0$ for every fixed $\epsilon>0$ I.i.d. terms, finite mean and variance. (Finite mean alone suffices, but then you lose the rate.) Convergence in probability of the sample mean, plus an explicit $n$ that meets a stated tolerance. L19 slide 5; §1; B&T §5.2–5.3
Central limit theorem $\mathbf{P}(Z_n\le c)\to\Phi(c)$ for every $c$; in practice $\mathbf{P}(S_n\le s)\approx\Phi\!\big(\tfrac{s-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\big)$ I.i.d. terms, finite variance, $n$ large. Only $\mu$ and $\sigma$ of the summands matter. A number, not a bound — an approximation to a CDF, with no error guarantee attached. L19 slide 8, L20 slide 2; §2; B&T §5.4

Read the table down the "what it needs" column and the ladder is obvious: each row assumes strictly more than the row above and returns a strictly sharper statement. Markov knows only the mean; Chebyshev adds the variance; the weak law adds independence and identical distribution; the CLT adds a large $n$ and, in exchange for giving up the guarantee, hands back an actual number.

Fig. 5.1 — Which inequality or limit theorem? The single question that decides it is whether you owe a guarantee or a number (L19 slides 2, 5; L20 slide 2; B&T §5.1–5.4).
Recipe — picking a tool for a tail probability (L19 slides 2, 5; L20 slide 2).
  1. Ask what the answer is for. A promise to a client ("the error will not exceed…") must be a bound; a design number ("how many samples do I buy?") can be either; a reported probability is almost always the CLT.
  2. Check the hypotheses you actually have. Only a mean, and $X\ge0$? Markov is the only legal move. Mean and variance? Chebyshev. Also i.i.d. and a sample mean? Weak law. Also $n$ large? CLT.
  3. If the quantity is a sample mean, write $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n$ first. Every WLLN computation is Chebyshev applied to $M_n$ with that variance; there is no second idea.
  4. Bound the unknown variance if you must. For a Bernoulli$(f)$ poll, $\sigma^2=f(1-f)\le 1/4$, attained at $f=1/2$; using $1/4$ keeps the bound valid whatever $f$ turns out to be (L19 slide 6).
  5. Standardize before using a table. Subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation, then and only then read $\Phi$. For an integer-valued $S_n$, apply the $\tfrac12$ correction (§2; L20 slide 6).
  6. Sanity-check the output. A "probability" above 1 means the bound is vacuous, not wrong — report it as "no useful bound" rather than as an answer.
Example 5.1 (the same tail, four ways — L19 slide 2; B&T §5.1). Let $X$ be exponential with parameter $\lambda=1$, so $\mu=1$ and $\sigma=1$. Bound and then compute $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 4)$ with each tool in the table, and say what the comparison teaches.
Solution

Markov. $X\ge 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[X]=1$, so with $a=4$, $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge 4)\le\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{4}=\frac{1}{4}=0.25 .$$

Chebyshev. The event $\{X\ge4\}$ is contained in $\{|X-\mu|\ge 3\}$, because $X\ge4$ forces $X-1\ge3$. Monotonicity of probability then lets us bound the smaller event by the larger one, and with $\sigma^2=1$, $c=3$: $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge 4)\le\mathbf{P}(|X-1|\ge 3)\le\frac{\sigma^2}{c^2}=\frac{1}{9}=0.111111 .$$

Exact. The exponential survival function gives $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge 4)=e^{-4}=0.01831564 .$$

Reading the three. Markov overstates the truth by a factor $0.25/0.01831564=13.6$, Chebyshev by $0.111111/0.01831564=6.1$. Push out to $a=10$ and the overstatement becomes $2203\times$ and $272\times$ respectively (Figure 5.2, left): bounds degrade in the far tail, exactly where you most want them. There is no CLT row here, because $X$ is a single random variable and not a sum — the CLT has nothing to say about it. That is the point of the flowchart: the CLT is not "a better Chebyshev", it answers a different question.

Why the bounds cannot be improved. Neither inequality is sloppy; both are tight over the class of distributions satisfying their hypotheses. For Markov, take $X=a$ with probability $\mu/a$ and $X=0$ otherwise: the mean is $a\cdot(\mu/a)+0=\mu$ as required, and $\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)=\mu/a$ exactly — at $a=4,\mu=1$ that is $0.25$, the bound. For Chebyshev with $k=2$, take $X=\pm 2\sigma$ each with probability $1/(2k^2)=0.125$ and $X=0$ with probability $1-1/k^2=0.75$: the mean is $0$ by symmetry, the variance is $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=2(0.125)(2\sigma)^2+0.75(0)^2=\sigma^2$ — verified as $1.0$ in the computes for $\sigma=1$ — and $\mathbf{P}(|X|\ge2\sigma)=2(0.125)=0.25=1/k^2$ exactly. The looseness you see on the exponential is the price of a statement that must also cover these two adversaries.

Example 5.2 (the pollster, priced twice — L19 slide 6, L20 slide 4; B&T §5.2, §5.4). A fraction $f$ of the population answers yes. You poll $n$ people independently, $X_i=1$ for yes and $0$ for no, and report $M_n=(X_1+\cdots+X_n)/n$. You want $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-f|\ge 0.01)\le 0.05$ — one percentage point of error, ninety-five percent confidence. How large must $n$ be, by Chebyshev and by the CLT?
Solution

Setup. $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=f$ and $\operatorname{var}(X_i)=f(1-f)$, which is at most $1/4=0.25$ over $f\in[0,1]$ (the parabola peaks at $f=1/2$). Since $f$ is precisely what you do not know, use the worst case $\sigma^2=1/4$; this only makes the requirement more conservative. Then $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n\le 1/(4n)$.

Chebyshev route. With $\epsilon=0.01$, $$\mathbf{P}(|M_n-f|\ge 0.01)\le\frac{\operatorname{var}(M_n)}{\epsilon^2} =\frac{1/(4n)}{(0.01)^2}=\frac{1}{4n(0.0001)}=\frac{2500}{n}.$$ Demanding $2500/n\le 0.05$ gives $n\ge 2500/0.05=50000$. Check at that value: the bound is $2500/50000=0.05$ exactly ✓, while at $n=10000$ it is $2500/10000=0.25$ — five times the tolerance, so ten thousand respondents provably will not do by this argument.

CLT route. Standardize. $M_n-f$ has standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt n$, so $$\mathbf{P}(|M_n-f|\ge 0.01)=\mathbf{P}\!\left(\frac{|M_n-f|}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\ge \frac{0.01\sqrt n}{\sigma}\right)\approx\mathbf{P}\!\left(|Z|\ge\frac{0.01\sqrt n}{\sigma}\right) =2\Big(1-\Phi\big(\tfrac{0.01\sqrt n}{\sigma}\big)\Big),$$ using the symmetry of the normal in the last step. Setting this equal to $0.05$ requires $0.01\sqrt n/\sigma=z_{0.975}=1.959964$, the $97.5$th standard normal percentile. With $\sigma=1/2$, $$\sqrt n=\frac{1.959964\times 0.5}{0.01}=97.998,\qquad n=9603.647\ \Rightarrow\ n=9604 .$$

The gap. $50000/9604=5.21$. Chebyshev charges you five times as many respondents for the same promise (Figure 5.2, right). Worse — or better, depending on your budget — a poll of $50000$ people actually achieves, by the CLT, an error probability of about $7.74\times 10^{-6}$, not $0.05$: the Chebyshev-sized poll is roughly six thousand times more accurate than its own guarantee claims. Neither number is wrong. Chebyshev's is a theorem about all distributions with variance $\le 1/4$; the CLT's is an approximation that exploits the extra fact that $M_n$ is a normalized sum and is therefore nearly normal. You pay for certainty with sample size.

Fig. 5.2 — What looseness costs. Left: on $X\sim\text{Exp}(1)$ both bounds sit far above the truth and the gap widens in the tail (Example 5.1). Right: the same $\pm1\%$, $95\%$ promise priced by Chebyshev and by the CLT (Example 5.2; L19 slide 6, L20 slide 4). Numbers from computes/g6_s5.py.
Interpretation — a bound and an approximation are different products. A bound is a contract: it is never violated, it holds for the worst distribution consistent with your hypotheses, and it is therefore priced for that worst case. An approximation is a forecast: it is usually excellent, it comes with no promise, and it can fail badly when its own hypotheses (many terms, comparable sizes, finite variance) are strained. Engineering practice uses both — the CLT to choose $n$, Chebyshev to state what you can defend if challenged.
Practice 5.1. A repair time $X$ is nonnegative with mean $\mathbb{E}[X]=10$ minutes and variance $\operatorname{var}(X)=25$; nothing else about its distribution is known. Bound $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 40)$ by Markov and by Chebyshev, and say which hypothesis buys the improvement.
Solution

Markov. $X\ge0$ and the mean is $10$, so with $a=40$, $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge 40)\le\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{40}=\frac{10}{40}=0.25 .$$

Chebyshev. Chebyshev bounds a two-sided event, so first embed the one-sided event: $X\ge40$ forces $X-10\ge30$, hence $\{X\ge40\}\subseteq\{|X-10|\ge30\}$ and monotonicity of probability gives $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge 40)\le\mathbf{P}(|X-10|\ge 30)\le\frac{\operatorname{var}(X)}{30^2} =\frac{25}{900}=0.027778 .$$

Comparison. Chebyshev is $0.25/0.027778=9.0$ times tighter, and the extra hypothesis paying for it is exactly one number — the variance. With the mean alone Markov is the only legal move (§1); with mean and variance you should always take Chebyshev. Note that both are still "at most" statements: the true probability could be anything from $0$ to $0.027778$.

Practice 5.2. You average $n$ i.i.d. measurements of an unknown constant. Each has variance $\sigma^2=4$. You want $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge 0.5)\le 0.01$. Find $n$ by the weak law (Chebyshev) and by the CLT, and then check what Chebyshev says about the CLT's $n$.
Solution

Chebyshev / WLLN route. $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n=4/n$, so $$\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge 0.5)\le\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}=\frac{4}{n(0.25)}=\frac{16}{n},$$ and $16/n\le0.01$ requires $n\ge 16/0.01=1600$.

CLT route. Standardizing, $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\approx 2\big(1-\Phi(\epsilon\sqrt n/\sigma)\big)$; setting this to $0.01$ needs $\epsilon\sqrt n/\sigma=z_{0.995}=2.575829$, so $$\sqrt n=\frac{2.575829\times 2}{0.5}=10.303,\qquad n=106.158\ \Rightarrow\ n=107 .$$

The check. $1600/107=14.95$: the guarantee costs fifteen times the data. And at $n=107$ the Chebyshev bound is $16/107=0.149533$ — fifteen times the tolerance, so Chebyshev cannot certify the CLT's sample size even though the CLT's answer is, for any remotely normal-ish summand, correct. This is the pollster of Example 5.2 in miniature: the ratio is not a fixed $5.21$, it depends on the confidence level demanded (here $99\%$ rather than $95\%$, and the Chebyshev penalty grows as the tail requirement tightens, because $1/\delta$ grows faster than $z_{1-\delta/2}^2$).

5.2 The Bayesian-estimation table

Again fix the notation. $\Theta$ is the unknown, treated as a random variable with prior $p_\Theta$ (discrete) or $f_\Theta$ (continuous) — this is what makes the framework Bayesian. $X$ is the observation, linked to $\Theta$ by the model $p_{X|\Theta}$ or $f_{X|\Theta}$; $x$ is the value actually seen. $\hat\Theta=g(X)$ is an estimator, a rule turning data into a guess, and $\hat\theta=g(x)$ is the resulting estimate, a number. $\tilde\Theta=\hat\Theta-\Theta$ is the estimation error. All four rows below are read off the same posterior, so the row you pick is a choice of what to report, not a choice of model.

ObjectFormulaWhat it needsWhat it optimizes / givesSource
Posterior $f_{\Theta|X}(\theta|x)=\dfrac{f_\Theta(\theta)\,f_{X|\Theta}(x|\theta)}{f_X(x)}$,   $f_X(x)=\displaystyle\int f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X|\Theta}(x|\theta)\,d\theta$ (and the three discrete/mixed variants) Prior and observation model; the ability to do the normalizing sum or integral. Everything. It is the complete answer; each row below is a summary of it. L21 slides 3–4; §3; B&T §8.1
MAP estimate $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\max_\theta f_{\Theta|X}(\theta|x)$ — equivalently $\arg\max_\theta f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X|\Theta}(x|\theta)$, since $f_X(x)$ does not depend on $\theta$ The posterior up to a constant only. No integration needed. Minimizes $\mathbf{P}(\hat\Theta\neq\Theta)$ when $\Theta$ is discrete — the natural choice for hypothesis testing. L21 slide 5, L22 slide 1; §3; B&T §8.2
LMS estimate $\hat\Theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$; for each $x$, $\hat\theta=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$ The full posterior, and one integration against it. Minimizes $\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-g(X))^2]$ over all functions $g$. Resulting MSE $=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)]$. L21 slides 6–7, L22 slide 1; §4; B&T §8.3
Linear LMS $\hat\Theta_L=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+\dfrac{\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)}{\operatorname{var}(X)}\big(X-\mathbb{E}[X]\big)$ Five numbers: $\mathbb{E}[\Theta],\mathbb{E}[X],\operatorname{var}(X),\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)$ for the estimator itself, plus $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$ to report its MSE. No posterior, no integral. Minimizes $\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-aX-b)^2]$ over $a,b$. Resulting MSE $=(1-\rho^2)\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$, where $\rho$ is the correlation coefficient. L22 slides 5–6; §4; B&T §8.4

Two structural remarks tie the rows together. First, the error decomposition $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta)+\operatorname{var}(\tilde\Theta)$ (L22 slide 4) says the prior variance splits into the part the data explained and the part left over — so the MSE of the LMS estimator can never exceed $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$, the MSE of ignoring the data and reporting $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]$. Second, the linear-LMS MSE formula $(1-\rho^2)\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$ makes the same statement quantitatively for the restricted class: $\rho^2$ is literally the fraction of the prior variance a straight line can remove.

Fig. 5.3 — Which Bayesian estimator? The left branch is available whenever you can do the Bayes integral; the right branch is what remains when you cannot (L21 slides 3–5; L22 slides 1, 5–6; B&T §8.1–8.4).
Recipe — choosing an estimator (L21 slide 5; L22 slides 1, 5–6).
  1. Is $\Theta$ discrete or continuous? Discrete and you must commit to one value: MAP, because it minimizes the probability of being wrong. Continuous and errors are graded by size: LMS.
  2. Can you normalize the posterior? If the integral $f_X(x)=\int f_\Theta f_{X|\Theta}\,d\theta$ is doable, you have the whole posterior and every row is open. If not, MAP is still available — it needs the numerator only.
  3. Do you only know first and second moments? Then linear LMS is not a compromise, it is the whole of what the information supports. Compute the four numbers the estimator needs — $\mathbb{E}[\Theta],\mathbb{E}[X],\operatorname{var}(X),\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)$ — and substitute; add the fifth, $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$, as soon as you want to quote the resulting MSE $(1-\rho^2)\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$. Five numbers in all: two means, two variances, one covariance (§4).
  4. Before reporting, quote a spread. $\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X=x)$ for LMS, $(1-\rho^2)\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$ for linear LMS. An estimate without a spread hides whether the data did anything at all.
  5. If everything in sight is normal, stop early. With a normal prior and normal noise the posterior is normal, so its mode equals its mean: MAP $=$ LMS $=$ linear LMS, all three the same weighted average $\hat\Theta_L=\big(\mu/\sigma_0^2+\sum_i X_i/\sigma_i^2\big)\big/\big(1/\sigma_0^2+\sum_i 1/\sigma_i^2\big)$ (L22 slide 8, "the cleanest linear LMS example").
Example 5.3 (three summaries of one posterior — L21 slides 4–5, L22 slide 1; B&T §8.2–8.3). A coin has unknown bias $\Theta$ with a uniform prior on $[0,1]$. You toss it $5$ times and see $5$ heads. Compute the posterior, its MAP estimate, its conditional mean, and the mean squared error of each; then say which you would report.
Solution

The posterior. Given $\Theta=\theta$, the number of heads $K$ in $n=5$ independent tosses is binomial, so $\mathbf{P}(K=5\mid\Theta=\theta)=\binom{5}{5}\theta^5(1-\theta)^0=\theta^5$. The prior is $f_\Theta(\theta)=1$ on $[0,1]$. Bayes for a continuous unknown with discrete data (L21 slide 4) gives $$f_{\Theta|K}(\theta\mid 5)=\frac{f_\Theta(\theta)\,\mathbf{P}(K=5\mid\Theta=\theta)} {\int_0^1 f_\Theta(t)\,\mathbf{P}(K=5\mid\Theta=t)\,dt} =\frac{\theta^5}{\int_0^1 t^5\,dt}=\frac{\theta^5}{1/6}=6\theta^5,\qquad 0\le\theta\le1 .$$ This is the Beta$(6,1)$ density. It is strictly increasing on $[0,1]$ and heavily skewed (skewness $-1.283$).

MAP. Maximize $6\theta^5$ over $[0,1]$: the derivative $30\theta^4$ is positive throughout, so the maximum is at the right endpoint, $$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=1 .$$ The most probable single value of the bias, after five heads, is "the coin always comes up heads".

Conditional mean. Integrate against the posterior: $$\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid K=5]=\int_0^1\theta\cdot 6\theta^5\,d\theta=6\int_0^1\theta^6\,d\theta =6\cdot\frac{1}{7}=\frac{6}{7}=0.857143 .$$ The posterior median, for comparison, is $0.890899$ (from $\int_0^m 6\theta^5 d\theta=m^6=1/2$, i.e. $m=2^{-1/6}$).

Posterior variance and the two MSEs. The second moment is $\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2\mid K=5]=6\int_0^1\theta^7d\theta=6/8=0.75$, so $$\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid K=5)=0.75-\Big(\tfrac67\Big)^2=0.75-0.734694=0.015306 =\frac{6}{392}.$$ For any fixed report $c$, the conditional MSE is $\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-c)^2\mid K=5]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2\mid\cdot]-2c\,\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid\cdot]+c^2$. At $c=6/7$ this is the posterior variance $0.015306$ — the minimum, as the theory promises. At $c=1$ (the MAP value) it is $$0.75-2(1)(0.857143)+1=0.035714=\tfrac{1}{28},$$ which is $2.33$ times larger. At the median $c=0.890899$ it is $0.016446$.

Which to report. If you must place a bet on a single value and are scored on squared error — "how far off is my estimate of the bias?" — report $6/7$. Do not promote the MAP value $1$ into an answer to the yes/no question "is this coin two-headed?": here $\Theta$ is continuous with a uniform prior, so $\mathbf{P}(\Theta=1)=0$, and therefore $\mathbf{P}(\hat\Theta=\Theta)=0$ for every estimator — MAP's error-minimizing property (§3; Gotcha 4 below) is vacuous in this model, because there is no rule it could beat. A genuine "two-headed?" test needs a different prior, one with an atom at $\theta=1$ — say $\mathbf{P}(\Theta=1)=q$ and the remaining mass $1-q$ spread uniformly on $[0,1]$ — and only then does the posterior probability of the hypothesis become a number you can compare against $1/2$. Under the uniform prior actually assumed here that probability is $0$. If you can report anything at all, report the posterior $6\theta^5$: it tells the reader both numbers, and it tells them the estimate is skewed, which neither single number does.

Example 5.4 (what linearity costs — L22 slides 5–6; B&T §8.4). Let $\Theta$ be uniform on $[0,1]$ and let the observation be $X=\Theta^2$ — noiseless, but a nonlinear function of the unknown. Compute the LMS estimator and its MSE, then the linear LMS estimator and its MSE, and compare.
Solution

LMS. Given $X=x$, the value of $\Theta$ is determined: since $\Theta\ge0$, $\Theta=\sqrt X$. Hence $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]=\sqrt X$ and the mean squared error is $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\sqrt X)^2\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\Theta)^2\big]=0 .$$ The data determine the unknown exactly, so the optimal estimator is perfect.

Linear LMS — the five ingredients. Moments of a uniform on $[0,1]$ are $\mathbb{E}[\Theta^k]=1/(k+1)$, so $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=1/2$, $\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2]=1/3$, $\mathbb{E}[\Theta^3]=1/4$, $\mathbb{E}[\Theta^4]=1/5$. Then $$\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=\tfrac13-\tfrac14=\tfrac1{12}=0.083333,$$ $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2]=\tfrac13,\qquad \operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[\Theta^4]-\big(\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2]\big)^2 =\tfrac15-\tfrac19=\tfrac{9-5}{45}=\tfrac{4}{45}=0.088889,$$ $$\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)=\mathbb{E}[\Theta^3]-\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2]\mathbb{E}[\Theta] =\tfrac14-\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac14-\tfrac16=\tfrac{1}{12}=0.083333 .$$

The estimator. Substituting into the formula, $$a=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)}{\operatorname{var}(X)}=\frac{1/12}{4/45} =\frac{45}{48}=\frac{15}{16}=0.9375,\qquad b=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-a\,\mathbb{E}[X]=\tfrac12-\tfrac{15}{16}\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac12-\tfrac{5}{16} =\tfrac{3}{16}=0.1875,$$ so $\hat\Theta_L=0.9375\,X+0.1875$. A least-squares fit to four million simulated pairs returns $a=0.93736$, $b=0.18757$ ✓.

The MSE. The squared correlation coefficient is $$\rho^2=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)^2}{\operatorname{var}(X)\operatorname{var}(\Theta)} =\frac{(1/12)^2}{(4/45)(1/12)}=\frac{1/144}{1/135}=\frac{135}{144}=\frac{15}{16}=0.9375,$$ so $\rho=0.968246$, and $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\hat\Theta_L)^2\big]=(1-\rho^2)\operatorname{var}(\Theta) =\tfrac{1}{16}\cdot\tfrac{1}{12}=\tfrac{1}{192}=0.005208$$ (simulation: $0.005200$ ✓).

Comparison. Doing nothing — reporting the prior mean $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=0.5$ — has MSE $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=0.083333$. The straight line removes $93.75\%$ of that, down to $0.005208$. The optimal estimator removes $100\%$, down to $0$. So linear LMS is very good here and still infinitely worse than the truth in relative terms. The moral: $\rho$ measures linear association only, and a $\rho$ of $0.97$ still leaves a residual that is entirely explainable — by a curve. Linear LMS is the right tool when you genuinely lack the posterior, not when you are merely avoiding an integral you could do.

Interpretation — the four rows are four levels of surrender. The posterior surrenders nothing. The conditional mean surrenders the shape and keeps the best single number under squared error. MAP surrenders the shape and keeps the most likely single number. Linear LMS surrenders the posterior itself and keeps only what five moments can express. Each step down costs accuracy and buys computability; the exam question, or the engineering budget, tells you where to stop.
Practice 5.3. A coin with unknown bias $\Theta$, uniform prior on $[0,1]$, is tossed $3$ times and shows $2$ heads. Find the posterior, its MAP estimate, its mean, the posterior variance, and the MSE penalty for reporting the MAP instead of the mean.
Solution

Posterior. $\mathbf{P}(K=2\mid\Theta=\theta)=\binom32\theta^2(1-\theta)=3\theta^2(1-\theta)$; the factor $3$ and the prior density $1$ are constants in $\theta$, so $$f_{\Theta|K}(\theta\mid2)=\frac{\theta^2(1-\theta)}{\int_0^1 t^2(1-t)\,dt} =\frac{\theta^2(1-\theta)}{\tfrac13-\tfrac14}=\frac{\theta^2(1-\theta)}{1/12} =12\,\theta^2(1-\theta),\qquad 0\le\theta\le1,$$ the Beta$(3,2)$ density.

MAP. Maximize $\theta^2-\theta^3$: the derivative $2\theta-3\theta^2=\theta(2-3\theta)$ vanishes at $\theta=2/3$ (and at the boundary $\theta=0$, a minimum), so $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=2/3=0.666667$. Unlike Example 5.3 the maximum is interior, because the one tail keeps the density from rising to the endpoint.

Mean and variance. For Beta$(a,b)$, $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=a/(a+b)$ and $\mathbb{E}[\Theta^2]=a(a+1)/\big((a+b)(a+b+1)\big)$; with $a=3,b=2$, $$\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid K=2]=\tfrac35=0.6,\qquad \mathbb{E}[\Theta^2\mid K=2]=\frac{3\cdot4}{5\cdot6}=0.4,$$ $$\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid K=2)=0.4-0.36=0.04=\tfrac1{25}.$$ (The posterior median is $0.614272$, between the two — the posterior is only mildly skewed.)

The penalty. $\mathbb{E}[(\Theta-c)^2\mid K=2]=0.4-2c(0.6)+c^2$; at $c=2/3$ this is $0.4-0.8+0.444444=0.044444$ against $0.04$ at the mean, a factor $1.11$. Compare Example 5.3's $2.33$: the penalty for reporting the mode tracks the skew of the posterior, and this posterior is far less skewed than Beta$(6,1)$.

Practice 5.4. Let $\Theta$ be uniform on $[0,1]$ and $W$ uniform on $[-1,1]$, independent, and observe $X=\Theta+W$. Compute the linear LMS estimator and its MSE. Then find $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ exactly and say how much the linearity restriction actually cost here.
Solution

The moments. $\mathbb{E}[\Theta]=1/2$ and $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)=1/12=0.083333$; $\mathbb{E}[W]=0$ and $\operatorname{var}(W)=2^2/12=1/3$. Hence $\mathbb{E}[X]=1/2$ and, by independence (variances add), $$\operatorname{var}(X)=\tfrac1{12}+\tfrac13=\tfrac5{12}=0.416667,\qquad \operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)=\operatorname{cov}(\Theta+W,\Theta)=\operatorname{var}(\Theta) =\tfrac1{12}=0.083333,$$ the last step because $\operatorname{cov}(W,\Theta)=0$ under independence.

The estimator. $$a=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)}{\operatorname{var}(X)}=\frac{1/12}{5/12}=\tfrac15=0.2, \qquad b=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]-a\,\mathbb{E}[X]=\tfrac12-\tfrac15\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac25=0.4,$$ so $\hat\Theta_L=0.2X+0.4$ (least squares on four million simulated pairs: $a=0.20022$, $b=0.40003$ ✓). The estimator shrinks hard toward the prior mean $0.5$, correctly: the noise variance is four times the prior variance.

Its MSE. $\rho^2=\operatorname{cov}^2/(\operatorname{var}(X)\operatorname{var}(\Theta)) =(1/144)/\big((5/12)(1/12)\big)=1/5=0.2$, so $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\Theta-\hat\Theta_L)^2\big]=(1-\rho^2)\operatorname{var}(\Theta) =0.8\times\tfrac1{12}=\tfrac1{15}=0.066667$$ (simulation $0.066624$ ✓), against $0.083333$ for ignoring the data: the observation removes only $20\%$ of the prior variance.

The exact LMS estimator. Given $X=x$, the posterior density is proportional to $f_\Theta(\theta)f_W(x-\theta)=1\cdot\tfrac12$ on the set where both factors are nonzero, i.e. $\theta\in[\max(0,x-1),\min(1,x+1)]$ — so $\Theta\mid X=x$ is uniform on that overlap and $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$ is its midpoint. Explicitly $\tfrac{x+1}{2}$ for $-1\le x\le0$, $\tfrac12$ for $0\le x\le1$, and $\tfrac{x}{2}$ for $1\le x\le2$: piecewise linear with a flat middle, not a single straight line. Its MSE is $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)]=\int L(x)^3/24\,dx=1/16=0.0625$, where $L(x)$ is the length of the overlap (simulation $0.062470$ ✓).

What linearity cost. $0.066667/0.0625=1.07$ — seven percent. Contrast Example 5.4, where the same restriction turned a perfect estimator into one with positive MSE. The moral is that the cost of the linear restriction is a property of the model, not a constant; here the best rule is nearly linear, so the cheap estimator is nearly optimal.

5.3 The five gotchas that cost the most

Gotcha 1 — Chebyshev is a guarantee, not an estimate, and it is often uselessly loose. At $k=2$ the bound $\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge2\sigma)\le 1/4=0.25$ is honest, but the actual tail is $0.045500$ for a normal and $0.049787$ for an exponential — five times smaller. At $k=3$ the bound is $0.111111$ against a normal tail of $0.002700$, a factor of $41$. For a uniform on $[0,1]$ the true probability at $k=2$ is exactly $0$, since $2\sigma=0.577>0.5$ puts the event outside the support entirely. Never quote a Chebyshev bound as "the probability"; quote it as "at most". And never conclude from a loose bound that an event is likely — a bound of $0.9$ is compatible with a true probability of $0$.
Gotcha 2 — the CLT approximates CDFs, not PMFs or PDFs. The theorem says $\mathbf{P}(Z_n\le c)\to\Phi(c)$, a statement about cumulative probabilities only (L20 slide 2, where the slide spells out "not a statement about convergence of PDFs or PMFs"). Applied to $S_n\sim$ Binomial$(36,0.5)$, with $\mu=18$ and $\sigma=3$: the exact $\mathbf{P}(S_n\le 21)$ is $0.878508$, and $\Phi\big(\tfrac{21-18}{3}\big)=\Phi(1)=0.841345$ — an error of $0.037163$, entirely because the discrete CDF jumps at $21$ while the normal one does not. The $\tfrac12$ correction repairs it: $\Phi\big(\tfrac{21.5-18}{3}\big)=\Phi(1.166667)=0.878327$, now off by $0.000180$, a $206$-fold improvement. For a PMF value there is no theorem at all, only the De Moivre–Laplace practice of integrating the normal across a unit window (L20 slide 7): $\mathbf{P}(S_n=19)$ is exactly $0.125110$, and $\Phi(0.5)-\Phi(0.166667)=0.125279$ recovers it, while naively reading the normal density at $19$ gives $0.125794$. (The slide rounds the lower $z$ to $0.17$ and reads $0.6915-0.5675=0.1240$ from the four-digit table, printing $0.124$; with the exact $z=\tfrac16$ the value is $0.125279$. The discrepancy is table rounding, not a different method.) Rule: with a lattice-valued sum, always spread each integer over $[k-\tfrac12,k+\tfrac12]$.
Gotcha 3 — convergence in probability is not convergence of anything else (L19 slide 4). Let $Y_n=n$ with probability $1/n$ and $Y_n=0$ otherwise — the example posed on the slide itself. For any $\epsilon\in(0,1)$, $\mathbf{P}(|Y_n-0|\ge\epsilon)=1/n\to0$, so $Y_n\to0$ in probability. Yet $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]=n\cdot\tfrac1n=1$ for every $n$ — it does not converge to $0$ — and $\operatorname{var}(Y_n)=n^2\cdot\tfrac1n-1^2=n-1$, which is $99$ at $n=100$ and diverges. So "$M_n\to\mu$ in probability" licenses statements about probabilities of intervals and nothing more; it does not license $\mathbb{E}[g(M_n)]\to g(\mu)$, and it is not the pointwise statement "$M_n(\omega)\to\mu$ for each outcome $\omega$" (that stronger claim is the strong law, B&T §5.5, and it is a different theorem with a different proof). Notice also the asymmetry of the WLLN quantifiers: $\epsilon$ is fixed first, then $n\to\infty$. Statements where $\epsilon$ shrinks with $n$ — such as $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge c/\sqrt n)$ — are CLT territory, not WLLN territory, and there the probability tends to a nonzero constant.
Gotcha 4 — MAP is not the posterior mean, and for skewed posteriors the gap is large. In Example 5.3 the Beta$(6,1)$ posterior has MAP $=1$ and mean $=6/7=0.857143$, a gap of $0.142857$ — and reporting the MAP costs $0.035714$ in mean squared error against $0.015306$ for the mean, a $2.33\times$ penalty. The gap shrinks as the posterior becomes symmetric: three heads in five tosses gives Beta$(4,3)$ with MAP $0.6$ and mean $0.571429$ (gap $0.028571$), and seventy heads in a hundred tosses gives Beta$(71,31)$ with MAP $0.7$ and mean $0.696078$ (gap $0.003922$). Two corollaries. First, quoting "the MAP estimate minimizes error" is only correct for discrete $\Theta$ and the error criterion $\mathbf{P}(\hat\Theta\ne\Theta)$; under squared error the minimizer is always $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$. Second, MAP is not invariant under reparametrization — the mode of the posterior of $\Theta$ and the mode of the posterior of $\Theta^2$ are not related by squaring — whereas the whole posterior is.
Gotcha 5 — linear LMS needs only five numbers, and that is both the selling point and the bill. $\hat\Theta_L$ depends on the joint distribution only through the four numbers $\mathbb{E}[\Theta],\mathbb{E}[X],\operatorname{var}(X),\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)$ (the fifth, $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$, is needed only to state the resulting MSE $(1-\rho^2)\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$ — two means, two variances and one covariance in total, exactly as in §4), so it is computable when nothing else is — and it is therefore blind to every feature those numbers miss. Example 5.4 is the extreme case: with $X=\Theta^2$ the data determine $\Theta$ exactly, yet linear LMS still carries MSE $1/192=0.005208$ because a straight line cannot be $\sqrt{x}$. Two associated traps. (i) $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X^3]$, because $X$ and $X^3$ carry the same information, but the linear LMS estimates based on $X$ and on $X^3$ are different — so with linear LMS the choice of variable matters, and you are free to feed it $X$, $X^3$, or several transforms at once (L22 slide 9). (ii) A large correlation is not a licence: $\rho^2=0.9375$ in Example 5.4 sounds like near-perfection, but $\rho$ measures only the linear part of the dependence. When prior and noise are all normal the trap disappears, because then $\hat\Theta_L=\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ exactly (L22 slide 8).
Practice 5.5 (which gotcha?). Each statement below is wrong or unsupported. Name the gotcha it violates and give the corrected statement. (a) "Chebyshev at $k=3$ gives $0.111$, so about one experiment in nine lands more than $3\sigma$ from the mean." (b) "$S_{36}\sim$ Binomial$(36,0.5)$, so $\mathbf{P}(S_{36}=19)\approx\varphi(1/3)/3$, the normal density at $19$." (c) "$Y_n\to 0$ in probability, therefore $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]\to0$." (d) "After five heads the posterior peaks at $\theta=1$, so the most probable explanation is that the coin is two-headed." (e) "$\rho^2=0.9375$ between $X$ and $\Theta$, so a linear rule captures $94\%$ of what is knowable."
Solution

(a) Gotcha 1. A bound is not a frequency. $1/k^2=0.111111$ is an upper bound valid for every distribution with that variance; for a normal the actual two-sided $3\sigma$ tail is $0.002700$, forty-one times smaller, and for a uniform on $[0,1]$ it is exactly $0$. Correct statement: "at most one experiment in nine".

(b) Gotcha 2. The CLT is a statement about CDFs; there is no theorem about PMF values. Spread the integer over $[18.5,19.5]$ and difference the normal CDF: $\Phi(0.5)-\Phi(0.166667)=0.125279$ against the exact $0.125110$. Reading the density instead gives $0.125794$ — it happens to be close here, but it is an unlicensed move.

(c) Gotcha 3. Convergence in probability says nothing about expectations. The standard counterexample is the one in Gotcha 3 itself: $Y_n=n$ with probability $1/n$, else $0$, has $\mathbf{P}(|Y_n|\ge\epsilon)=1/n\to0$ yet $\mathbb{E}[Y_n]=1$ for every $n$. Correct statement: "$\mathbf{P}(|Y_n|\ge\epsilon)\to0$ for every fixed $\epsilon>0$", and nothing more.

(d) Gotcha 4 (and Example 5.3). With a continuous prior every single value has probability $0$, so "the coin is two-headed" has posterior probability $0$ no matter how the density behaves at $\theta=1$; a density value is not a probability. To ask the yes/no question at all you must put an atom on $\theta=1$ in the prior. Correct statement: "the posterior density is largest near $\theta=1$", which is a statement about where the mass concentrates, not about a hypothesis.

(e) Gotcha 5. $\rho$ measures the linear part of the dependence only. In Example 5.4, $\rho^2=0.9375$ while the optimal estimator $\sqrt X$ has MSE exactly $0$ — the remaining $6.25\%$ of prior variance was not noise, it was curvature. Correct statement: "a linear rule removes $93.75\%$ of the prior variance", with no claim about what is knowable.

Practice 5.6 (which tool?). For each task name the single tool from the two tables, and the one hypothesis that makes the tools below it unavailable. (i) $X\ge0$ is a claim size with known mean only; you must promise a bound on $\mathbf{P}(X\ge 5\mathbb{E}[X])$. (ii) I.i.d. terms with known mean and variance; you must promise a sample size that makes $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)$ small. (iii) $S_n$ is a sum of $200$ i.i.d. integer-valued terms; you must report $\mathbf{P}(S_n\le 240)$ as a number. (iv) $\Theta\in\{\text{signal present},\text{absent}\}$; you must announce one of the two. (v) You know $\mathbb{E}[\Theta],\mathbb{E}[X]$, both variances and $\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)$ and nothing else; you must produce an estimate.
Solution

(i) Markov. Only the mean is known, so Chebyshev is unavailable; the bound is $\mathbb{E}[X]/(5\mathbb{E}[X])=0.2$, whatever the distribution. The blocking hypothesis is the missing variance.

(ii) The weak law, i.e. Chebyshev applied to $M_n$ with $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n$: $n\ge\sigma^2/(\delta\epsilon^2)$. The CLT is unavailable as a promise — not because a hypothesis fails but because it returns an approximation, and the word in the task is "promise" (Fig. 5.1).

(iii) The CLT, with the $\tfrac12$ correction: $\Phi\big((240.5-n\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt n)\big)$. The task asks for a number, not a bound, and the terms are lattice-valued, which is what makes the correction mandatory (Gotcha 2).

(iv) MAP. $\Theta$ is discrete and you must commit to one value, which is exactly the criterion $\mathbf{P}(\hat\Theta\ne\Theta)$ that MAP minimizes. LMS would return a number between the two labels, which is not an admissible announcement.

(v) Linear LMS. Without the prior and the observation model there is no posterior, so the top three rows of the §5.2 table are unavailable; $\hat\Theta_L=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+ \frac{\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)}{\operatorname{var}(X)}(X-\mathbb{E}[X])$ is the whole of what the information supports, and $\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$ lets you quote its MSE.

5.4 Bridge to G7: drop the prior

Everything in §3 and §4 rested on one modeling decision: $\Theta$ was given a distribution. That is what let us write $f_{\Theta|X}$ at all, because Bayes' rule needs a $f_\Theta$ to invert. L21 slide 2 puts the alternative on the same slide, deliberately: in classical statistics $\theta$ is an unknown constant, not a random variable. The model is written $p_X(x;\theta)$ — a whole family of distributions indexed by $\theta$, with a semicolon rather than a conditioning bar, because there is nothing to condition on. There is no prior, hence no posterior, hence none of the four rows of §5.2 exists in that world.

Read the last two rows together and the bridge is short. Maximum likelihood is the MAP rule with $f_\Theta$ set to a constant, so every MAP computation in §3 already contains a classical one inside it; the Example 5.3 posterior $6\theta^5$ is maximized at $\theta=1$, and so is the likelihood $\theta^5$ — with a uniform prior they agree exactly. And confidence intervals, the central object of G7, are built out of §1 and §2: the estimator $\hat\Theta_n$ is a sample mean, the weak law says it converges to $\theta$, and the CLT converts "converges" into the $\pm 1.96\,\hat\sigma/\sqrt n$ that appears in every reported poll. That is why limit theorems and Bayesian inference sit in the same group: G6 §1–§2 supply the machinery that G7 uses to answer G6 §3–§4's questions without a prior.

Interpretation — the disagreement is about what is random. Both frameworks compute with the same likelihoods and often produce the same arithmetic. They differ on what probability is being taken over. The Bayesian averages over the unknown, holding the data fixed at what you saw; the classicist averages over hypothetical repeat data, holding the unknown fixed at whatever it truly is. Neither is a theorem — the choice is a modeling decision about whether a prior for $\theta$ is meaningful and defensible. In G7 you will see the cost of dropping it: without a prior there is no single "probability that $\theta$ lies here", and the answers become statements about procedures rather than about parameters.
Practice 5.7. A coin is tossed $100$ times and gives $60$ heads. (a) With a uniform prior on the bias $\Theta$, give the MAP estimate, the posterior mean, and a $95\%$ posterior interval. (b) Classically, give the maximum-likelihood estimate and the $95\%$ confidence interval $\hat p\pm z_{0.975}\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$. (c) The two intervals nearly coincide; state precisely how their meanings differ.
Solution

(a) Bayesian. The likelihood is $\theta^{60}(1-\theta)^{40}$ and the uniform prior is constant, so the posterior is proportional to it: Beta$(61,41)$. Its mode is $(61-1)/(61+41-2)=60/100=0.6$, so $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=0.6$. Its mean is $61/102=0.598039$ — pulled a hair toward the prior mean $0.5$, because the prior adds one imaginary head and one imaginary tail. Its standard deviation is $0.048310$, and the central $95\%$ posterior interval (the $2.5$th and $97.5$th posterior percentiles) is $$[0.501744,\ 0.690691].$$

(b) Classical. Maximizing $\theta^{60}(1-\theta)^{40}$ — the same maximization with the prior deleted, which here changes nothing — gives $\hat p=60/100=0.6$. The estimated standard error is $\sqrt{0.6(0.4)/100}=0.048990$, so with $z_{0.975}=1.959964$ the half-width is $0.096018$ and the interval is $$[0.503982,\ 0.696018].$$

(c) The difference. Numerically the intervals differ by less than a quarter of a percentage point, and the point estimates agree exactly — with a uniform prior MAP is maximum likelihood. The statements are not the same statement. The Bayesian interval says: given the $60$ heads I actually saw, the posterior assigns probability $0.95$ to $\Theta$ lying in $[0.501744,0.690691]$ — a probability about the unknown, with the data held fixed. The confidence interval says: the procedure "take $\hat p\pm1.959964\,\hat\sigma/\sqrt n$" produces, over hypothetical repetitions of the experiment, intervals that cover the fixed true $\theta$ $95\%$ of the time — a probability about the interval, with the unknown held fixed. Once the data are in, $[0.503982,0.696018]$ either contains $\theta$ or it does not, and classically there is no probability to attach to which. G7 develops the second story; the arithmetic will look familiar because it is built from the WLLN and the CLT of §1–§2 (B&T §9.1).

G6 in twelve lines.
  • $\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)\le\mathbb{E}[X]/a$ for $X\ge0$ — Markov, mean only (B&T §5.1).
  • $\mathbf{P}(|X-\mu|\ge c)\le\sigma^2/c^2$ — Chebyshev, mean and variance (B&T §5.1).
  • $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n$ is the engine of everything about sample means.
  • $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2)\to0$ — WLLN, convergence in probability (B&T §5.2–5.3).
  • $Z_n=(S_n-n\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt n)$; $\mathbf{P}(Z_n\le c)\to\Phi(c)$ — CLT, CDFs only (B&T §5.4).
  • Integer-valued sums: add the $\tfrac12$ correction; $0.878508$ exact vs $0.841345$ without and $0.878327$ with, at $n=36,p=0.5$.
  • Bernoulli poll: $\sigma^2=f(1-f)\le1/4$; $\pm1\%$ at $95\%$ costs $n=50000$ by Chebyshev, $n=9604$ by CLT.
  • $f_{\Theta|X}(\theta|x)=f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X|\Theta}(x|\theta)/f_X(x)$ — four versions, one idea (B&T §8.1).
  • MAP maximizes the posterior; it needs no normalizing constant and minimizes $\mathbf{P}(\text{error})$ for discrete $\Theta$ (B&T §8.2).
  • $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ minimizes MSE over all estimators; its MSE is $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(\Theta\mid X)]$ (B&T §8.3).
  • $\hat\Theta_L=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+\frac{\operatorname{cov}(X,\Theta)}{\operatorname{var}(X)}(X-\mathbb{E}[X])$, MSE $=(1-\rho^2)\operatorname{var}(\Theta)$ — five moments (two means, two variances, one covariance), nothing else (B&T §8.4).
  • Delete the prior and MAP becomes maximum likelihood: that is the door into G7 (B&T §9.1).
