Section 1 gave you inequalities. Markov and Chebyshev are honest: they are guaranteed upper bounds, valid for every distribution with the stated moments, and the weak law of large numbers falls out of Chebyshev in three lines. The price of that honesty is that the bounds are loose — often by one or two orders of magnitude. This section buys precision by giving up the guarantee.

The trade is worth making because of a fact that has no business being true. Take any distribution with a finite variance — discrete, continuous, symmetric, skewed, it does not matter — add up $n$ independent copies, recenter and rescale, and the resulting distribution stops depending on what you started with. It converges to one universal shape, the standard normal. That is the central limit theorem (CLT), and it is why the bell curve is everywhere.

Interpretation (where this sits between §1 and §3). All three of §1's results are statements about the sum $S_n=X_1+\dots+X_n$:
  • Markov / Chebyshev bound $\mathbf{P}(|S_n-n\mathbb{E}[X]|\ge a)$ crudely, using one or two moments.
  • The weak law says the deviation $S_n-n\mathbb{E}[X]$ is $o(n)$: divide by $n$ and the randomness disappears, $M_n\to\mathbb{E}[X]$.
  • The CLT says the deviation is exactly of order $\sqrt{n}$: divide by $\sqrt{n}$ instead of by $n$, and the randomness does not disappear — it settles down to a fixed shape.
So $\sqrt{n}$ is the knife's edge. Scale down harder than $\sqrt{n}$ and you get a constant (the weak law); scale down less and you get something that blows up; scale by exactly $\sqrt{n}$ and you get the normal distribution (B&T §5.4, p. 273: "what is special about this particular scaling is that it keeps the variance at a constant").

2.1 The statement, and why standardization is forced on us

Fix the setup for the whole section. Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with common mean $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and common finite variance $\sigma^2$; let

$$S_n = X_1+X_2+\cdots+X_n$$

be the $n$-fold sum. Here $n$ is the number of terms, $\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}$ is the common standard deviation of a single $X_i$, and nothing else about the distribution of $X_i$ will ever be used.

Why we cannot look at $S_n$ itself. Compute its two moments, using linearity of expectation and the fact that variances of independent random variables add (G2 §4):

$$\mathbb{E}[S_n] = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_i]=n\,\mathbb{E}[X],\qquad \operatorname{var}(S_n)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{var}(X_i)=n\sigma^2,\qquad \sigma_{S_n}=\sqrt{n}\,\sigma .$$

Both the center and the spread run away to infinity as $n$ grows, so the distribution of $S_n$ cannot converge to anything. Two fixes suggest themselves and both fail on their own:

  • Center only: $S_n-n\mathbb{E}[X]$ has mean $0$ but standard deviation $\sqrt{n}\,\sigma\to \infty$ — it spreads out without limit.
  • Divide by $n$: $M_n=S_n/n$ has mean $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}\to 0$ — it collapses to a point, which is exactly the weak law of §1 and carries no shape information at all.

The unique scaling that fixes both moments simultaneously is to subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation.

Definition (standardized sum; L20 slide 1). The standardized sum is $$Z_n \;=\; \frac{S_n-\mathbb{E}[S_n]}{\sigma_{S_n}} \;=\; \frac{S_n-n\,\mathbb{E}[X]}{\sqrt{n}\,\sigma} \;=\;\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n-n\,\mathbb{E}[X]}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} .$$ The numerator recenters ($\mathbb{E}$ of it is $0$); the denominator rescales so that the spread is $1$, whatever $n$ is.

Mean $0$ and variance $1$: already verified in §1. L20 slide 1 states these two facts without proof, and we do not need to redo the algebra here: $Z_n$ is the same standardized variable introduced in §1, Example 1.4, where both moments were checked — $\mathbb{E}[Z_n]=\big(\mathbb{E}[S_n]-n\mathbb{E}[X]\big)/(\sigma\sqrt n)=0$ by linearity, and $\operatorname{var}(Z_n)=\operatorname{var}(S_n)/(\sigma\sqrt n)^2=n\sigma^2/(n\sigma^2)=1$ by the rule $\operatorname{var}(aS+b)=a^2\operatorname{var}(S)$ (shifting does not change spread, scaling squares it — G2 §3). The variance step is the only place independence enters the bookkeeping: it is used once, inside $\operatorname{var}(S_n)=n\sigma^2$. Notation: §0 and §1 wrote the common mean as $\mu$; from here on we write $\mathbb{E}[X]$ for exactly the same quantity, matching L20 and B&T §5.4.

Figure 2.1 shows the two steps happening to a concrete sum: panel (a) is the raw $S_{36}$, panel (b) subtracts $n\mathbb{E}[X]$, panel (c) divides by $\sigma\sqrt n$ — and only after both steps does a fixed limiting curve appear.

The Central Limit Theorem (L20 slide 1; B&T §5.4, p. 273). Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be i.i.d. with mean $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, and let $$Z_n=\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n-n\,\mathbb{E}[X]}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} .$$ Let $Z$ be a standard normal random variable (mean $0$, variance $1$). Then for every real number $c$, $$\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}(Z_n\le c)\;=\;\mathbf{P}(Z\le c)\;=\;\Phi(c),$$ where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF, read from the normal tables (G3 §1).

Three things deserve to be said out loud about this statement.

  1. The hypotheses are almost nothing. Independence, identical distribution, and a finite variance. The $X_i$ may be discrete, continuous, or mixed; bounded or unbounded; symmetric or wildly skewed. B&T §5.4 (p. 274): "Besides independence, and the implicit assumption that the mean and variance are finite, it places no other requirement on the distribution of the $X_i$."
  2. The conclusion involves only $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\sigma^2$. Those two numbers are the entire input; everything else about the distribution is washed out.
  3. What converges is a CDF, evaluated at a fixed $c$ — a statement about probabilities of events, not about densities. §2.2 is devoted to this point because it is the one students get wrong.

Figure 2.2 makes point 2 visible: two bases that look nothing alike produce the same limiting shape, and the only thing that differs is how fast they get there.

Interpretation (why "universal" is the right word — L20 slide 2). The slide lists three payoffs, and each is worth unpacking.
  • Universality: only means and variances matter. Suppose two engineers model the same noisy measurement, one with a uniform error distribution, one with a triangular one. If they match the mean and the variance, then for a sum of many such errors their answers agree, no matter how different their models look. This is why arguing about the exact shape of a small random effect is usually a waste of time: after summing, only two numbers survive.
  • An accurate computational shortcut. Computing $\mathbf{P}(S_n\le c)$ exactly means an $n$-fold convolution — a sum over an exponentially growing set of ways to reach $c$. The CLT replaces it with one subtraction, one division and one table lookup. In Example 2.1 below the exact answer is a sum of 22 binomial coefficients; the CLT answer is $\Phi(1.1667)$.
  • Justification of normal models. Whenever a physical quantity is the accumulation of many small independent contributions — thermal noise, measurement error, the height of a person as a sum of many genetic and environmental increments — the CLT predicts it should look normal. That prediction is confirmed empirically over and over, and the CLT explains why rather than just noting the fact (B&T §5.4, p. 274).
Gotcha (finite variance is not decoration). Drop the finite-variance hypothesis and the theorem is false, not merely unproven. If the $X_i$ are Cauchy — density $f(x)=\frac{1}{\pi(1+x^2)}$, for which $\mathbb{E}[|X|]=\infty$ — then $S_n/n$ has exactly the same Cauchy distribution as a single $X_1$, for every $n$. No amount of averaging helps, and no normal limit appears. Whenever you invoke the CLT, you are quietly asserting $\sigma^2<\infty$.
Practice 2.1. $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are i.i.d. with $\mathbb{E}[X]=4$ and $\sigma^2=25$. Write down $\mathbb{E}[S_{100}]$, $\operatorname{var}(S_{100})$, $\sigma_{S_{100}}$, and the standardized variable $Z_{100}$ explicitly.
Answer

$\mathbb{E}[S_{100}]=100\times 4=400$ by linearity. $\operatorname{var}(S_{100})=100\times 25=2500$ because independent variances add. $\sigma_{S_{100}}=\sqrt{2500}=50$ — note it is not $100\times 5$; standard deviations do not add, variances do. Hence $Z_{100}=(S_{100}-400)/50$.

Practice 2.2. True or false: if the $X_i$ are already normal, the CLT is needed to conclude that $S_n$ is normal.
Answer

False, and the distinction matters. A sum of independent normal random variables is exactly normal for every $n$, including $n=1$ — that is a property of the normal family (proved by convolution in G3 §4), not a limit theorem. Consequently $Z_n$ is exactly $N(0,1)$ for all $n$ and the max CDF gap is $0$. The CLT is only interesting when the base distribution is not normal.

2.2 What exactly does the theorem say? (and what it does not)

L20 slide 2 puts the warning in two lines: "CDF of $Z_n$ converges to normal CDF — not a statement about convergence of PDFs or PMFs." Here is why that distinction is not pedantry.

Gotcha (the single most common CLT error). The CLT says $$\mathbf{P}(Z_n\le c)\to\Phi(c)\quad\text{for each fixed } c.$$ It does not say $p_{Z_n}(c)\to\varphi(c)$, and for discrete $X_i$ that latter statement is flatly false. Take $X_i$ Bernoulli$(1/2)$, $n=16$. Then $S_{16}$ is an integer, so $Z_{16}$ takes only the 17 values $(k-8)/2$, $k=0,\dots,16$, and $\mathbf{P}(Z_{16}=z)=0$ for every other $z$ — including $z=0.1$, where the normal density is $\varphi(0.1)=0.397$. Worse, at the values it does hit, the probabilities go to zero: the largest atom of $Z_{16}$ has probability $0.196381$, of $Z_{64}$ only $0.099347$, and it keeps shrinking like $1/\sqrt{n}$. A PMF whose every value tends to $0$ cannot converge to a density. What is happening is that the atoms get closer together (spacing $1/(\sigma\sqrt n)$) at exactly the rate their heights shrink, so their accumulated mass — the CDF — converges perfectly well. Figure 2.3 puts the two views side by side.
Fig. 2.3 — Why the CLT is a CDF statement (L20 slide 2). (a) The standardized PMF of $Z_n$ for $X_i\sim$ Bernoulli$(1/2)$: as $n$ goes from 16 (blue) to 64 (green) every individual probability halves in size and doubles in count; nothing converges to the orange density. (b) The same $n=16$ distribution as a CDF: the staircase already hugs $\Phi$, with a worst-case vertical gap of $0.0982$ over all $z$ (key widget_parity, row $n=16$). The gap shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$: $0.0700$ at $n=32$, $0.0497$ at $n=64$.
Interpretation (what the theorem buys you in practice). Since a CDF determines all interval probabilities, CDF convergence is enough for everything you actually want: $$\mathbf{P}(a\le Z_n\le b)=\mathbf{P}(Z_n\le b)-\mathbf{P}(Z_n<a)\;\longrightarrow\;\Phi(b)-\Phi(a).$$ The only quantity CDF convergence does not hand you is the probability of a single point — and §2.4 shows how the $1/2$ correction recovers even that, by turning "the atom at $k$" into "the interval $[k-\tfrac12,\,k+\tfrac12]$", which is an interval probability.

Normal approximation: treating $S_n$ as if it were normal

L20 slide 2 makes the practical move explicit: treat $Z_n$ as if it were normal, and therefore treat $S_n$ as if it were normal too. The second half follows from the first because normality is preserved by linear transformations (G3 §1): if $Z_n\approx N(0,1)$ and $S_n=n\mathbb{E}[X]+\sigma\sqrt{n}\,Z_n$, then $S_n$ is approximately normal with mean $n\mathbb{E}[X]$ and variance $n\sigma^2$. Both descriptions give identical numbers; use whichever is less error-prone for the problem at hand.

Gotcha (moderate $n$ — L20 slide 2). The slide asks "Can we use it when $n$ is moderate?" and answers: "Yes, but no nice theorems to this effect. Symmetry helps a lot." Take that literally. There is no universal threshold — no "$n\ge 30$" rule that is actually a theorem. What governs the accuracy is how far the base distribution is from normal, and skewness is the dominant defect. Intuition (the $1/\sqrt n$ rate, and where it comes from). The bottom three rows of that table shrink by roughly $\sqrt{2}$ when $n$ doubles — from $n=16$ to $n=32$, $0.03328\to 0.02352$, $0.09819\to 0.06997$, $0.20619\to 0.14721$, each a factor of about $1.41$. That is the signature of a $1/\sqrt n$ rate. The uniform row is the exception: it runs $0.00174\to 0.00087$, a factor of $2.01$, i.e. a $1/n$ rate. The reason is that the leading correction term in the Edgeworth expansion is proportional to the skewness of the base, so for a base that is both symmetric and non-lattice — like the uniform on $[0,1]$ — that term vanishes identically and the $1/\sqrt n$ bound below, which only ever sees the third absolute moment, is simply not tight. Where the rate really is $1/\sqrt n$ it is a theorem: the Berry–Esseen bound says $$\sup_c\big|\mathbf{P}(Z_n\le c)-\Phi(c)\big|\;\le\;\frac{C\,\rho}{\sigma^3\sqrt n},\qquad \rho=\mathbb{E}\big[|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|^3\big],$$ with an absolute constant $C\approx 0.4748$. For Bernoulli$(0.5)$, $\rho=\tfrac12(\tfrac12)^3+ \tfrac12(\tfrac12)^3=\tfrac18$ and $\sigma^3=(1/2)^3=1/8$, so the bound is $0.4748/\sqrt n$: at $n=16$ that is $0.11870$ and at $n=32$ it is $0.08393$ (keys berry_esseen_bern5) — correctly bounding the true gaps $0.09819$ and $0.06997$, and not by much. Notice what the bound depends on: the third absolute central moment, i.e. exactly the asymmetry of the base distribution. This is the theorem behind "symmetry helps a lot".

Answer

At $n=16$ the gaps are about $0.098$ for $p=0.5$ and $0.206$ for $p=0.1$ (key gap_table, row $n=16$, columns bern5 and bern1) — the skewed coin is roughly twice as bad. For Bernoulli$(p)$ with $q=1-p$, $$\rho=\mathbb{E}|X-p|^3 = p\,q^3+q\,p^3 = pq(p^2+q^2),\qquad \sigma^3=(pq)^{3/2},$$ so $\rho/\sigma^3=(p^2+q^2)/\sqrt{pq}$. 2.3 Using it: the normal approximation recipe

All CLT computations follow the same four steps, and B&T §5.4 (p. 275) boxes them as a procedure. Here it is with the two steps the book leaves implicit made explicit.

Recipe (normal approximation for $\mathbf{P}(S_n\le c)$; B&T §5.4 p. 275).
  1. Identify the summands. Write the event in terms of a sum $S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n$ of i.i.d. variables. Sometimes this needs a reformulation — see Example 2.3, where "how many parts in 320 minutes" becomes "how long do 100 parts take".
  2. Get $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\sigma^2$ for a single term, then $$\mathbb{E}[S_n]=n\,\mathbb{E}[X],\qquad \operatorname{var}(S_n)=n\sigma^2,\qquad \sigma_{S_n}=\sigma\sqrt{n}.$$
  3. Standardize the threshold. Compute the normalized value $$z=\frac{c-n\,\mathbb{E}[X]}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} .$$ This is one number; it says "the threshold $c$ sits $z$ standard deviations above the mean of $S_n$".
  4. Look up $\Phi(z)$ and read off $\mathbf{P}(S_n\le c)\approx\Phi(z)$. For the other tail use $\mathbf{P}(S_n>c)\approx 1-\Phi(z)$; for negative arguments use the symmetry $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$, since normal tables list only $z\ge 0$; for an interval use $\mathbf{P}(a\le S_n\le c)\approx \Phi(z_c)-\Phi(z_a)$.
Two extra steps you must not skip: (5) if $S_n$ is integer-valued, apply the $1/2$ correction of §2.4 before looking anything up; (6) if $\sigma^2$ is unknown but bounded, use the largest possible $\sigma^2$ — this makes the tail estimate an upper bound rather than an estimate (§2.5). Figure 2.5 at the end of §2.5 turns this recipe, together with §1's bounds, into a single decision chart: read it top to bottom to decide which tool to reach for before running these steps.
Example 2.1 (L20 slide 5 — [UNSOLVED EXAMPLE]: the binomial the slide leaves blank; = B&T Example 5.12). Let $S_n$ be Binomial$(n,p)$ with $n=36$, $p=0.5$. Find $\mathbf{P}(S_n\le 21)$. The slide poses the question, leaves a blank where the lecturer works it on the board, and prints only the exact value $\sum_{k=0}^{21}\binom{36}{k}(1/2)^{36}=0.8785$.
Solution (all six steps, plus the exact value recomputed)

Step 1 — the summands. A Binomial$(n,p)$ variable is a sum: $S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_{36}$ with $X_i$ i.i.d. Bernoulli$(0.5)$, $X_i=1$ if the $i$th trial succeeds (L20 slide 5). So the CLT applies with no reformulation.

Step 2 — moments. For a Bernoulli$(p)$ variable, $\mathbb{E}[X]=p=0.5$ and $\sigma^2=p(1-p)=0.5\times0.5=0.25$ (G2 §3). Hence $$\mathbb{E}[S_{36}]=np=36\times0.5=18,\qquad \operatorname{var}(S_{36})=np(1-p)=36\times0.25=9,\qquad \sigma_{S_{36}}=\sqrt{9}=3 .$$ [DERIVATION GAP filled: the slide uses $18$ and $3$ on the next two slides without ever computing them.]

Step 3 — standardize. With $c=21$, $$z=\frac{21-18}{3}=\frac{3}{3}=1.0 .$$

Step 4 — look up. $\Phi(1.00)=0.8413$ from the normal table ($0.841345$ to six places), so $$\mathbf{P}(S_{36}\le 21)\approx 0.841345 .$$

Step 5 — the correction (previewing §2.4). $S_{36}$ is integer-valued, so replace the cut $21$ by $21.5$: $$z=\frac{21.5-18}{3}=\frac{3.5}{3}=1.166667,\qquad \Phi(1.1667)=0.878327 .$$ (The table value at the rounded $z=1.17$ is $0.878999$; B&T rounds this to $0.879$.)

Step 6 — no unknown variance here, so nothing to do.

Compare with the exact answer. Recomputing the slide's sum in computes/g6_s2.py (key b36_exact_le21): $$\sum_{k=0}^{21}\binom{36}{k}\Big(\frac12\Big)^{36}=0.878508,$$ confirming the slide's $0.8785$. The errors are $$|0.841345-0.878508|=0.037163\quad\text{(no correction)},\qquad |0.878327-0.878508|=0.000180\quad\text{(corrected)} .$$ The $1/2$ correction improves the answer by a factor of $206$. That is the whole point of §2.4.

Example 2.2 (B&T Example 5.9 — the airplane cargo). We load 100 packages whose weights are independent and uniformly distributed between 5 and 50 pounds. What is the probability that the total weight exceeds 3000 pounds?
Solution

Step 1. $S_{100}=X_1+\cdots+X_{100}$, $X_i\sim$ Uniform$[5,50]$ i.i.d., $X_i$ = weight of the $i$th package in pounds. We want $\mathbf{P}(S_{100}>3000)$.

Step 2. For a uniform on $[a,b]$ (G3 §1), $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{a+b}{2}=\frac{5+50}{2}=27.5,\qquad \sigma^2=\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}=\frac{45^2}{12}=\frac{2025}{12}=168.75 .$$ Hence $\mathbb{E}[S_{100}]=100\times 27.5=2750$, $\operatorname{var}(S_{100})=100\times 168.75=16875$, and $\sigma_{S_{100}}=\sqrt{16875}=129.903811$.

Step 3. $\displaystyle z=\frac{3000-2750}{129.903811}=\frac{250}{129.903811}=1.924501$. (The book rounds the denominator to $129.9$ and reports $z=1.92$.)

Step 4. $\Phi(1.924501)=0.972854$, so $$\mathbf{P}(S_{100}\le 3000)\approx 0.972854 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbf{P}(S_{100}>3000)\approx 1-0.972854=\boxed{0.027146}.$$ Using the book's rounded $z=1.92$ gives $\Phi(1.92)=0.972571$ and the answer $0.027429$, which the book prints as $0.0274$. The two differ only through the rounding of $z$ — a reminder that quoting four decimals from a two-decimal table lookup is false precision.

Step 5. The weights are continuous, so there is no $1/2$ correction. Applying one here would be an error (see the Gotcha in §2.4).

Example 2.3 (B&T Example 5.10 — the machine, where step 1 does the work). A machine processes parts one at a time; processing times are independent and uniform on $[1,5]$. Let $N_{320}$ be the number of parts finished within 320 time units. Approximate $\mathbf{P}(N_{320}\ge 100)$.
Solution

Step 1 — the reformulation. $N_{320}$ is not a sum of i.i.d. variables, so the CLT does not apply to it directly. But the two events $$\{N_{320}\ge 100\}\quad\text{and}\quad \{S_{100}\le 320\},\qquad S_{100}=X_1+\cdots+X_{100},$$ are the same event: at least 100 parts are done by time 320 exactly when the total time for the first 100 parts is at most 320. (This "inverse" trick — counting process versus arrival times — is the same duality used for Poisson processes in G4 §3.) Now $S_{100}$ is an i.i.d. sum.

Step 2. $\mathbb{E}[X]=(1+5)/2=3$ and $\sigma^2=(5-1)^2/12=16/12=1.333333$. So $\mathbb{E}[S_{100}]=300$, $\operatorname{var}(S_{100})=133.333333$, $\sigma_{S_{100}}=11.547005$.

Step 3. $\displaystyle z=\frac{320-300}{11.547005}=\frac{20}{11.547005}=1.732051$ (the book writes $1.73$).

(a) Here $n=100$ and the threshold is $c=440$.

2.4 De Moivre–Laplace: the $1/2$ correction for integer-valued sums

Everything so far approximated a discrete distribution by a continuous one without comment. For $\mathbf{P}(S_n\le c)$ with $c$ far from the mean that is tolerable; near the mean it is not, and for $\mathbf{P}(S_n=k)$ it is a disaster — the normal model assigns probability $0$ to every single point. The fix is a half-unit shift, and it is worth deriving rather than memorizing.

The derivation, on the lecture's own example

Take $S_n\sim$ Binomial$(36,0.5)$ again, with $\mathbb{E}[S_n]=18$ and $\sigma_{S_n}=3$ (Example 2.1). The starting observation is L20 slide 6's first line:

$$\mathbf{P}(S_n\le 21)=\mathbf{P}(S_n<22),\qquad\text{because } S_n \text{ is an integer.}$$

Both statements are exactly true for the discrete variable: there is no probability mass strictly between 21 and 22. But the normal approximation does not know that. Approximating the left-hand side puts the cut at 21 and gives $\Phi\big(\frac{21-18}{3}\big)=\Phi(1)=0.841345$; approximating the right-hand side puts the cut at 22 and gives $\Phi\big(\frac{22-18}{3}\big)=\Phi(1.333333)=0.908789$. Two answers, differing by $0.067$, for the same exact quantity. The approximation is ambiguous, and the ambiguity is exactly the mass of the bar at 21.

Recipe (the compromise — L20 slide 6). Split the difference: cut halfway between the two integers, at $21.5$. In general, when $S_n$ is integer-valued, $$\mathbf{P}(S_n\le k)\approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{k+\tfrac12-n\mathbb{E}[X]}{\sigma\sqrt n}\right), \qquad \mathbf{P}(S_n\ge k)\approx 1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{k-\tfrac12-n\mathbb{E}[X]}{\sigma\sqrt n}\right).$$ The rule behind both: the probability bar standing at the integer $k$ is matched by the area under the normal density over the interval $[k-\tfrac12,\,k+\tfrac12]$. Every event of the form "$S_n$ lies in a set of integers" is then approximated by the area over the union of those unit intervals. [DERIVATION GAP filled: L20 slide 6 draws this picture but never states the rule in words nor evaluates it.] Figure 2.4 draws both halves of this argument — the bar-to-area match in panel (a), and the sliver the uncorrected cut throws away in panel (b).
Fig. 2.4 — The $1/2$ correction, redrawing the hand sketch of L20 slide 6 (B&T §5.4, its Figure 5.3). (a) The bar of the Binomial$(36,\tfrac12)$ PMF at $k=19$ has exact height $0.125110$; the shaded area of the normal density over $[18.5,19.5]$ is $0.125279$ — agreement to three decimals. (b) Cutting the CDF at $21$ omits the right half of the bar at $21$: the missing sliver is worth $0.878327-0.841345=0.036982$, which is nearly the whole error of the uncorrected approximation ($0.037163$).

Why "half a bar" is the right amount, quantitatively. The uncorrected cut at $k$ captures the normal area up to $k$; the bar at $k$ occupies $[k-\frac12,k+\frac12]$, so the cut at $k$ picks up its left half and throws away its right half. The discarded mass is approximately half the bar, $\tfrac12 p_{S_n}(k)$. For $k=21$ the exact bar height is $p_{S_{36}}(21)=0.081024$, so the discarded mass is $\tfrac12\times 0.081024=0.040512$ — within $9\%$ of the observed error $0.037163$, the small remainder being the curvature of the density across the bar. The correction adds that missing half back, and the residual error collapses from $3.7\times10^{-2}$ to $1.8\times10^{-4}$.

De Moivre–Laplace approximation to the binomial (L20 slide 7; B&T §5.4, p. 279). If $S_n$ is Binomial$(n,p)$, $n$ is large, and $k\le l$ are nonnegative integers, then $$\mathbf{P}(k\le S_n\le l)\;\approx\; \Phi\!\left(\frac{l+\tfrac12-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{k-\tfrac12-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right).$$ Taking $k=l$ gives an approximation to the PMF itself — something the plain CLT could never provide. B&T adds the practical rider: when $p\approx1/2$ this is very good for $n$ as low as 40 or 50; when $p$ is near 0 or 1 a larger $n$ is needed.
Example 2.5 (L20 slide 7 — the PMF of the binomial, every step). With $n=36$, $p=0.5$, approximate $\mathbf{P}(S_n=19)$ and compare with the exact $\binom{36}{19}(1/2)^{36}$.
Solution (the slide's chain, with the two gaps filled)

Step 1 — turn the point into an interval. Because $S_n$ takes only integer values, $$\mathbf{P}(S_n=19)=\mathbf{P}(18.5\le S_n\le 19.5)$$ exactly — the interval $[18.5,19.5]$ contains no integer other than 19. [Gap filled: the slide asserts this equality without saying that it depends on integrality.] This is the whole trick: a point event has been rewritten as an interval event, which is what a CDF limit can handle.

Step 2 — standardize both endpoints. Recall $\mathbb{E}[S_n]=18$ and $\sigma_{S_n}=3$ (carried over from Example 2.1 — the slide reuses them silently). Subtracting 18 and dividing by 3 preserves the inequalities: $$18.5\le S_n\le 19.5 \iff \frac{18.5-18}{3}\le \frac{S_n-18}{3}\le \frac{19.5-18}{3} \iff 0.166667\le Z_n\le 0.5 .$$ (The slide rounds $0.5/3=0.1\overline{6}$ to $0.17$.)

Step 3 — apply the CLT to the interval. $$\mathbf{P}(S_n=19)\approx \mathbf{P}(0.166667\le Z\le 0.5) =\mathbf{P}(Z\le 0.5)-\mathbf{P}(Z\le 0.166667) =\Phi(0.5)-\Phi(0.166667).$$ The middle step is just $\mathbf{P}(a\le Z\le b)=F(b)-F(a)$ for a continuous variable (G3 §1), where the two boundary points contribute nothing.

Step 4 — the table. $\Phi(0.5)=0.691462$ and $\Phi(0.166667)=0.566184$, so $$\mathbf{P}(S_n=19)\approx 0.691462-0.566184=\boxed{0.125279}.$$ With the slide's rounded arguments: $\Phi(0.5)-\Phi(0.17)=0.6915-0.5675=0.1240$, which is what the slide prints as $0.124$.

Step 5 — the exact value. $\binom{36}{19}=8\,597\,496\,600$ and $$\binom{36}{19}\Big(\frac12\Big)^{36}=0.125110$$ (key b36_exact_eq19), matching the slide's $0.1251$. The de Moivre–Laplace error is $0.000169$, a relative error of $0.135\%$. Rounding $z$ to two decimals, as the slide does, costs more ($0.124$ versus the true $0.1251$, a relative error of $0.9\%$) than the approximation itself — the table lookup, not the CLT, is the accuracy bottleneck here.

Gotcha (three ways to misuse the correction).
  • Do not apply it to continuous variables. In Example 2.2 the package weights are uniform on $[5,50]$; there are no "bars" and no gaps between values, so shifting the cut by $0.5$ simply introduces an error of $0.5/\sigma_{S_n}$ in $z$. The correction exists to repair a discretization artifact; with no discretization there is nothing to repair.
  • Get the direction right. For an upper tail you must widen, not shift blindly: $\mathbf{P}(S_n\ge k)$ uses $k-\tfrac12$, while $\mathbf{P}(S_n\le k)$ uses $k+\tfrac12$. The mnemonic is "always include the whole bar at $k$", so the cut moves away from the region you are keeping. Getting it backwards makes the answer worse than no correction at all.

2.5 Chebyshev versus the CLT: the same question, three answers

Section 1 and this section now offer competing tools for the same tail probability. rec21 P1 is built to put them side by side on one question, so we do that first and draw the general lesson after.

Example 2.6 (rec21 P1 — Markov, Chebyshev and the CLT on one question). Let $X_1,\dots,X_{10}$ be independent and uniformly distributed over $[0,1]$. Estimate $\mathbf{P}(X_1+\cdots+X_{10}\ge 7)$ using (a) the Markov inequality, (b) the Chebyshev inequality, (c) the central limit theorem.
Solution (official solution, with all three [SOLUTION GAP]s filled)

Common setup. Write $X=\sum_{i=1}^{10}X_i$. For a single uniform on $[0,1]$, $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=\tfrac12$ and $\operatorname{var}(X_i)=\tfrac1{12}$ (G3 §1). Hence, by linearity and by independence, $$\mathbb{E}[X]=10\times\tfrac12=5,\qquad \operatorname{var}(X)=10\times\tfrac1{12}=\tfrac{10}{12}=\tfrac56=0.833333,\qquad \sigma_X=\sqrt{5/6}=0.912871 .$$ [Gap filled: the official solution writes $\operatorname{var}(X)=10/12$ without deriving it.]

(a) Markov. Markov's inequality (§1) requires only a nonnegative random variable and its mean: $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge a)\le\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}\quad\text{for } a>0 .$$ The $X_i$ are nonnegative, so $X\ge0$ and the hypothesis holds. With $a=7$: $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge 7)\le \frac{5}{7}=0.714286 .$$ The official solution prints $0.7142$. Interpretation: this says the event has probability at most $71\%$, which is true but useless — a probability is at most 1 anyway, and we have barely improved on that.

(b) Chebyshev. Chebyshev's inequality bounds a two-sided deviation: $$\mathbf{P}(|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\ge a)\le\frac{\operatorname{var}(X)}{a^2}.$$ Our event $\{X\ge7\}$ is $\{X-5\ge 2\}$, one side only, with $a=2$. The official solution's first line is $$2\,\mathbf{P}(X-5\ge2)=\mathbf{P}(|X-5|\ge2),$$ and it is used without comment. [Gap filled — why this is legitimate:] the distribution of $X$ is symmetric about its mean 5. Indeed, if $U$ is uniform on $[0,1]$ then so is $1-U$, so $(X_1,\dots,X_{10})$ and $(1-X_1,\dots,1-X_{10})$ have the same joint distribution; summing, $X$ and $10-X$ have the same distribution, i.e. $X-5$ and $-(X-5)$ have the same distribution. Hence the two tails are equal, $$\mathbf{P}(X-5\ge 2)=\mathbf{P}(X-5\le -2),$$ and since the two events are disjoint their union $\{|X-5|\ge2\}$ has exactly twice the probability. Now bound: $$2\,\mathbf{P}(X-5\ge 2)=\mathbf{P}(|X-5|\ge 2)\le\frac{\operatorname{var}(X)}{2^2} =\frac{10/12}{4}=\frac{10}{48}=0.208333,$$ so $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge7)\le\frac12\cdot\frac{10}{48}=\frac{10}{96}=\frac{5}{48}=0.104167 .$$ [Gap filled: the final arithmetic $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{10/12}{4}=\tfrac{10}{96}=\tfrac5{48}$ is not shown on the sheet.] The official answer is $0.1042$. Seven times better than Markov, and it used one extra number (the variance).

(c) Central limit theorem. Now we approximate rather than bound. Start by flipping to the lower tail, since tables give $\Phi$: $$\mathbf{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{10}X_i\ge7\Big)=1-\mathbf{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{10}X_i\le 7\Big)$$ (the boundary point has probability 0 because $X$ is continuous). Standardize inside: $$\mathbf{P}\Big(\sum X_i\le 7\Big) =\mathbf{P}\!\left(\frac{\sum X_i-5}{\sqrt{10/12}}\le\frac{7-5}{\sqrt{10/12}}\right) \approx\Phi\!\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{10/12}}\right).$$ [Gap filled: the numerical evaluation is not shown.] Here $$\sqrt{10/12}=\sqrt{0.833333}=0.912871,\qquad \frac{2}{0.912871}=2.190890\approx 2.19,$$ and the table gives $\Phi(2.19)=0.985738$. Therefore $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge7)\approx 1-0.985738=0.014262,$$ the official $0.0143$. (With the unrounded $z=2.190890$: $1-\Phi(2.190890)=0.014230$.)

The exact answer, for scoring. The sum of $n$ i.i.d. uniforms on $[0,1]$ has the Irwin–Hall distribution, whose CDF is $$\mathbf{P}(X\le x)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor x\rfloor}(-1)^k\binom{n}{k}(x-k)^n .$$ Evaluated exactly with rational arithmetic at $n=10$, $x=7$ (key r1_exact_frac): $$\mathbf{P}(X\ge 7)=\frac{24427}{1814400}=0.013463 .$$ Now score the three tools against it:

The one-sided Chebyshev (Cantelli) inequality needs no symmetry: $$\mathbf{P}(X-\mathbb{E}[X]\ge a)\le\frac{\operatorname{var}(X)}{\operatorname{var}(X)+a^2} =\frac{5/6}{5/6+4}=0.172414 .$$ Here it is worse than the symmetry route ($0.104167$), which is the usual trade: extra assumptions buy sharper bounds.

Interpretation (what you are really trading). Markov, Chebyshev and Cantelli are bounds: whatever the distribution, the true probability is provably below them. The CLT is an approximation: it is usually far more accurate, but it comes with no guarantee at all and it can err on either side. Choose accordingly.
  • Need a certificate ("prove the failure probability is below $10^{-3}$")? Use a bound. An approximation is not a proof.
  • Need a number ("how many voters must I poll?")? Use the CLT — and note that its answer, being sharper, is smaller, which is exactly the direction that saves money.
  • Have only the mean? Markov is your only option. Have the variance too? Chebyshev. Have a large $n$? The CLT dominates both, by a lot.

Polling, redux: the CLT versus Chebyshev on sample size

Example 2.7 (L20 slide 4 — [UNSOLVED EXAMPLE]: the pollster's problem, finished; = B&T Example 5.11). Let $f$ be the (unknown) fraction of the population favoring a candidate. Poll $n$ people at random and set $$X_i=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if the } i\text{th person polled says yes,}\\ 0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases} \qquad M_n=\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n}.$$ How large must $n$ be so that $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-f|\ge 0.01)\le 0.05$? The slide sets the problem up, reduces it to a normal-tail statement, and stops — no numerical answer appears on any slide.
Solution (the slide's chain completed, with its unstated step supplied)

Step 1 — the model. The $X_i$ are i.i.d. Bernoulli$(f)$: each randomly selected person says yes with probability $f$, independently. So $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=f$ and $\sigma^2=f(1-f)$.

Step 2 — rewrite the event as a statement about the standardized sum. The slide's algebra, line by line: $$|M_n-f|\ge 0.01 \iff \left|\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n-nf}{n}\right|\ge 0.01 \iff \left|\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n-nf}{\sqrt n\,\sigma}\right|\ge \frac{0.01\,n}{\sqrt n\,\sigma} =\frac{0.01\sqrt n}{\sigma},$$ where the second step multiplies both sides by $n/(\sqrt n\,\sigma)>0$, which preserves the inequality. The middle quantity is exactly $|Z_n|$.

Step 3 — the CLT. Treating $Z_n$ as standard normal, $$\mathbf{P}(|M_n-f|\ge0.01)\approx\mathbf{P}\!\left(|Z|\ge\frac{0.01\sqrt n}{\sigma}\right).$$

Step 4 — remove the unknown $\sigma$. The slide's next line replaces $0.01\sqrt n/\sigma$ by $0.02\sqrt n$ with no justification. [DERIVATION GAP filled:] for a Bernoulli variable $\sigma=\sqrt{f(1-f)}$, and the function $f\mapsto f(1-f)$ on $[0,1]$ is maximized at $f=1/2$ with value $1/4$ (its derivative $1-2f$ vanishes there), so $$\sigma=\sqrt{f(1-f)}\le\sqrt{1/4}=\tfrac12 \;\Longrightarrow\;\frac{0.01\sqrt n}{\sigma}\ge\frac{0.01\sqrt n}{1/2}=0.02\sqrt n .$$ A larger threshold means a smaller tail probability, so $$\mathbf{P}\!\left(|Z|\ge\frac{0.01\sqrt n}{\sigma}\right)\le\mathbf{P}\big(|Z|\ge0.02\sqrt n\big),$$ which is the slide's last line. We have converted an unknown into a worst case — Recipe step 6.

Step 5 — impose the requirement and invert the table. By the symmetry of the normal density, $\mathbf{P}(|Z|\ge t)=2\big(1-\Phi(t)\big)$. So we need $$2\big(1-\Phi(0.02\sqrt n)\big)\le 0.05 \iff 1-\Phi(0.02\sqrt n)\le 0.025 \iff \Phi(0.02\sqrt n)\ge 0.975 .$$ Read the normal table backwards: $\Phi(1.96)=0.975002$, so the condition is $$0.02\sqrt n\ge 1.96 \iff \sqrt n\ge 98 \iff n\ge 98^2=9604 .$$ $$\boxed{n=9604 \text{ people suffice.}}$$ Check: $2\big(1-\Phi(0.02\sqrt{9604})\big)=2\big(1-\Phi(1.96)\big)=0.049996\le0.05$ ✓ (key poll_achieved). 2.6 Two consequences: Stirling's formula, and where the CLT must not go

The last two items on the syllabus are a triumph and a trap: the CLT producing a famous formula about factorials, and the CLT apparently proving something absurd about the Poisson distribution.

Example 2.8 (rec21 P3 — sums of unit-mean Poissons and Stirling's formula). Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be independent Poisson random variables with mean and variance $1$, and let $S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i$.
(a) Show that $S_n$ is Poisson with mean and variance $n$.
(b) Show how the CLT suggests $n!\approx\sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n$ for large $n$.
Solution (official solution, with the [SOLUTION GAP] filled)

(a) The Poisson-process argument (official solution). Interpret $X_i$ as the number of arrivals in the $i$th interval of length 1 in a Poisson process of rate $\lambda=1$ (G4 §3): the number of arrivals in an interval of length $\tau$ is Poisson with parameter $\lambda\tau=\tau$, so each $X_i$ is indeed Poisson with mean 1, and arrivals in disjoint intervals are independent, so the $X_i$ are independent. Laying the $n$ intervals end to end, $S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n$ counts the arrivals in one interval of length $n$. Therefore $S_n$ is Poisson with parameter $n$: $$\mathbf{P}(S_n=k)=e^{-n}\frac{n^k}{k!},\qquad \mathbb{E}[S_n]=\operatorname{var}(S_n)=n .$$ (One can also prove this by convolution or transforms, but the process argument is one line.)

(b) Stirling from the CLT. The plan: compute $\mathbf{P}(S_n=n)$ — the probability that the Poisson sum lands exactly on its own mean — in two ways, exactly and by the CLT, and equate.

Way 1: exactly. Put $k=n$ in the Poisson PMF from part (a): $$\mathbf{P}(S_n=n)=e^{-n}\frac{n^n}{n!} .$$

Way 2: by the CLT with the $1/2$ correction. Since $\mathbb{E}[S_n]=n$ and $\sigma_{S_n}=\sqrt n$, the standardized sum is $Z_n=(S_n-n)/\sqrt n$. Now, step by step: $$\mathbf{P}(S_n=n)=\mathbf{P}\Big(n-\tfrac12<S_n<n+\tfrac12\Big),$$ which is exact because $S_n$ is integer-valued — this is the $1/2$ correction of §2.4, and it is the reason the whole derivation works. Standardize by subtracting $n$ and dividing by $\sqrt n$: $$=\mathbf{P}\!\left(\frac{-1}{2\sqrt n}<\frac{S_n-n}{\sqrt n}\le\frac{1}{2\sqrt n}\right) \approx\mathbf{P}\!\left(\frac{-1}{2\sqrt n}<Z\le\frac{1}{2\sqrt n}\right),$$ the approximation being the CLT (with $Z$ standard normal). Write the last probability as an integral of the standard normal density: $$=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-1/(2\sqrt n)}^{1/(2\sqrt n)}e^{-z^2/2}\,dz .$$ [Gap filled — the "second approximation":] the interval of integration has length $$\frac{1}{2\sqrt n}-\Big(\frac{-1}{2\sqrt n}\Big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt n},$$ and it shrinks to zero as $n$ grows, so by the mean-value form of the fundamental theorem of calculus the integral equals (length) $\times$ (integrand at some interior point), and that interior point may be taken to be $z=0$ in the limit. Since $e^{-z^2/2}\big|_{z=0}=e^0=1$, $$\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\cdot 1=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}} .$$

Equate the two ways. $$e^{-n}\frac{n^n}{n!}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}} \;\Longrightarrow\; n!\approx n^n e^{-n}\sqrt{2\pi n}=\boxed{\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}},$$ which is Stirling's formula.

Historical footnote (from the solution sheet). De Moivre first found the form $n!\approx n^{n+1/2}e^{-n}\cdot(\text{constant})$ with a complicated expression for the constant; his friend Stirling identified the constant as $\sqrt{2\pi}$.

Interpretation (why this derivation is remarkable). Stirling's formula is a statement about factorials — pure combinatorics, no randomness anywhere. The CLT proves it by inventing a random variable whose PMF at one point happens to contain $n!$, and then reading that PMF off the normal curve. The $\sqrt{2\pi}$ in Stirling's formula is literally the $\sqrt{2\pi}$ in the normal density. Note also which ingredient carries the content: without the $1/2$ correction, the CLT would have said $\mathbf{P}(S_n=n)\approx 0$ and the derivation would have produced nothing.
Example 2.9 (L20 slide 8 — the Poisson-versus-normal paradox, resolved). The slide poses an argument decorated with "(??)" and "(????)": Poisson arrivals during a unit interval equal the sum of $n$ independent Poisson counts over $n$ intervals of length $1/n$. Let $n\to\infty$ and apply the CLT (??). Poisson = normal (????). What is wrong?
Solution (the resolution the lecturer gives orally; the slide writes only the question marks)

The argument, stated carefully. Fix a Poisson process of rate $\lambda=1$ and let $N$ be the number of arrivals in $[0,1]$, so $N\sim$ Poisson$(1)$. Split $[0,1]$ into $n$ subintervals of length $1/n$ and let $Y_i^{(n)}$ be the count in the $i$th. These are independent, identically distributed (each Poisson with parameter $1/n$), and $N=Y_1^{(n)}+\cdots+Y_n^{(n)}$ exactly. So $N$ is an i.i.d. sum of $n$ terms for every $n$. Letting $n\to\infty$ and "applying the CLT" would say $N$ is normal — but $N$ is Poisson$(1)$, a discrete variable taking the values $0,1,2,\dots$, which is certainly not normal.

The error. The CLT is a statement about a fixed sequence $X_1,X_2,\dots$ whose common distribution does not depend on $n$; the limit $n\to\infty$ adds new terms drawn from that same fixed distribution. In the argument above, the summands' distribution changes with $n$: the $i$th summand is Poisson$(1/n)$, and as $n$ grows each summand becomes more and more degenerate (it is $0$ with probability $e^{-1/n}\to1$). We are not adding more copies of one distribution; we are re-splitting one fixed random variable into ever finer pieces, whose sum is the same $N$ every time. [DERIVATION GAP filled: the slide marks the flaw with "(??)" but never writes the resolution.] Formally, the standardized sum here is $$\frac{N-n\cdot(1/n)}{\sqrt{n}\cdot\sqrt{1/n}}=\frac{N-1}{1},$$ which does not depend on $n$ at all — there is no limit being taken, and nothing converges to anything. The CLT hypothesis "i.i.d., distribution fixed as $n\to\infty$" is doing real work.

The correct picture — two different limits of the binomial (L20 slide 8). Both approximations to Binomial$(n,p)$ are limits, but along different paths:

  • $p$ fixed, $n\to\infty$: normal. Each summand keeps a fixed distribution, so the CLT applies. The variance $np(1-p)\to\infty$ and the distribution spreads over $\sqrt{n}$ values.
  • $np=\mu$ fixed, $n\to\infty$, $p\to0$: Poisson. The summands' distribution changes with $n$ (they become rarer and rarer), so the CLT does not apply; the Poisson limit of G4 §3 applies instead. The variance stays bounded at $np(1-p)\to\mu$.
The slide's two examples make the choice concrete: with $p=1/100$, $n=100$ use Poisson ($\mu=1$); with $p=1/10$, $n=500$ use normal ($np=50$, $\sigma=6.708204$). The numbers confirm it (key poisson_vs_normal): for $n=100$, $p=0.01$ the largest PMF error is $0.001850$ for the Poisson approximation and $0.124215$ for the normal one — the normal is off by a factor of $67$, because it cannot reproduce the huge atom $\mathbf{P}(S=0)=0.366$. For $n=500$, $p=0.1$ the largest CDF error is $0.013335$ for Poisson and $0.007906$ for normal, and the normal now wins.

Rule of thumb. Compare $np$ with $n$: if $np$ stays small (say $np\lesssim 10$) while $n$ is large, the mass is concentrated on a few integers and Poisson is the right model; if $np(1-p)$ is large (say $\ge 10$), the mass is spread over many integers and the normal approximation, with the $1/2$ correction, is the right model.

Practice 2.11. Use the Stirling derivation in reverse: estimate $\binom{2m}{m}2^{-2m}$ for large $m$, i.e. the probability that $2m$ fair coin flips give exactly $m$ heads.
Answer

$S_{2m}\sim$ Binomial$(2m,1/2)$ has mean $m$ and $\sigma_{S}=\sqrt{2m\cdot\tfrac14}=\sqrt{m/2}$. §2 cheatsheet — the Central Limit Theorem
  • Standardize: $Z_n=\dfrac{S_n-n\mathbb{E}[X]}{\sigma\sqrt n}$, which has $\mathbb{E}[Z_n]=0$ and $\operatorname{var}(Z_n)=1$ for every $n$. $\sqrt n$ is the unique scaling that keeps both moments fixed.
  • CLT (L20 slide 1; B&T §5.4): $X_i$ i.i.d. with finite $\sigma^2$ $\Rightarrow$ $\mathbf{P}(Z_n\le c)\to\Phi(c)$ for every $c$. Hypotheses: independence, identical distribution, finite variance — nothing else.
  • What converges is the CDF, not the PMF/PDF. For lattice $X_i$ every atom of $Z_n$ has probability $\to0$; the atoms merely get closer together as fast as they shrink.
  • Normal approximation (Recipe, §2.3): (1) write it as an i.i.d. sum; (2) $\mathbb{E}[S_n]=n\mathbb{E}[X]$, $\sigma_{S_n}=\sigma\sqrt n$; (3) $z=(c-n\mathbb{E}[X])/(\sigma\sqrt n)$; (4) $\mathbf{P}(S_n\le c)\approx\Phi(z)$, with $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$; (5) correct if integer; (6) use worst-case $\sigma^2$ if unknown.
  • $1/2$ correction / de Moivre–Laplace (L20 slides 6–7; B&T §5.4 p. 279): for integer-valued $S_n$, the bar at $k$ ↔ the area over $[k-\tfrac12,k+\tfrac12]$, so $\mathbf{P}(k\le S_n\le l)\approx\Phi\big(\frac{l+1/2-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\big)- \Phi\big(\frac{k-1/2-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\big)$. On Binomial$(36,\tfrac12)$ it cuts the worst CDF error from $0.066030$ to $0.000754$, and it is the only way to approximate a PMF value.
  • Never correct a continuous variable, and always widen away from the region you keep ($k-\tfrac12$ for an upper tail, $k+\tfrac12$ for a lower one).
  • Accuracy: no theorem gives a threshold $n$; symmetry helps enormously. Berry–Esseen: gap $\le 0.4748\,\rho/(\sigma^3\sqrt n)$ with $\rho=\mathbb{E}|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|^3$ — the rate is $1/\sqrt n$ and the constant is governed by skew.
  • Bounds vs. approximation (rec21 P1): on $\mathbf{P}(\sum_{1}^{10}U_i\ge7)$ the answers are Markov $0.714286$, Chebyshev+symmetry $0.104167$, Cantelli $0.172414$, CLT $0.014230$, exact $0.013463$. Markov/Chebyshev are guaranteed; the CLT is merely accurate.
  • Polling (L20 slide 4; B&T Example 5.11): for $\mathbf{P}(|M_n-f|\ge0.01)\le0.05$ use $\sigma\le\tfrac12$ and solve $2(1-\Phi(0.02\sqrt n))\le0.05$: $n=9604$, against Chebyshev's $50{,}000$ — a factor of $5.21$, constant in $\epsilon$ (but not in the confidence level $\delta$: the ratio is $1/(\delta(z^*)^2)$).
  • Stirling (rec21 P3): $\mathbf{P}(S_n=n)$ for Poisson$(n)$, computed exactly ($e^{-n}n^n/n!$) and by the corrected CLT ($1/\sqrt{2\pi n}$), gives $n!\approx\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^n$.
  • Do not push the CLT through a changing distribution (L20 slide 8): Binomial$(n,p)$ with $p$ fixed $\to$ normal; with $np$ fixed and $p\to0$ $\to$ Poisson. The "Poisson = normal" argument fails because the summands' distribution depends on $n$.
