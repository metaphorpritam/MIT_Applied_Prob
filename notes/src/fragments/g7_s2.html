§0 and §1 set the classical stage: $\theta$ is a fixed unknown constant, there is no prior, and an estimator $\hat\Theta_n$ is a rule that turns data into a guess. §1 studied one particular rule — the sample mean — and asked whether it is unbiased and consistent. But where do estimators come from? Writing down $\hat\Theta_n=(X_1+\cdots+X_n)/n$ requires already knowing that $\theta$ is a mean. If the unknown is the endpoint of a uniform interval, or the temperature inside a blackbody, or the slope of a line, there is no obvious sample average to reach for.

Maximum likelihood is the general-purpose answer, and it is a single sentence: among all candidate values of $\theta$, report the one under which the data you actually saw would have been the most probable. It is mechanical, it applies to any parametric model you can write down, and — as L24 slide 2 stresses — it is the exact classical mirror of the Bayesian MAP rule of G6 §3, with the prior deleted. This section defines the likelihood function carefully (§2.1), runs the recipe on the models where calculus works (§2.2 for the one-parameter cases, §2.3 for rec23 P2's joint mean-and-variance fit), on the model where calculus fails (§2.4), and then in full detail on the two recitation problems that close the course: the photon counter of rec24 P1 (§2.5) and the least-squares fits of rec24 P2 (§2.6). §2.7 collects the theoretical guarantees that make the recipe respectable.

2.1 The likelihood function, and what it is not

The classical model is a family of probability models indexed by the parameter. L23 slide 2 puts it bluntly: "mathematically: many models, one for each possible value of $\theta$." The notation carries a semicolon rather than a conditioning bar precisely to remind you that $\theta$ is a knob on the model, not an event you can condition on.

Definition (likelihood function; L23 slide 3, B&T §9.1 p. 462). Let $X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ be the data vector, with joint PMF $p_X(x;\theta)$ (discrete case) or joint PDF $f_X(x;\theta)$ (continuous case), where $\theta$ ranges over a set $\Theta_{\mathrm{par}}$ of candidate parameter values. After the data have been observed and frozen at the numerical value $x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, the likelihood function is $$L(\theta) \;=\; p_X(x;\theta)\qquad\text{or}\qquad L(\theta)\;=\;f_X(x;\theta),$$ viewed as a function of $\theta$ alone. The maximum likelihood (ML) estimate is $$\hat\theta_{\text{ML}} \;=\; \arg\max_{\theta}\; L(\theta),$$ and the ML estimator $\hat\Theta_n$ is the same rule applied to the random data $(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ — a random variable, written with a capital letter.

Every symbol: $n$ is the number of observations; $x_i$ is the numeric value of the $i$th observation; $\theta$ is the unknown but non-random parameter; $L(\theta)$ is a number attached to each candidate $\theta$; $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}$ is a number computed from the data; $\hat\Theta_n$ is the random variable you get by feeding random data into the same formula. The distinction between $\hat\theta$ (a number) and $\hat\Theta_n$ (a random variable) is exactly the distinction L23 slide 3 makes by printing both.

When the $X_i$ are independent — which they are in every model in this section — the joint PMF factors, so the likelihood is a product: $$L(\theta) \;=\; p_X(x;\theta) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_{X_i}(x_i;\theta),$$ by the definition of independence for random variables (G1 §3, G2 §4). Products of $n$ small numbers underflow and differentiate badly, so we almost always maximize the log-likelihood $$\ell(\theta) \;=\; \log L(\theta) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log p_{X_i}(x_i;\theta).$$ This is legitimate because $\log$ is strictly increasing: if $\ell(\theta_1)>\ell(\theta_2)$ then $L(\theta_1)>L(\theta_2)$ and conversely, so the two functions have exactly the same maximizers. L23 slide 3 performs this step silently in the exponential example; we will always write it out.

What the number $L(\theta)$ means

Interpretation. $L(\theta)$ is the probability (or probability density) of the data you already have, computed inside the model indexed by $\theta$. Maximizing it answers the question B&T §9.1 p. 463 phrases as: "What is the value of $\theta$ under which the observations we have seen are most likely to arise?" You are not asking which $\theta$ is probable — in the classical world $\theta$ has no probability. You are asking which model would have been least surprised by what happened.

Figure 2.1 makes the geometry concrete for $n=10$ tosses of a coin with unknown bias $\theta$, of which $k=7$ came up heads. The left panel shows four members of the model family — the binomial PMFs $p_X(k;\theta)=\binom{10}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{10-k}$ for $\theta\in\{0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\}$. Each set of stems is a legitimate PMF and sums to $1$ across the data axis. The right panel reads the four highlighted heights — the values at the observed $k=7$ — and plots them, together with all the intermediate $\theta$'s, against $\theta$. That curve is the likelihood function. It is assembled by taking one number out of each model, and there is no reason on earth for the collected numbers to add up to anything in particular.

Fig. 2.1 — Where the likelihood function comes from (L23 slide 3; B&T §9.1, its Figure 9.2). Left: four models, one per $\theta$; each stem set sums to $1$ across the data. Right: the same four numbers read off at the observed $k=7$, extended to all $\theta$. The area under the right-hand curve is $\int_0^1\binom{10}{7}\theta^7(1-\theta)^3\,d\theta=1/11=0.0909$, not $1$.
Gotcha. The likelihood is not a probability distribution over $\theta$, and $L(\theta)$ is not "the probability that the parameter equals $\theta$" (B&T §9.1 p. 463 issues this warning explicitly). Three symptoms of the same fact:
  • It does not integrate to 1 in $\theta$. For Figure 2.1, $\int_0^1 \binom{10}{7}\theta^{7}(1-\theta)^{3}\,d\theta = 120\cdot\frac{7!\,3!}{11!} = 120\times 0.000758 = 0.090909 = 1/11$. What does sum to $1$ is the sum over the data at any fixed $\theta$: $\sum_{k=0}^{10}\binom{10}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{10-k}=1$ at $\theta=0.3$ and at $\theta=0.7$ alike (numerically $1.000000$ in both cases) (verified numerically in computes/g7_s2.py, keys bern_binom_area, bern_datasum_0.3, bern_datasum_0.7).
  • In the continuous case it is a density in $x$, never in $\theta$. Consequently $L(\theta)$ can exceed $1$ — for the uniform model of §2.4 with $\theta=0.5$ and one observation, $L=1/\theta=2$. A "probability" of $2$ would be nonsense; a density value of $2$ is routine.
  • It is not invariant to reparametrization the way a density is. If you re-express the model in terms of $\psi=h(\theta)$, a density would pick up a Jacobian factor $|d\theta/d\psi|$. The likelihood does not: $L$ is just a relabeled list of numbers, so $\hat\psi_{\text{ML}}=h(\hat\theta_{\text{ML}})$ exactly. That is the invariance principle of §2.7 — and it is a property a genuine density would not have.

ML versus MAP: the same maximization, minus the prior

L23 slide 3 and L24 slide 2 place the two rules side by side: $$\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=\arg\max_\theta\ p_X(x;\theta), \qquad \hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\max_\theta\ p_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x) =\arg\max_\theta\ \frac{p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)\,p_\Theta(\theta)}{p_X(x)} .$$ (The lecture slide prints the denominator as $p_Y(y)$; by context the intended normalizer is $p_X(x)$ — a slide typo, flagged in the L24 transcript.) The denominator $p_X(x)$ does not involve $\theta$, so it can be dropped from the maximization, leaving $$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\max_\theta\ p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)\,p_\Theta(\theta).$$ Now suppose the prior is flat: $p_\Theta(\theta)=c$ for every candidate $\theta$ (or $f_\Theta(\theta)=c$ over a bounded range). Then the factor $c$ is a positive constant, and multiplying an objective by a positive constant does not move its maximizer, so $$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta\ c\cdot p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta) = \arg\max_\theta\ p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta) = \hat\theta_{\text{ML}},$$ where the last equality is the observation that the conditional PMF $p_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)$ and the classical model $p_X(x;\theta)$ are the same list of numbers, written with different punctuation. This is B&T §9.1 p. 463: ML estimation is MAP estimation with a flat prior.

Gotcha. "ML $=$ MAP with a flat prior" is a statement about the maximizer, not about the philosophy. The Bayesian keeps a whole posterior distribution and can say $\mathbf{P}(0.6\le\Theta\le0.8\mid X=x)=0.71$; the classical statistician cannot write that sentence down, because $\theta$ is not random. Also, "flat" is not reparametrization-proof: a prior that is flat in $\theta$ is not flat in $\theta^2$, so MAP moves under reparametrization while ML does not (§2.7). Finally, when the parameter range is unbounded there is no flat proper prior at all, so the equality is a limiting statement, not a literal one.
Recipe (maximum likelihood; L23 slide 3, B&T §9.1 pp. 462–466).
  1. Write the model. Identify the parameter $\theta$ (or vector $(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_m)$), its allowed range, and the PMF/PDF of a single observation, $p_{X_i}(x_i;\theta)$.
  2. Write the likelihood. Multiply over the independent observations: $L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n p_{X_i}(x_i;\theta)$. Keep track of any indicator factors that restrict the range of the data — they are what breaks calculus in §2.4.
  3. Take logs. $\ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n \log p_{X_i}(x_i;\theta)$. Drop additive constants that do not contain $\theta$; they cannot affect the argmax.
  4. Maximize. If $\ell$ is differentiable and the maximum is interior, solve $d\ell/d\theta=0$ (one equation per unknown parameter). If the range of the data depends on $\theta$, do not differentiate: argue directly about where $L$ is largest.
  5. Check that it is a maximum. Either sign-check $\ell'$ on both sides of the stationary point, or verify $\ell''<0$ there, or note that $\ell$ is concave. A stationary point can be a minimum or a saddle.
  6. Promote the estimate to an estimator. Replace $x_i$ by $X_i$ to get $\hat\Theta_n$, then ask the §1 questions: is it unbiased? consistent? what is its mean squared error? what confidence interval goes with it?

Practice 2.1. You observe a single value $x=3$ from a Poisson($\theta$) distribution. Evaluate $L(\theta)$ at $\theta=1,3,5$, and say in one sentence what each number is. Then explain why $L(3)\approx0.224$ is not "the probability that $\theta=3$."
Solution

The model is $p_X(k;\theta)=e^{-\theta}\theta^k/k!$, so with the data frozen at $k=3$ the likelihood is $L(\theta)=e^{-\theta}\theta^3/6$. Then $L(1)=e^{-1}/6=0.367879/6=0.061313$, $L(3)=e^{-3}\cdot27/6=0.049787\times4.500000=0.224042$, and $L(5)=e^{-5}\cdot125/6=0.006738\times20.833333=0.140374$.

Each number is a probability of the data: $L(1)$ is the probability that a Poisson(1) source emits exactly $3$ counts, $L(3)$ the probability that a Poisson(3) source does, and $L(5)$ the probability that a Poisson(5) source does. Three different models, three different probabilities, all attached to the same observed outcome.

It is not the probability that $\theta=3$, first and foremost because $\theta$ is not a random variable in the classical framework — it has no probability, so the sentence has no referent. This particular model is a nice trap, because the Poisson likelihood does happen to integrate to $1$ over $\theta$: $\int_0^\infty e^{-\theta}\theta^{k}/k!\,d\theta=\Gamma(k+1)/k!=k!/k!=1$ for every $k$ (numerically $1.000000$ at $k=3$). That is a coincidence of this one parametrization, not a licence to read $L$ as a density. The test that never lies is the reparametrization test of the Gotcha above: re-express the model in terms of $\psi=\sqrt{\theta}$, so $L^{*}(\psi)=e^{-\psi^{2}}\psi^{6}/6$, and now $\int_0^\infty L^{*}(\psi)\,d\psi=\Gamma(7/2)/12=0.276946\ne1$. A genuine density would have picked up the Jacobian $|d\theta/d\psi|=2\psi$ and stayed normalized; the likelihood does not, because it was never a density in the parameter.

Practice 2.2. A classmate says: "MAP with a flat prior equals ML, so Bayesian and classical inference are the same thing." Give two concrete things a Bayesian can report that a classical statistician cannot, and one situation where the two point estimates genuinely differ even with a flat prior.
Solution

(i) A Bayesian can report $\mathbf{P}(\Theta\in[a,b]\mid X=x)$ — an honest probability about the unknown. The classical statistician's confidence interval (§1) is a probability statement about the interval, not about $\theta$. (ii) A Bayesian can report the posterior mean $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X=x]$, the LMS estimate of G6 §4, which minimizes mean squared error over all estimators; no such optimality statement exists classically because there is no distribution over $\theta$ to average against.

They differ whenever the Bayesian summarizes the posterior by something other than its mode. B&T Example 9.2 is the canonical case: with a flat prior and $k$ heads in $n$ tosses, the MAP estimate (and hence the ML estimate) is $k/n$, but the posterior mean is $(k+1)/(n+2)$. For $n=10$, $k=7$ these are $0.7$ versus $8/12=0.6667$. They agree only asymptotically.

2.2 The smooth case: differentiate the log-likelihood

Bernoulli / binomial (B&T Example 9.2; L23 slide 2's coin)

Model: $X_1,\dots,X_n$ i.i.d. Bernoulli($\theta$), $\theta\in[0,1]$ the unknown probability of a head, $x_i\in\{0,1\}$ the observed outcomes, and $k=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$ the number of heads.

Step 1–2, the likelihood. A single observation has $p_{X_i}(x_i;\theta)=\theta^{x_i}(1-\theta)^{1-x_i}$ — check: at $x_i=1$ this is $\theta$, at $x_i=0$ it is $1-\theta$. By independence, $$L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n \theta^{x_i}(1-\theta)^{1-x_i} =\theta^{\sum_i x_i}(1-\theta)^{n-\sum_i x_i}=\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k},$$ collecting exponents. Step 3, logs: $$\ell(\theta)=k\log\theta+(n-k)\log(1-\theta).$$ Step 4, differentiate: using $\frac{d}{d\theta}\log\theta=1/\theta$ and, by the chain rule, $\frac{d}{d\theta}\log(1-\theta)=-1/(1-\theta)$, $$\ell'(\theta)=\frac{k}{\theta}-\frac{n-k}{1-\theta} \;=\;0 \;\Longrightarrow\; k(1-\theta)=(n-k)\theta \;\Longrightarrow\; k-k\theta=n\theta-k\theta \;\Longrightarrow\; k=n\theta,$$ where the first implication multiplies through by $\theta(1-\theta)>0$ (legal for $0<\theta<1$) and the $k\theta$ terms cancel. Hence $$\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=\frac{k}{n},\qquad \hat\Theta_n=\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n}.$$ Step 5, check: $\ell''(\theta)=-k/\theta^2-(n-k)/(1-\theta)^2<0$ for $0<\theta<1$ whenever $0<k<n$, so $\ell$ is strictly concave and the stationary point is the global maximum. (If $k=0$ the likelihood $(1-\theta)^n$ is decreasing and the maximum is at the boundary $\hat\theta=0$; if $k=n$ it is at $\hat\theta=1$ — the boundary cases the derivative argument misses.)

Numbers. With $n=10$, $k=7$: $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=7/10=0.7$. The sequence likelihood $\theta^7(1-\theta)^3$ takes the values $0.00007501$ at $\theta=0.3$, $0.00097656$ at $0.5$, $0.00179159$ at $0.6$, $\mathbf{0.00222357}$ at $0.7$, $0.00167772$ at $0.8$ and $0.00047830$ at $0.9$ — a genuine interior peak at $0.7$. So this coin's data are about $29.6$ times more probable under $\theta=0.7$ than under $\theta=0.3$ ($0.00222357/0.00007501$). Note that the ML estimator is exactly the sample mean of §1, so everything §1 proved applies: it is unbiased, $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]=\theta$, and consistent by the weak law (B&T Example 9.2). For the polling data of rec23 P3, $n=1200$ with $684$ supporters gives $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=684/1200=0.57$; §1 builds the confidence interval around it.

Exponential (L23 slide 3; B&T Example 9.3)

L23 slide 3 states this example and skips three steps; here they are. Model: $X_1,\dots,X_n$ i.i.d. exponential with rate $\theta>0$, so $f_{X_i}(x_i;\theta)=\theta e^{-\theta x_i}$ for $x_i\ge0$. The interpretation of B&T Example 9.3 is that $X_i$ are interarrival times and $\theta$ is the arrival rate.

$$L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n \theta e^{-\theta x_i} =\theta^{n}\exp\Big(-\theta\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\Big)=\theta^n e^{-\theta y_n},\qquad y_n=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i,$$ using $\prod e^{a_i}=e^{\sum a_i}$. Taking logs, and using $\log(ab)=\log a+\log b$ and $\log e^{u}=u$: $$\ell(\theta)=n\log\theta-\theta y_n .$$ Differentiating, $\ell'(\theta)=n/\theta-y_n$. Setting this to zero gives $n/\theta=y_n$, i.e. $$\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=\frac{n}{y_n}=\frac{n}{x_1+\cdots+x_n},\qquad \hat\Theta_n=\frac{n}{X_1+\cdots+X_n}.$$ The second derivative is $\ell''(\theta)=-n/\theta^2<0$ for all $\theta>0$, so $\ell$ is strictly concave and this is the global maximum — the check L23 slide 3 omits.

On the five interarrival times $0.42,\ 1.31,\ 0.08,\ 2.05,\ 0.64$: $y_5=4.5$, the sample mean is $0.9$, and $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=5/4.5=1.111111$. Panel (c) of Figure 2.3 shows the log-likelihood peaking there.

L23 slide 4 uses this same estimator to illustrate that ML need not be unbiased: with $n=1$, $\hat\Theta_1=1/X_1$ and $\mathbb{E}[1/X_1]=\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{x}\theta e^{-\theta x}dx=\infty$, because near $x=0$ the integrand behaves like $\theta/x$, whose integral diverges logarithmically. So $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_1]\ne\theta$ — spectacularly. It is consistent: by the weak law $(X_1+\cdots+X_n)/n\to\mathbb{E}[X]=1/\theta$ in probability, and since $g(t)=1/t$ is continuous at $t=1/\theta\ne0$, convergence in probability is preserved under $g$, giving $\hat\Theta_n=g\big((X_1+\cdots+X_n)/n\big)\to g(1/\theta)=\theta$.

Practice 2.3 (B&T Problem 9.7). Derive the ML estimator of the parameter $\lambda$ of a Poisson random variable from i.i.d. observations $X_1,\dots,X_n$. Apply it to the counts $3,1,4,0,2,5,2,3$. Is it unbiased? consistent?
Solution

$p_{X_i}(x_i;\lambda)=e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x_i}/x_i!$ for $x_i=0,1,2,\dots$ Hence $$L(\lambda)=\prod_{i=1}^n \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x_i}}{x_i!} =e^{-n\lambda}\,\lambda^{\sum_i x_i}\Big/\prod_i x_i! ,$$ and, taking logs and discarding the constant $-\log\prod_i x_i!$ (no $\lambda$ in it), $$\ell(\lambda)=-n\lambda+\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\Big)\log\lambda .$$ Differentiating: $\ell'(\lambda)=-n+\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_i x_i$, which is zero when $\lambda=\frac1n\sum_i x_i$. Since $\ell''(\lambda)=-\frac{1}{\lambda^2}\sum_i x_i<0$ (assuming at least one nonzero count), this is the global maximum. So $\hat\lambda_{\text{ML}}=\frac1n\sum_i x_i$ — the sample mean again.

Numerically: $\sum_i x_i=3+1+4+0+2+5+2+3=20$ with $n=8$, so $\hat\lambda=20/8=2.5$.

Unbiased: $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Lambda_n]=\frac1n\sum_i\mathbb{E}[X_i]=\frac1n\cdot n\lambda=\lambda$ for every $\lambda$. Consistent: by the weak law the sample mean converges in probability to $\mathbb{E}[X]=\lambda$.

Bonus (invariance, §2.7). The ML estimate of $\mathbf{P}(X=0)=e^{-\lambda}$ is $e^{-\hat\lambda}=e^{-2.5}=0.082085$, not the empirical fraction of zeros, which is $1/8=0.1250$. Both are reasonable estimators; only the first is the ML one.

2.3 Normal: mean and variance together (rec23 P2)

Example 2.1 (rec23 Problem 2 — B&T Example 9.4, p. 464). Estimate the mean $\mu$ and the variance $v$ of a normal distribution from $n$ independent observations $X_1,\dots,X_n$. (The recitation solution sheet contains no worked solution — only the pointer "Example 9.4 in the textbook (page 464)" — so everything below fills that gap.)
Solution

Step 1, the model. The parameter is now a two-dimensional vector $\theta=(\mu,v)$ with $\mu\in\mathbb{R}$ and $v>0$; here $\mu$ is the unknown mean and $v$ the unknown variance (not the standard deviation — this matters when we differentiate). A single observation has density $$f_{X_i}(x_i;\mu,v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2v}\Big\}.$$

Step 2, the likelihood. By independence, $$f_X(x;\mu,v)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2v}\Big\} =\frac{1}{(2\pi v)^{n/2}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{1}{2v}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\mu)^2\Big\},$$ where the $n$ copies of $(2\pi v)^{-1/2}$ multiply to $(2\pi v)^{-n/2}$ and the exponents add.

Step 3, logs. Using $\log(abc)=\log a+\log b+\log c$ and $\log e^{u}=u$: $$\ell(\mu,v)=-\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac{n}{2}\log v-\frac{1}{2v}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\mu)^2 .$$ The first term is a constant and can be ignored for maximization, but we keep it so the numerical values below are the true log-likelihood.

Step 4a, differentiate in $\mu$. Only the last term contains $\mu$. By the chain rule, $\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}(x_i-\mu)^2=-2(x_i-\mu)$, so $$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu}=-\frac{1}{2v}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(-2(x_i-\mu)\big) =\frac{1}{v}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\mu).$$ Setting this to zero and multiplying by $v>0$: $\sum_i (x_i-\mu)=0$, i.e. $\sum_i x_i - n\mu=0$, so $$\hat\mu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i=m_n,$$ the sample mean. This is the fact L23 slide 5 asserts without proof ("Sample mean often turns out to also be the ML estimate. E.g., if $X_i\sim N(\theta,\sigma^2)$") — that unsolved example is now solved.

Step 4b, differentiate in $v$. Now use $\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\log v=1/v$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\big(\tfrac{1}{2v}S\big)=-\tfrac{S}{2v^2}$ where $S=\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2$ does not involve $v$: $$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial v}=-\frac{n}{2v}+\frac{1}{2v^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\mu)^2 .$$ Set to zero, multiply through by $2v^2>0$: $$-nv+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\mu)^2=0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat v=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\hat\mu)^2=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-m_n)^2 ,$$ substituting the already-found $\hat\mu=m_n$ (legitimate because the $\mu$-equation did not involve $v$, so the pair solves both equations simultaneously). Writing these as estimators, in §1's symbols, $$M_n=\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n},\qquad \bar S_n^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i-M_n)^2 .$$ Note the bar: §1.6 reserved $\bar S_n^2$ for the divisor-$n$ version and $\hat S_n^2$ for the divisor-$(n-1)$ sample variance, exactly as L23 slide 7 does, and this section keeps that convention. Maximum likelihood delivers the barred one.

Step 5, check it is a maximum. B&T's footnote on p. 464 gives the clean route. For every $i$ write $x_i-\mu=(x_i-m_n)+(m_n-\mu)$ and square: $$(x_i-\mu)^2=(x_i-m_n)^2+2(x_i-m_n)(m_n-\mu)+(m_n-\mu)^2 .$$ Sum over $i$. The cross term vanishes because $\sum_i (x_i-m_n)=\sum_i x_i-n m_n=0$ by the definition of $m_n$, so $$\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\mu)^2=n s_n^2+n(m_n-\mu)^2,\qquad s_n^2=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-m_n)^2 .$$ Substituting into the likelihood gives B&T's factored form $$f_X(x;\mu,v)=\frac{1}{(2\pi v)^{n/2}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{n s_n^2}{2v}\Big\} \exp\Big\{-\frac{n(m_n-\mu)^2}{2v}\Big\}.$$ Read it off: for any fixed $v>0$ the only $\mu$-dependent factor is $\exp\{-n(m_n-\mu)^2/2v\}\le1$, with equality exactly at $\mu=m_n$ — so $\hat\mu=m_n$ is the global maximizer in $\mu$, no second-derivative test needed. Plugging $\mu=m_n$ back leaves $g(v)=(2\pi v)^{-n/2}e^{-ns_n^2/2v}$, whose log is $-\frac n2\log(2\pi)-\frac n2\log v-\frac{n s_n^2}{2v}$ with derivative $-\frac{n}{2v}+\frac{n s_n^2}{2v^2}=\frac{n}{2v^2}(s_n^2-v)$: positive for $v<s_n^2$, negative for $v>s_n^2$. So $g$ increases then decreases, and $\hat v=s_n^2$ is the global maximum.

A worked numerical instance. Take the $n=10$ sample $4.62,\ 6.28,\ 3.31,\ 7.05,\ 5.44,\ 2.87,\ 5.91,\ 4.03,\ 6.77,\ 3.52$. The sum is $49.80$, so $$\hat\mu=m_{10}=\frac{49.80}{10}=4.9800 .$$ The deviations $x_i-m_{10}$ are $-0.36,\ 1.30,\ -1.67,\ 2.07,\ 0.46,\ -2.11,\ 0.93,\ -0.95,\ 1.79,\ -1.46$ (they sum to $0$, as they must), and $\sum_i (x_i-m_{10})^2=20.6602$. The ML variance estimator is biased — and by exactly how much

Compare the ML answer $\bar S_n^2=\frac1n\sum_i(X_i-M_n)^2$ with the sample variance $\hat S_n^{2}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i(X_i-M_n)^2$ of §1.6–§1.7 that L23 slide 7 and rec23 P3 prefer. They differ only in the denominator, and the ML one is smaller. Here is the exact computation of its expectation, for any distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $v$ (normality is not needed).

Start from the algebraic identity proved above, with the true mean $\mu$ in place of the generic $\mu$: $\sum_i (X_i-\mu)^2=n\bar S_n^2+n(M_n-\mu)^2$, hence $$\bar S_n^2=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\mu)^2-(M_n-\mu)^2 .$$ Take expectations term by term (linearity of expectation):

  • $\mathbb{E}\big[(X_i-\mu)^2\big]=v$ for each $i$, by the definition of variance; so the first term contributes $\frac1n\cdot n\cdot v=v$.
  • $\mathbb{E}\big[(M_n-\mu)^2\big]=\operatorname{var}(M_n)=v/n$, because $M_n$ has mean $\mu$ (so this is exactly its variance) and, by independence, $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_i \operatorname{var}(X_i)=\frac{nv}{n^2}=v/n$.
Therefore $$\mathbb{E}\big[\bar S_n^2\big]=v-\frac{v}{n}=\frac{n-1}{n}\,v, \qquad\text{bias}=\mathbb{E}\big[\bar S_n^2\big]-v=-\frac{v}{n}.$$ Dividing by $n-1$ instead of $n$ multiplies by $n/(n-1)$ and restores exactness: $\mathbb{E}[\hat S_n^{2}]=v$. That is the derivation L23 slide 7 states but does not carry out.

Numerically, at $v=4$: $\mathbb{E}[\bar S_n^2]=2.0000$ at $n=2$ (bias $-2$), $3.2000$ at $n=5$ (bias $-0.8$), $3.6000$ at $n=10$ (bias $-0.4$), $3.9600$ at $n=100$ (bias $-0.04$) and $3.9960$ at $n=1000$ (bias $-0.004$). A $400{,}000$-replication Monte Carlo at $n=5$, $v=4$ returns an average $\bar S_5^2$ of $3.2015$ against the predicted $3.2000$. In the worked sample above, the ratio of the two estimates is exactly $\hat v/\hat s^2_{10}=2.0660/2.2956=0.9=(n-1)/n$.

Interpretation. The ML variance under-shoots because it measures spread about $M_n$, and $M_n$ is the point that minimizes the sum of squared deviations for this particular sample. Any other center — including the true $\mu$ — would give a larger sum. So the sample looks tighter than the population is, by exactly the amount of "wobble" in the center, namely $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=v/n$. The $n-1$ denominator is the bookkeeping fix. Note that ML is not trying to be unbiased: it optimizes a different objective, and B&T p. 465 records $\bar S_n^2$ as merely asymptotically unbiased, since $(n-1)/n\to1$.
Gotcha. Do not "fix" the ML answer by reflex. §1's MSE decomposition $\mathrm{MSE}=\operatorname{var}+(\text{bias})^2$ (L23 slide 4) means an unbiased estimator can have a larger mean squared error than a biased one. Unbiasedness is a property, not a goal, and which of $\bar S_n^2$ and $\hat S_n^{2}$ you want depends on what you are optimizing. Also note that $\sqrt{\hat S_n^{2}}$ is not an unbiased estimator of the standard deviation even though $\hat S_n^{2}$ is unbiased for the variance — unbiasedness does not survive nonlinear transformation, while ML (§2.7) does.

2.4 When calculus fails: the uniform $[0,\theta]$ model (rec23 P1)

Example 2.2 (rec23 Problem 1 — B&T Example 9.1, p. 463). Romeo and Juliet start dating, but Juliet is late on any date by a random amount $X$, uniformly distributed over $[0,\theta]$; the parameter $\theta>0$ is unknown. Juliet was late by $x$ on the first date. Find the ML estimate of $\theta$. (The recitation solution sheet again only points at the textbook, so every step below fills that gap.)
Solution

The likelihood, written honestly. The uniform density on $[0,\theta]$ is $$f_X(x;\theta)=\begin{cases}1/\theta, & 0\le x\le\theta,\\[2pt] 0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$ The crucial feature — the one that breaks the recipe's calculus branch — is that the second line depends on $\theta$: the support of the data moves with the parameter. With the data frozen at $x$, view this as a function of $\theta$: $$L(\theta)=\begin{cases}1/\theta, & \theta\ge x,\\[2pt] 0,&\theta<x.\end{cases}$$ The condition $0\le x\le\theta$ has been re-read as $\theta\ge x$; that is the whole trick.

Maximize by inspection. For $\theta<x$ the likelihood is $0$ — such a model could not have produced the observation at all. For $\theta\ge x$ it is $1/\theta$, which is strictly decreasing in $\theta$. So the largest value of $L$ over the feasible region is attained at the smallest feasible $\theta$, namely $\theta=x$, where $L=1/x$. Hence $$\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=x .$$ Why not differentiate? Because $dL/d\theta=-1/\theta^2$ is never $0$ — there is no stationary point anywhere. The maximum is a corner: $L$ jumps from $0$ up to $1/x$ at $\theta=x$ and then slides down. Setting the derivative to zero would return nothing at all.

With $n$ observations. By independence, $$L(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n} f_{X_i}(x_i;\theta) =\begin{cases}\theta^{-n}, & \theta\ge\max_i x_i,\\[2pt] 0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}$$ because the product of the $n$ indicator conditions "$\theta\ge x_i$ for every $i$" is exactly the single condition "$\theta\ge\max_i x_i$", and the $n$ copies of $1/\theta$ multiply to $\theta^{-n}$. The same argument applies: $\theta^{-n}$ is strictly decreasing, so $$\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=\max_{i}x_i,\qquad \hat\Theta_n=\max_i X_i .$$ Numerically, on the five lateness values $3.1,\ 7.4,\ 2.2,\ 6.9,\ 5.3$: $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=7.4$, at which $L=7.4^{-5}=4.507\times10^{-5}$. Compare $L(7.0)=0$ (infeasible — a model with $\theta=7$ could never have produced the observation $7.4$), $L(8.0)=8^{-5}=3.052\times10^{-5}$ and $L(10.0)=10^{-5}=1.000\times10^{-5}$: strictly downhill from $7.4$. And the derivative at the optimum is $dL/d\theta=-5(7.4)^{-6}=-3.045\times10^{-5}\ne0$.

Bayesian footnote. B&T Example 9.1 notes that the corresponding MAP estimate in Example 8.2 (a flat prior on $[0,1]$) is also $x$ — the ML/MAP correspondence of §2.1 in action, and the same model rec22 treated Bayesianly in G6 §3.

Fig. 2.4 — The corner solution (rec23 P1; B&T Example 9.1 and Problem 9.8). (a) The likelihood has no stationary point: the maximum sits at the left edge of the feasible set $\{\theta\ge\max_i x_i\}$. (b) Because $\max_i X_i\le\theta$ always, the ML estimator can only under-shoot; at $n=5$, $\theta=8$ its mean is $\frac56\theta=6.667$.

Is $\max_i X_i$ any good? Bias, variance and MSE

The estimator $\hat\Theta_n=\max_i X_i$ is never larger than $\theta$, so it must be biased downward. Here is the exact calculation. Let $M=\max_i X_i$. For $0\le m\le\theta$, $$F_M(m)=\mathbf{P}(M\le m)=\mathbf{P}(X_1\le m,\dots,X_n\le m) =\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}(X_i\le m)=\Big(\frac{m}{\theta}\Big)^{n},$$ using independence and the uniform CDF $\mathbf{P}(X_i\le m)=m/\theta$ on $[0,\theta]$. Differentiating gives the density $f_M(m)=n m^{n-1}/\theta^{n}$ (G3 §4). Then $$\mathbb{E}[M]=\int_0^{\theta} m\,\frac{n m^{n-1}}{\theta^{n}}\,dm =\frac{n}{\theta^{n}}\int_0^{\theta} m^{n}\,dm =\frac{n}{\theta^{n}}\cdot\frac{\theta^{n+1}}{n+1} =\frac{n}{n+1}\,\theta,$$ $$\mathbb{E}[M^{2}]=\frac{n}{\theta^{n}}\int_0^{\theta} m^{n+1}dm=\frac{n}{n+2}\theta^{2}, \qquad \operatorname{var}(M)=\frac{n}{n+2}\theta^2-\Big(\frac{n}{n+1}\Big)^2\theta^2 =\frac{n\,\theta^{2}}{(n+1)^{2}(n+2)} ,$$ where the last simplification puts the two fractions over the common denominator $(n+1)^2(n+2)$ and uses $n(n+1)^2-n^2(n+2)=n(n^2+2n+1)-n^2(n+2)=n$. The bias is $\mathbb{E}[M]-\theta=-\theta/(n+1)$, and by the §1 decomposition $$\mathrm{MSE}=\operatorname{var}+(\text{bias})^2 =\frac{n\theta^2}{(n+1)^2(n+2)}+\frac{\theta^2}{(n+1)^2} =\frac{\theta^2\big(n+(n+2)\big)}{(n+1)^2(n+2)} =\frac{2\theta^{2}}{(n+1)(n+2)},$$ the last step using $n+n+2=2(n+1)$ and cancelling one factor of $n+1$.

Numbers (all in computes/g7_s2.py, key unif_moment_table): $\mathbb{E}[M]/\theta=0.500000$ at $n=1$, $0.833333$ at $n=5$, $0.909091$ at $n=10$, $0.980392$ at $n=50$, $0.990099$ at $n=100$; correspondingly $\mathrm{MSE}/\theta^2$ falls from $0.3333$ to $1.941\times10^{-4}$. At $n=5$, $\theta=8$ the theory gives $\mathbb{E}[M]=8\cdot\frac56=6.6667$, and a $400{,}000$-replication simulation returns $6.6670$.

Interpretation. The bias $-\theta/(n+1)$ vanishes as $n\to\infty$, so $\hat\Theta_n$ is asymptotically unbiased, and since its MSE $\to0$ it is consistent. Notice the speed: the error decays like $1/n$, not like $1/\sqrt n$. Estimating a boundary is a fundamentally easier problem than estimating a mean, because every new observation can only push the estimate up toward the truth, never randomly past it. This is also why the CLT-based confidence intervals of §1 do not apply here: the limiting distribution of $n(\theta-\hat\Theta_n)$ is exponential, not normal, so §2.7's asymptotic-normality guarantee explicitly excludes this model.
Gotcha. The tell-tale sign that you must not differentiate is that the range of the data depends on the parameter — here $0\le x_i\le\theta$. Whenever an indicator function of $\theta$ appears in the density, treat the likelihood as a function with a cliff in it and reason about the feasible set first, the shape second. Mechanically writing "$\ell(\theta)=-n\log\theta$, so $\ell'(\theta)=-n/\theta=0$" gives no solution and, worse, invites the answer "$\theta=\infty$" if you misread the sign.

An easy repair: since $\mathbb{E}[M]=\frac{n}{n+1}\theta$, the rescaled estimator $\tilde\Theta_n=\frac{n+1}{n}\max_i X_i$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\tilde\Theta_n]=\frac{n+1}{n}\cdot\frac{n}{n+1}\theta=\theta$ exactly — unbiased for every $n$ (this is what B&T Problem 9.8 asks for). On the rec23 data with $n=5$, the factor is $6/5=1.2$ and the corrected estimate is $1.2\times7.4=8.880$. Note it is not the ML estimate, and it can exceed every observation — which is exactly the point, since $\theta$ must.

Practice 2.5 (B&T Problem 9.9). $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are i.i.d. uniform on $[\theta,\theta+1]$, with $\theta$ unknown. Find the ML estimator. Apply it to $4.30,\ 4.95,\ 4.62,\ 5.10,\ 4.41$. What is unusual about the answer?
Solution

Each density is $f_{X_i}(x_i;\theta)=1$ for $\theta\le x_i\le\theta+1$ and $0$ otherwise. So $$L(\theta)=\begin{cases}1,&\theta\le x_i\le\theta+1\text{ for every }i,\\ 0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$ The joint condition says $\theta\le\min_i x_i$ and $\theta+1\ge\max_i x_i$, i.e. $$\max_i x_i-1\;\le\;\theta\;\le\;\min_i x_i .$$ On that whole interval $L=1$; outside it $L=0$. So every $\theta$ in the interval is a maximum likelihood estimate — the ML estimate is not unique.

Numbers: $\max_i x_i=5.10$ and $\min_i x_i=4.30$, so any $\theta\in[5.10-1,\ 4.30]=[4.10,\ 4.30]$ works; the set has width $0.20$. Common conventions pick the midpoint $4.20$ or one of the endpoints. What is unusual is precisely the non-uniqueness: the ML recipe returns a set, and any tie-break is a choice made outside the ML principle. (As $n$ grows the interval shrinks to a point, so the ambiguity is asymptotically harmless and the estimator is consistent.)

Practice 2.6. For the uniform $[0,\theta]$ model with $n=5$, compare the MSE of the ML estimator $M=\max_i X_i$ with that of the bias-corrected $\tilde\Theta_5=\frac65 M$, both as multiples of $\theta^2$. Which would you rather use?
Solution

ML: $\mathrm{MSE}(M)=\frac{2\theta^2}{(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac{2\theta^2}{6\times7} =\frac{\theta^2}{21}=0.047619\,\theta^2$.

Corrected: it is unbiased, so its MSE equals its variance, $\operatorname{var}\big(\tfrac65 M\big)=\big(\tfrac65\big)^2\operatorname{var}(M) =\frac{36}{25}\cdot\frac{5\theta^2}{6^2\cdot7}=\frac{36}{25}\cdot\frac{5\theta^2}{252} =\frac{180}{6300}\theta^2=\frac{\theta^2}{35}=0.028571\,\theta^2$.

Here the correction wins on both counts — it removes the bias and lowers the MSE by $40\%$ ($0.028571/0.047619=0.6$). So for this model the bias-corrected estimator dominates, and one would prefer it. (That is a fact about this model, not a general rule: §2.3's Gotcha remains — a bias correction can raise the MSE in other problems.)

2.5 rec24 Problem 1: the blackbody photon counter, end to end

Example 2.3 (rec24 Problem 1 — B&T Problem 9.10). A blackbody at temperature $\theta$ radiates photons. The number $K$ of photons detected in a fixed one-second interval has PMF $$p_K(k;\theta)=\frac{1}{Z(\theta)}\,e^{-k/\theta},\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots$$ where $Z(\theta)$ is a normalization factor (physicists: the partition function). Photon counts in non-overlapping intervals are independent. (a) Determine $Z(\theta)$. (b) Compute $\mu_K=\mathbb{E}_\theta[K]$ and $\sigma_K^2=\operatorname{var}_\theta(K)$. (c) From counts $k_1,\dots,k_n$ in $n$ non-overlapping one-second intervals, find the ML estimator $\hat\Theta_n$ of $\theta$, assuming a hot body $\theta\gg1$ (you may use $1/(e^{1/\theta}-1)\approx\theta$). (d) Find $n$ such that the noise-to-signal ratio of $\hat K=\frac1n\sum_i K_i$ equals $0.01$. (e) Give a $95\%$ confidence interval for that situation.
Solution

(a) The partition function. A PMF must sum to $1$ over its range: $$1=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_K(k;\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-k/\theta}}{Z(\theta)} =\frac{1}{Z(\theta)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\big(e^{-1/\theta}\big)^{k},$$ where the last step rewrites $e^{-k/\theta}=(e^{-1/\theta})^k$ by the law of exponents, exposing a geometric series with ratio $r=e^{-1/\theta}$. For any $\theta>0$ we have $1/\theta>0$, hence $0<r<1$ and the series converges (the recitation solution uses this silently); the geometric sum formula of G2 §1 gives $\sum_{k\ge0}r^k=1/(1-r)$. Therefore $$1=\frac{1}{Z(\theta)}\cdot\frac{1}{1-e^{-1/\theta}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;Z(\theta)=\frac{1}{1-e^{-1/\theta}}\;}$$ Sanity check at $\theta=1$: $Z=1/(1-e^{-1})=1/(1-0.367879)=1.5820$; at $\theta=5$, $Z=5.5167$; at $\theta=100$, $Z=100.5008$. For large $\theta$, $Z(\theta)\approx\theta$ — as it must, since a hot body spreads its counts over about $\theta$ values.

(b) Mean and variance — by recognizing a shifted geometric. Substitute $Z(\theta)$ back: $$p_K(k;\theta)=\big(1-e^{-1/\theta}\big)\,\big(e^{-1/\theta}\big)^{k},\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots$$ Set $p=1-e^{-1/\theta}$, so that $e^{-1/\theta}=1-p$ and $$p_K(k;\theta)=(1-p)^{k}\,p,\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots$$ This is the geometric PMF of G2 §1, shifted one unit left: the usual geometric random variable $N$ (the trial number of the first success) has $p_N(j)=(1-p)^{j-1}p$ for $j=1,2,\dots$, so $K=N-1$ counts the failures before the first success. Here $p=1-e^{-1/\theta}$ plays the role of the success probability. At $\theta=100$, $p=0.009950$; at $\theta=5$, $p=0.181269$; at $\theta=1$, $p=0.632121$.

G2 §3 gives $\mathbb{E}[N]=1/p$ and $\operatorname{var}(N)=(1-p)/p^2$. Shifting by the constant $-1$ moves the mean by $-1$ and leaves the variance unchanged (variance is translation invariant): $$\mu_K=\mathbb{E}[N]-1=\frac1p-1,\qquad \sigma_K^2=\operatorname{var}(N)=\frac{1-p}{p^{2}} .$$ Now put $p=1-e^{-1/\theta}$ back. For the mean, put both terms over the common denominator $1-e^{-1/\theta}$: $$\mu_K=\frac{1}{1-e^{-1/\theta}}-1=\frac{1-(1-e^{-1/\theta})}{1-e^{-1/\theta}} =\frac{e^{-1/\theta}}{1-e^{-1/\theta}} .$$ Multiply numerator and denominator by $e^{1/\theta}$ (a positive number, so nothing changes), and use $e^{-1/\theta}e^{1/\theta}=1$: $$\mu_K=\frac{e^{-1/\theta}\cdot e^{1/\theta}}{\big(1-e^{-1/\theta}\big)e^{1/\theta}} =\frac{1}{e^{1/\theta}-1}.$$ For the variance, $1-p=e^{-1/\theta}$ and $p^2=(1-e^{-1/\theta})^2$, so $$\sigma_K^2=\frac{e^{-1/\theta}}{\big(1-e^{-1/\theta}\big)^{2}} .$$ The recitation asserts the identity $\sigma_K^2=\mu_K^2+\mu_K$; here is the algebra. Write $u=e^{-1/\theta}$ and $q=1-u$, so $\mu_K=u/q$ and $\sigma_K^2=u/q^2$. Then $$\mu_K^{2}+\mu_K=\frac{u^{2}}{q^{2}}+\frac{u}{q} =\frac{u^{2}}{q^{2}}+\frac{uq}{q^{2}} =\frac{u^{2}+uq}{q^{2}} =\frac{u\,(u+q)}{q^{2}} =\frac{u\cdot 1}{q^{2}} =\frac{u}{q^{2}}=\sigma_K^{2},$$ using $u+q=u+(1-u)=1$ in the second-to-last step. So $$\boxed{\;\mu_K=\frac{1}{e^{1/\theta}-1},\qquad \sigma_K^{2}=\mu_K^{2}+\mu_K\;}$$ Checks at $\theta=100$: $\mu_K=99.500833$, $\sigma_K^2=99.500833^2+99.500833=9999.9167$, matching $(1-p)/p^2=9999.9167$; $\sigma_K=99.9996$. Summing the PMF numerically over $k=0,\dots,399{,}999$ gives $1.000000$, mean $99.500833$ and variance $9999.9167$ — the formulas check out exactly.

Note $\sigma_K\approx\mu_K$ when $\mu_K$ is large: a hot blackbody's photon count fluctuates by about $100\%$ of its own mean. That is why part (d) needs ten thousand samples.

(c) The ML estimator. By independence across the $n$ intervals, $$p_K(k_1,\dots,k_n;\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{e^{-k_i/\theta}}{Z(\theta)} =\frac{1}{Z(\theta)^{n}}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_i\Big),$$ again adding exponents. Taking logs, with $\log Z(\theta)^{-n}=-n\log Z(\theta)$: $$\ell(\theta)=-n\log Z(\theta)-\frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_i = n\log\big(1-e^{-1/\theta}\big)-\frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_i ,$$ where the second form substitutes $Z(\theta)=1/(1-e^{-1/\theta})$, so $-\log Z(\theta)=\log(1-e^{-1/\theta})$.

Differentiate — the chain rule the solution sheet skips. First, $\frac{d}{d\theta}\big(-\tfrac1\theta\big)=\tfrac{1}{\theta^{2}}$, so the second term contributes $+\frac{1}{\theta^2}\sum_i k_i$. For the first term, put $g(\theta)=1-e^{-1/\theta}$. Then $$\frac{d}{d\theta}e^{-1/\theta}=e^{-1/\theta}\cdot\frac{d}{d\theta}\Big(-\frac1\theta\Big) =e^{-1/\theta}\cdot\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad g'(\theta)=-\frac{e^{-1/\theta}}{\theta^{2}},$$ and by the chain rule $\frac{d}{d\theta}\,n\log g(\theta)=n\,g'(\theta)/g(\theta)$, i.e. $$\frac{d}{d\theta}\,n\log\big(1-e^{-1/\theta}\big) =-\,n\,\frac{e^{-1/\theta}}{\theta^{2}\big(1-e^{-1/\theta}\big)} .$$ Adding the two pieces and setting $\ell'(\theta)=0$: $$-\,n\,\frac{e^{-1/\theta}}{\theta^{2}\big(1-e^{-1/\theta}\big)}+\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_i=0 .$$ Multiply through by $\theta^{2}/n$ (positive, so the equation is unchanged): $$\frac{e^{-1/\theta}}{1-e^{-1/\theta}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_i \;=\; s_n ,$$ the sample mean of the counts. But the left-hand side is exactly $\mu_K$, which part (b) simplified to $1/(e^{1/\theta}-1)$. So the ML equation is $$\frac{1}{e^{1/\theta}-1}=s_n \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \mu_K(\theta)=s_n .$$ The ML estimator sets the model mean equal to the sample mean.

Second-order check (missing from the solution sheet). $\mu_K(\theta)=1/(e^{1/\theta}-1)$ is strictly increasing in $\theta$ (as $\theta$ grows, $1/\theta$ shrinks, $e^{1/\theta}$ shrinks toward $1$, and the reciprocal blows up), running from $0$ at $\theta\to0^+$ to $\infty$ as $\theta\to\infty$. Hence $\ell'(\theta)=\frac{n}{\theta^2}\big(s_n-\mu_K(\theta)\big)$ is positive while $\mu_K(\theta)<s_n$ and negative once $\mu_K(\theta)>s_n$: $\ell$ rises then falls, so the unique stationary point is the global maximum.

Solve. Inverting $1/(e^{1/\theta}-1)=s_n$ exactly: $e^{1/\theta}-1=1/s_n$, so $e^{1/\theta}=1+1/s_n$, so $1/\theta=\log(1+1/s_n)$ and $$\hat\theta_{\text{exact}}=\frac{1}{\log\!\big(1+1/s_n\big)} .$$ For a hot body, $\theta\gg1$, the recitation's approximation $1/(e^{1/\theta}-1)\approx\theta$ applies — it is the first-order Taylor expansion $e^{u}\approx1+u$ at $u=1/\theta\approx0$, giving $e^{1/\theta}-1\approx1/\theta$ — and the ML equation collapses to $\theta\approx s_n$: $$\boxed{\;\hat\Theta_n=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_i\;}$$ the sample mean of the photon counts.

How good is the approximation? Expanding more carefully, $\frac{1}{\log(1+1/s)}=s+\frac12+O(1/s)$, so the exact estimate exceeds the hot-body one by about $1/2$, whatever $s_n$ is — the relative error therefore dies like $1/(2s_n)$. Tabulating exact versus approximate (key photon_ml_table): $s_n=0.5\Rightarrow0.910239$ vs $0.5$ ($-45.07\%$); $s_n=2\Rightarrow2.466303$ vs $2$ ($-18.91\%$); $s_n=10\Rightarrow10.492059$ vs $10$ ($-4.69\%$); $s_n=99.5\Rightarrow99.999167$ vs $99.5$ ($-0.50\%$); $s_n=250\Rightarrow250.499667$ vs $250$ ($-0.20\%$). The problem asks for two significant digits, and the approximation delivers them once $s_n\gtrsim50$.

A concrete data set. Twelve one-second counts $53,132,8,214,61,97,19,156,44,88,3,121$ sum to $996$, so $s_{12}=996/12=83.000000$. The hot-body ML estimate is $\hat\theta=83.000000$ (in whatever temperature units $\theta$ carries); the exact ML estimate is $1/\log(1+1/83)=83.499002$. Direct numerical maximization of $\ell$ returns $83.499002$ — a check that the calculus above is right. The log-likelihood values are $\ell(60)=-65.831996$, $\ell(80)=-65.109241$, $\ell(83.499)=-65.098088$ (the peak), $\ell(120)=-65.799866$, $\ell(160)=-67.164566$. Panel (b) of Figure 2.5 draws this curve.

(d) How many samples? The estimator is $\hat K=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n K_i$. Its mean is $\mu_{\hat K}=\mathbb{E}[\hat K]=\frac1n\sum_i\mathbb{E}[K_i]=\mu_K$ (unbiased), and — by independence, exactly, with no appeal to the CLT — $$\operatorname{var}(\hat K)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{var}(K_i) =\frac{n\sigma_K^{2}}{n^{2}}=\frac{\sigma_K^{2}}{n}, \qquad \sigma_{\hat K}=\frac{\sigma_K}{\sqrt n}.$$ Requiring the noise-to-signal ratio to equal $0.01$: $$\frac{\sigma_{\hat K}}{\mu_{\hat K}}=\frac{\sigma_K}{\sqrt n\,\mu_K}=0.01 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sqrt n=\frac{100\,\sigma_K}{\mu_K} =100\,\frac{\sqrt{\mu_K^{2}+\mu_K}}{\mu_K} =100\sqrt{\frac{\mu_K^{2}+\mu_K}{\mu_K^{2}}} =100\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{\mu_K}},$$ where $\sigma_K=\sqrt{\mu_K^2+\mu_K}$ comes from part (b) and the square root is pulled inside by dividing numerator and denominator under the radical by $\mu_K^2$. Squaring, $$n=10^{4}\Big(1+\frac{1}{\mu_K}\Big).$$ For a hot body $\mu_K\gg1$, the bracket $\to1$ and $n\to10^{4}=10{,}000$ — the recitation's answer, with the arithmetic shown. Exactly, at $\theta=100$ (so $\mu_K=99.500833$): $\sqrt n=100\sqrt{1+1/99.500833}=100.501252$, hence $n>10100.5017$, i.e. $n=10{,}101$ intervals — about $2.8$ hours of counting. The bracket matters only for cool bodies: $\mu_K=10$ needs $n=11{,}000$ and $\mu_K=1$ needs $n=20{,}000$.

Note on the problem statement. The solution sheet writes parts (d)–(e) in terms of the temperature estimator $\hat\Theta_n$ while the question poses them for the count estimator $\hat K$; in the hot-body limit of part (c) these are the same random variable, $\hat\Theta_n=\hat K$, which is why the two labels are used interchangeably. The question asks for equality (ratio $=0.01$) and the official solution works with the inequality ($<0.01$); both give $n\approx10^4$.

(e) The confidence interval. Part (d) fixed $\sigma_{\hat K}=\sigma_K/\sqrt n=0.01\,\mu_K$. Because $\hat K$ is an average of $n\approx10^4$ i.i.d. terms, the central limit theorem (G6 §2) says $(\hat K-\mu_K)/\sigma_{\hat K}$ is approximately standard normal, so for any $z>0$ $$\mathbf{P}\big(|\hat K-\mu_K|\le z\,\sigma_{\hat K}\big)\approx\Phi(z)-\Phi(-z)=2\Phi(z)-1 .$$ Setting $2\Phi(z)-1=0.95$ gives $\Phi(z)=0.975$, and the standard normal table (or scipy.stats.norm.ppf(0.975)) gives $z=1.959964\approx1.96$ — that is where the famous $1.96$ comes from, and checking backwards, $\Phi(1.96)=0.975002$ so $2\Phi(1.96)-1=0.950004$. Rearranging the event $|\hat K-\mu_K|\le1.96\,\sigma_{\hat K}$ into an interval for $\mu_K$ gives the $95\%$ confidence interval $$\big[\hat K-1.96\,\sigma_{\hat K},\ \hat K+1.96\,\sigma_{\hat K}\big] =\big[\hat K-1.96\times0.01\,\mu_K,\ \hat K+1.96\times0.01\,\mu_K\big] =\big[\hat K-0.0196\,\mu_K,\ \hat K+0.0196\,\mu_K\big].$$ At $\theta=100$, $\mu_K=99.500833$, so the half-width is $0.0196\times99.500833=1.950216$ counts: the mean photon count is pinned to about $\pm2$ out of $\approx99.5$, i.e. $\pm1.96\%$. In practice $\mu_K$ on the right is replaced by the estimate $\hat K$ itself (the §1 plug-in trick), giving the usable interval $\hat K(1\pm0.0196)$.

Practice 2.8. In rec24 P1(b) the variance satisfies $\sigma_K^2=\mu_K^2+\mu_K$. (i) What does that say about the coefficient of variation $\sigma_K/\mu_K$ for a very hot body? (ii) Contrast with a Poisson($\lambda$) count, for which $\sigma^2=\lambda$. (iii) Which of the two models needs more samples to pin its mean to $1\%$?
Solution

(i) $\dfrac{\sigma_K}{\mu_K}=\dfrac{\sqrt{\mu_K^2+\mu_K}}{\mu_K}=\sqrt{1+1/\mu_K}\to1$ as $\mu_K\to\infty$. The relative fluctuation of a single geometric count does not shrink as the body heats up: it stays at about $100\%$.

(ii) For Poisson, $\sigma/\lambda=\sqrt\lambda/\lambda=1/\sqrt\lambda\to0$: a bright Poisson source is self-averaging, a single observation already pins the mean to a relative accuracy of $1/\sqrt\lambda$.

(iii) The blackbody. Requiring $\sigma/(\sqrt n\,\mu)=0.01$ gives $n=10^4(1+1/\mu_K)\approx10^4$ for the geometric model, but $n=10^4/\lambda$ for the Poisson model — at $\lambda=100$ that is just $100$ intervals, a hundredfold saving. The heavy geometric tail is what costs you the extra data.

2.6 rec24 Problem 2: least squares is maximum likelihood

L24 slide 3 makes a claim that ties this section to the next one: the least-squares criterion that defines linear regression is not an arbitrary choice of penalty — it is the ML rule under the assumption of independent zero-mean normal noise. §3 develops regression proper; here we prove the ML connection and then carry out rec24 P2 numerically.

The reduction (L24 slide 3; B&T §9.2, pp. 478–479). Model the data as $$Y_i=\theta_0+\theta_1 x_i+W_i,\qquad i=1,\dots,n,$$ where the $x_i$ are known constants (not random), $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$ are the unknown parameters, and the noises $W_i$ are i.i.d. $N(0,\sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2$ known. Given $(\theta_0,\theta_1)$, the variable $Y_i$ is normal with mean $\theta_0+\theta_1x_i$ and variance $\sigma^2$, so $$f_Y(y;\theta_0,\theta_1)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}} \exp\Big\{-\frac{(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big\} = c\cdot\exp\Big\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^{2}\Big\},$$ with $c=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-n/2}$ a constant that contains no $\theta$. This is exactly the likelihood printed on L24 slide 3. Taking logs, $$\ell(\theta_0,\theta_1)=\log c-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^{2}.$$ The first term is constant and the coefficient $-1/(2\sigma^2)$ is a negative constant, so maximizing $\ell$ is the same as minimizing the sum $\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^2$. That is the least-squares criterion $(*)$ of L24 slide 3 — "take logs, same as $(*)$", now with the two suppressed observations stated: the constant $c$ drops out, and $\log$ is increasing while $-1/(2\sigma^2)$ flips max into min.

Interpretation. Squared error is not a law of nature; it is the fingerprint of the Gaussian. Choose Laplace noise $f_W(w)\propto e^{-|w|/b}$ instead and the same computation delivers least absolute deviations, $\min\sum_i|y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i|$, whose solution is a median-like fit that ignores outliers. L24 slide 3's summary — "least sq. $\leftrightarrow$ pretend $W_i$ i.i.d. normal" — is the honest statement: least squares is a modeling assumption in disguise. Note also that $\sigma^2$ cancels out of the minimization entirely, so the ML estimates of $\theta_0,\theta_1$ do not require knowing the noise level.

The solution formulas. Setting the two partial derivatives of $Q(\theta_0,\theta_1)=\sum_i (y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^2$ to zero gives (§3 derives them in full; they are quoted on L24 slide 4 and used by rec24 P2) $$\hat\theta_1=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\overline x)(y_i-\overline y)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\overline x)^{2}}, \qquad \hat\theta_0=\overline y-\hat\theta_1\overline x, \qquad \overline x=\frac1n\sum_i x_i,\quad \overline y=\frac1n\sum_i y_i .$$

Example 2.4 (rec24 Problem 2). For the five data pairs $$x: 0.8,\ 2.5,\ 5,\ 7.3,\ 9.1 \qquad y: -2.3,\ 20.9,\ 103.5,\ 215.8,\ 334$$ find the ML estimates of the parameters of (a) the linear model $Y_i=\theta_0+\theta_1x_i+W_i$ and (b) the quadratic model $Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i^{2}+V_i$, with $W_i\sim N(0,\sigma_1^2)$ and $V_i\sim N(0,\sigma_2^2)$ independent. Solution

(a) Linear model. The reduction above says: ML $=$ least squares, so use the formulas. 2.7 Why maximum likelihood is worth trusting

The recipe is easy to justify intuitively, but three theoretical properties are what make it the default method in practice. B&T states all three in the summary box on p. 466; none is proved in 6.041, so they are stated with their conditions and illustrated.

Property 1 (invariance principle; B&T §9.1 p. 465). If $\hat\theta_n$ is the ML estimate of $\theta$, then for any one-to-one function $h$, the ML estimate of the reparametrized quantity $\psi=h(\theta)$ is $h(\hat\theta_n)$.

Why it is true. The likelihood is a function on the parameter set; renaming the points of that set by $\psi=h(\theta)$ relabels the graph without changing any of its values, so the location of the peak is transported by $h$ and nothing else happens. This is where the §2.1 Gotcha pays off: a genuine density would acquire a Jacobian factor under relabeling and its peak would move. Example: with Poisson data, Practice 2.3's ML estimate of $\lambda$ is $2.5$, so the ML estimate of $\mathbf{P}(X=0)=e^{-\lambda}$ is $e^{-2.5}=0.082085$; and for the blackbody, the ML estimate of $\mu_K$ is $\mu_K(\hat\theta)$, which by the ML equation of §2.5 is exactly $s_n$. Contrast with unbiasedness, which is not invariant: the §2.3 Gotcha noted that an unbiased variance estimator gives a biased standard-deviation estimator.

Property 2 (consistency; B&T §9.1 pp. 465–466). When the $X_i$ are i.i.d. and under mild additional assumptions, each component of the ML estimator is consistent: $\hat\Theta_n\to\theta$ in probability, for every $\theta$.

Every example in this section obeys it, and in each one you can see the mechanism: the ML equation typically matches a sample average to a model quantity (Bernoulli: $\hat\theta=k/n$; normal: $\hat\mu=m_n$; blackbody: $\mu_K(\hat\theta)=s_n$), and the weak law makes the sample average converge; continuity of the matching then transfers convergence to $\hat\Theta_n$, as in the exponential argument of §2.2. Figure 2.3(a) shows the geometry: the log-likelihood peak sharpens as $n$ grows, so the data pin down $\theta$ ever more tightly.

Property 3 (asymptotic normality; B&T §9.1 p. 465). For a scalar parameter and under mild conditions, the distribution of $\big(\hat\Theta_n-\theta\big)/\sigma(\hat\Theta_n)$, where $\sigma^2(\hat\Theta_n)$ is the variance of $\hat\Theta_n$, approaches the standard normal. For a vector parameter, the same holds component-wise.

This is what licenses the §1 machinery for ML estimators in general: if you can also estimate $\sigma(\hat\Theta_n)$, then $\hat\Theta_n\pm z\,\hat\sigma(\hat\Theta_n)$ with $\Phi(z)=1-\alpha/2$ is an approximate $1-\alpha$ confidence interval — exactly the construction rec24 P1(e) carried out.

Gotcha. The words "under mild conditions" in Property 3 exclude precisely the uniform $[0,\theta]$ model of §2.4, because the range of the data depends on the parameter. There $\hat\Theta_n=\max_i X_i$ converges at rate $1/n$ rather than $1/\sqrt n$, its limiting distribution is exponential rather than normal, and it is biased for every finite $n$ — so no normal approximation, and none of the §1 confidence intervals. What is not lost is Property 2: $\max_i X_i$ is perfectly consistent, as §2.4's own Interpretation box proves (its MSE $2\theta^2/((n+1)(n+2))\to0$). Fast-but-non-normal is not the same as unreliable. The moral is narrower than "corner solutions break the theory": they break the shape of the limit, so their error bars must be computed by hand, which is exactly what §2.4 did.
Practice 2.10. $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are i.i.d. exponential with rate $\theta$ (§2.2). Using the invariance principle, write down the ML estimate of the mean interarrival time $\tau=1/\theta$ and of the probability $\mathbf{P}(X>1)=e^{-\theta}$, for the data $0.42,\ 1.31,\ 0.08,\ 2.05,\ 0.64$.
Solution

From §2.2, $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=n/\sum_i x_i=5/4.5=1.111111$. Both $\tau=1/\theta$ and $g(\theta)=e^{-\theta}$ are one-to-one on $\theta>0$, so invariance applies directly.

$\hat\tau=1/\hat\theta=1/1.111111=0.900000$ — which is just the sample mean $4.5/5=0.9$, as it must be, since inverting the estimator inverts the formula.

$\widehat{\mathbf{P}(X>1)}=e^{-\hat\theta}=e^{-1.111111}=0.329193$. Note this is not the empirical fraction of observations exceeding $1$, which is $2/5=0.4$: the two are different estimators of the same quantity, and only the first is the ML one.

§2 cheatsheet.
  • Likelihood. Freeze the data, sweep the parameter: $L(\theta)=p_X(x;\theta)$ or $f_X(x;\theta)$. It is a probability (density) of the data, never a distribution over $\theta$; it need not integrate to $1$ in $\theta$ (Fig. 2.1: area $=1/11$).
  • Recipe. Write $L=\prod_i p_{X_i}(x_i;\theta)$; take logs; if the data range does not move with $\theta$, solve $\ell'(\theta)=0$ and check $\ell''<0$; otherwise argue directly (Fig. 2.2).
  • ML $=$ MAP with a flat prior (L24 slide 2; B&T §9.1 p. 463), because the constant prior factors out of the maximization. But only the point estimate coincides.
  • Catalogue. Bernoulli: $\hat\theta=k/n$. Exponential: $\hat\theta=n/\sum_i x_i$ (biased at $n=1$, consistent). Normal: $\hat\mu=m_n$, $\hat v=\frac1n\sum_i(x_i-m_n)^2$ with $\mathbb{E}[\bar S_n^2]=\frac{n-1}{n}v$ (the $n-1$ denominator gives §1's unbiased $\hat S_n^2$). Uniform $[0,\theta]$: $\hat\theta=\max_i x_i$, with $\mathbb{E}[\max]=\frac{n}{n+1}\theta$ and $\mathrm{MSE}=\frac{2\theta^2}{(n+1)(n+2)}$. Poisson: $\hat\lambda=\overline x$. Blackbody: $\mu_K(\hat\theta)=s_n$, i.e. $\hat\theta=1/\log(1+1/s_n)\approx s_n$ when hot.
  • rec24 P1 numbers. $Z(\theta)=1/(1-e^{-1/\theta})$; $\mu_K=1/(e^{1/\theta}-1)$; $\sigma_K^2=\mu_K^2+\mu_K$; $n=10^4(1+1/\mu_K)\approx10{,}000$ for a $1\%$ noise-to-signal ratio; $95\%$ CI $=\hat K\pm0.0196\,\mu_K$.
  • Least squares $=$ ML with Gaussian noise (L24 slide 3): maximizing $c\,e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^2}$ is minimizing the SSE. rec24 P2: $y=40.53x-65.86$ (SSE $3455.00$) versus $y=4.09x^2-3.07$ (SSE $28.07$).
  • Guarantees. Invariance ($\hat\psi=h(\hat\theta)$, exact and always true), consistency and asymptotic normality (i.i.d. plus mild conditions). The uniform $[0,\theta]$ model is still consistent but violates the normality conditions: its limit law is exponential, at rate $1/n$.
