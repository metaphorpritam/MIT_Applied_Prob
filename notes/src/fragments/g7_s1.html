§0 made the classical turn: the unknown $\theta$ is a fixed number, not a random variable, so there is no prior, no posterior, and no $\mathbf{P}(\theta \in \cdot)$ to speak of. What survives is a family of models $p_X(x;\theta)$ — one model per candidate value of $\theta$ — and a function of the data that we hope lands near the truth. This section is about that function: what makes one estimator better than another, and how to report not just a guess but a range.

Three ideas organize everything. Bias asks whether the estimator is centered on the truth; consistency asks whether it converges to the truth as data accumulate; mean squared error combines the two into a single number and splits exactly as $\text{bias}^2+\text{variance}$ (L23 slide 4). Then confidence intervals turn a point estimate into an interval whose endpoints are random and which brackets $\theta$ with prescribed probability. The machinery is entirely borrowed from G6: the weak law gives consistency, and the central limit theorem gives the interval. What is new is the interpretation, and it is the single most misunderstood idea in statistics — §1.5 is devoted to getting it right.

1.1 Estimators, bias, and consistency

Before we can rank estimators we have to be precise about what one is, and in particular about the distinction the classical framework lives or dies on: the rule you apply to the data is random, the number it returns on your data is not, and the thing you are chasing is a fixed constant that is neither. The three definitions below pin those objects down and then attach the two properties L23 slide 4 asks of any estimator — that it be centered on the truth, and that it find the truth eventually.

Definition (estimator, estimate, estimation error; B&T §9.1). Let $X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ be the observation vector, whose distribution $p_X(x;\theta)$ (or $f_X(x;\theta)$ in the continuous case) depends on the unknown but non-random parameter $\theta$. An estimator is a random variable $$\hat{\Theta}_n \;=\; g(X_1,\dots,X_n)$$ for some function $g$ that does not involve $\theta$ (you could not compute it if it did). Its realized numerical value on the observed data $x_1,\dots,x_n$ is the estimate $\hat\theta_n=g(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ — a number, not a random variable. The estimation error is $$\tilde\Theta_n \;=\; \hat{\Theta}_n-\theta,$$ the amount by which the estimator misses. Because the distribution of $X$ depends on $\theta$, so does the distribution of $\hat\Theta_n$; we write $\mathbb{E}_\theta[\cdot]$ and $\operatorname{var}_\theta(\cdot)$ when that dependence matters, and drop the subscript when the context is clear.
Gotcha — capital hat versus lowercase hat. L23 keeps these strictly apart (see the lecture's notation footnote): $\hat\Theta_n$ is the estimator, a random variable, and it is what you take expectations and variances of; $\hat\theta_n$ is the estimate, the single number your data produced. "$\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_n]$" is meaningless — it is the expectation of a constant. Conversely "the estimator equals $0.57$" is sloppy: the estimator is a function; $0.57$ is its value on one data set. Throughout G7, capital $\hat\Theta$ = random, lowercase $\hat\theta$ = number, and plain $\theta$ = the unknown constant we are chasing. Contrast this with G6 §3–§4, where $\Theta$ itself was random and had a prior.
Definition (bias, unbiasedness, consistency; L23 slide 4, B&T §9.1). With $\hat\Theta_n$ an estimator of $\theta$:
  • The bias is the expected estimation error, $$b_\theta(\hat\Theta_n)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_\theta[\hat\Theta_n-\theta]\;=\;\mathbb{E}_\theta[\hat\Theta_n]-\theta .$$
  • $\hat\Theta_n$ is unbiased if $\mathbb{E}_\theta[\hat\Theta_n]=\theta$ for every possible value of $\theta$.
  • $\hat\Theta_n$ is asymptotically unbiased if $\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_\theta[\hat\Theta_n]=\theta$ for every $\theta$.
  • $\hat\Theta_n$ is consistent if $\hat\Theta_n\to\theta$ in probability as $n\to\infty$, for every $\theta$: for every $\epsilon>0$, $\mathbf{P}_\theta(|\hat\Theta_n-\theta|\ge\epsilon)\to 0$ (convergence in probability, G6 §1).
Interpretation — why L23 slide 4 shouts "should hold FOR ALL $\theta$ !!!". In the classical world there is no prior to average over, so a property that holds only at some values of $\theta$ is worthless: you do not know which $\theta$ you are in. An estimator that is unbiased at $\theta=1/2$ and wildly biased elsewhere is not unbiased. This universal quantifier is exactly what makes classical guarantees strong and hard to obtain — and it is why the "estimator" $\hat\Theta_n\equiv 0$, which has zero variance, is nonetheless useless: it is correct only at $\theta=0$ (B&T §9.1 makes this point with $\mathrm{MSE}=\theta^2$).
Example 1.1 (the exponential estimator of L23 slides 3–4, worked exactly). Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be i.i.d. exponential with parameter $\theta>0$, so $f_{X_i}(x;\theta)=\theta e^{-\theta x}$ for $x\ge 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=1/\theta$. The maximum likelihood estimator of $\theta$ (derived in §2; here we only study its properties) is $$\hat\Theta_n\;=\;\frac{n}{X_1+\cdots+X_n}\;=\;\frac{1}{M_n},\qquad M_n=\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n}.$$ The slide asserts (i) that at $n=1$ it is infinitely biased, $\mathbb{E}[1/X_1]=\infty$, and (ii) that it is nevertheless consistent. Prove both, and compute the bias exactly for every $n$.

(i) $\mathbb{E}[1/X_1]=\infty$. By the expected-value rule for a function of a continuous random variable (G3 §1), $$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{X_1}\right]=\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{x}\,\theta e^{-\theta x}\,dx .$$ The integrand blows up at $x\to 0$ like $\theta/x$, and $\int_0^c dx/x$ diverges. To see it cleanly, bound the integrand from below on the window $0<x<1/\theta$, where $e^{-\theta x}\ge e^{-1}$: $$\int_0^\infty \frac{\theta e^{-\theta x}}{x}\,dx \;\ge\; \int_0^{1/\theta}\frac{\theta e^{-1}}{x}\,dx\;=\;\theta e^{-1}\Big[\ln x\Big]_{0^+}^{1/\theta}=+\infty,$$ since $\ln x\to-\infty$ as $x\downarrow 0$. So the integral diverges and the estimator has no finite mean at $n=1$: its bias is infinite. Numerically, cutting the integral off at $x>\epsilon$ with $\theta=1$ gives $4.03793$, $8.633225$, $17.843465$ for $\epsilon=10^{-2},10^{-4},10^{-8}$ (A_trunc_eps0.01, A_trunc_eps0.0001, A_trunc_eps1e-08) — growing like $\ln(1/\epsilon)$, i.e. without bound.

(ii) The exact bias for $n\ge 2$. The sum $S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n$ of $n$ i.i.d. exponential($\theta$) variables is Erlang of order $n$ (G4 §3): $$f_{S_n}(s)=\frac{\theta^n s^{\,n-1}e^{-\theta s}}{(n-1)!},\qquad s\ge 0 .$$ Therefore, for $n\ge 2$, $$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{S_n}\right] &=\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{s}\cdot\frac{\theta^n s^{\,n-1}e^{-\theta s}}{(n-1)!}\,ds &&\text{expected-value rule}\\[3pt] &=\frac{\theta^n}{(n-1)!}\int_0^\infty s^{\,n-2}e^{-\theta s}\,ds &&\text{cancel one power of }s,\text{ pull out constants}\\[3pt] &=\frac{\theta^n}{(n-1)!}\cdot\frac{(n-2)!}{\theta^{\,n-1}} &&\int_0^\infty s^{k}e^{-\theta s}ds=k!/\theta^{k+1}\text{ with }k=n-2\ge 0\\[3pt] &=\frac{\theta}{n-1}. &&(n-2)!/(n-1)!=1/(n-1) \end{aligned} $$ (The step requires $n-2\ge 0$; at $n=1$ the exponent $s^{-1}$ is not integrable at the origin, which is exactly part (i).) Multiplying by $n$, $$\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]\;=\;n\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{S_n}\right]\;=\;\frac{n}{n-1}\,\theta,\qquad\text{so}\qquad b_\theta(\hat\Theta_n)=\frac{n}{n-1}\theta-\theta=\frac{\theta}{n-1}.$$ The estimator systematically overshoots. Numbers (A_Ehat_n*, A_bias_n*): the multiplier $n/(n-1)$ is $2$ at $n=2$ (a 100% bias!), $1.25$ at $n=5$, $1.1111$ at $n=10$, $1.0101$ at $n=100$. Quadrature confirms $\mathbb{E}[1/S_5]=0.25=\theta/(n-1)$ at $\theta=1$ (A_quad_check_n5), and $400\,000$ simulated samples at $n=5$ give $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_5]/\theta=1.2513$ against the exact $1.25$ (A_sim_mean_n5).

(iii) Consistency. Two steps, the second of which L23 slide 4 leaves implicit. First, the $X_i$ are i.i.d. with finite mean $1/\theta$, so the weak law of large numbers (G6 §1) gives $$M_n=\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n}\;\longrightarrow\;\mathbb{E}[X_i]=\frac{1}{\theta}\qquad\text{in probability}.$$ Second, convergence in probability is preserved by continuous functions: if $Y_n\to c$ in probability and $g$ is continuous at $c$, then $g(Y_n)\to g(c)$ in probability. Apply this with $g(t)=1/t$, which is continuous at $t=1/\theta>0$: $$\hat\Theta_n=\frac{1}{M_n}=g(M_n)\;\longrightarrow\;g(1/\theta)=\theta\qquad\text{in probability}.$$ So $\hat\Theta_n$ is consistent — for every $\theta>0$ — even though it is biased at every finite $n$.

(iv) Debiasing. Since $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]=\frac{n}{n-1}\theta$ exactly, the rescaled estimator $\frac{n-1}{n}\hat\Theta_n=\frac{n-1}{S_n}$ is exactly unbiased for every $n\ge 2$ and every $\theta$. This is the same trick that turns $\frac1n\sum(X_i-\hat\Theta_n)^2$ into the $\frac{1}{n-1}$ sample variance of §1.6.

Gotcha — unbiased and consistent are independent properties. Neither implies the other; Example 1.1 already shows biased-but-consistent. The other three corners:
  • Unbiased, not consistent: $\hat\Theta_n=X_1$ (throw away all data but the first observation). Then $\mathbb{E}[X_1]=\theta$ for every $n$ and every $\theta$ — flawlessly unbiased — yet its variance stays at $\sigma^2$ forever (J_X1_var), so it never concentrates and is not consistent.
  • Biased, consistent: $\hat\Theta_n=M_n+1/n$. Its bias is $1/n$ — $0.1$, $0.01$, $0.001$ at $n=10,100,1000$ (J_shift_bias_n10, J_shift_bias_n100, J_shift_bias_n1000) — never zero, but it vanishes, and $M_n\to\theta$ drags the whole thing to $\theta$.
  • Neither: $\hat\Theta_n\equiv 0$, unless $\theta$ happens to be $0$.
Unbiasedness is a statement about the center of the sampling distribution at fixed $n$; consistency is a statement about the whole distribution as $n\to\infty$.
Practice 1.1. $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are i.i.d. uniform on $[0,\theta]$ with $\theta>0$ unknown. Consider $\hat\Theta_n=\max\{X_1,\dots,X_n\}$. (a) Find $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]$ and its bias. (b) Is it consistent? (c) Build an exactly unbiased estimator from it.
Solution

(a) The maximum is below $t$ exactly when every observation is, so by independence, for $0\le t\le\theta$, $$F_{\hat\Theta_n}(t)=\mathbf{P}(\max_i X_i\le t)=\prod_{i=1}^n\mathbf{P}(X_i\le t)=\left(\frac{t}{\theta}\right)^{\!n},$$ using the uniform CDF $\mathbf{P}(X_i\le t)=t/\theta$. Differentiating, $f_{\hat\Theta_n}(t)=n t^{n-1}/\theta^n$ on $[0,\theta]$, so $$\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]=\int_0^\theta t\cdot\frac{n t^{n-1}}{\theta^n}\,dt=\frac{n}{\theta^n}\cdot\frac{\theta^{n+1}}{n+1}=\frac{n}{n+1}\theta .$$ The bias is $-\theta/(n+1)$: the maximum of the sample can never exceed $\theta$, so it must underestimate. At $n=12$ the multiplier is $0.923077$ (J_unif_E_max).

(b) Yes. For any $\epsilon\in(0,\theta)$, $\mathbf{P}(|\hat\Theta_n-\theta|\ge\epsilon)=\mathbf{P}(\hat\Theta_n\le\theta-\epsilon)=\big(1-\epsilon/\theta\big)^n\to 0$ geometrically, so $\hat\Theta_n\to\theta$ in probability. (Note it converges much faster than the $1/\sqrt n$ typical of sample means.)

(c) Scale: $\frac{n+1}{n}\max_i X_i$ has expectation $\frac{n+1}{n}\cdot\frac{n}{n+1}\theta=\theta$ for every $n$ and every $\theta$, hence exactly unbiased.

Practice 1.2. Someone proposes to estimate the unknown mean $\theta$ of an i.i.d. sample by $\hat\Theta_n=\frac{X_1+X_2}{2}$, ignoring the rest of the data. Is it unbiased? Consistent? What if instead $\hat\Theta_n=\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n+5}$?
Solution

First estimator. $\mathbb{E}[(X_1+X_2)/2]=\tfrac12(\theta+\theta)=\theta$, so unbiased for every $\theta$. But $\operatorname{var}=\tfrac14(\sigma^2+\sigma^2)=\sigma^2/2$ regardless of $n$, so the sampling distribution never concentrates: not consistent. Unbiasedness alone is no protection against ignoring data.

Second estimator. $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]=\frac{n\theta}{n+5}$, so the bias is $\frac{n\theta}{n+5}-\theta=-\frac{5\theta}{n+5}\ne 0$: biased, but asymptotically unbiased since $5\theta/(n+5)\to 0$. Consistency: write $\hat\Theta_n=\frac{n}{n+5}M_n$; the deterministic factor $n/(n+5)\to 1$ and $M_n\to\theta$ in probability by the weak law, so the product converges in probability to $1\cdot\theta=\theta$. Consistent.

1.2 Mean squared error: $\mathrm{MSE}=\mathrm{bias}^2+\mathrm{variance}$

Bias alone is a poor score card — it ignores spread — and variance alone ignores where the spread is centered. The mean squared error combines them, and splits exactly.

Theorem (MSE decomposition; L23 slide 4, B&T §9.1). For any estimator $\hat\Theta$ of $\theta$ with finite second moment, $$\mathrm{MSE}\;=\;\mathbb{E}_\theta\big[(\hat\Theta-\theta)^2\big]\;=\;\operatorname{var}_\theta(\hat\Theta)\;+\;\big(b_\theta(\hat\Theta)\big)^2 .$$ Derivation, every step. Abbreviate the estimation error by $\tilde\Theta=\hat\Theta-\theta$; it is a random variable because $\hat\Theta$ is, while $\theta$ is a constant.
  1. Apply the second-moment identity. For any random variable $Y$ with finite variance, $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[Y^2]-(\mathbb{E}[Y])^2$, i.e. $\mathbb{E}[Y^2]=\operatorname{var}(Y)+(\mathbb{E}[Y])^2$ (G2 §3). With $Y=\tilde\Theta$: $$\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat\Theta-\theta)^2\big]=\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta-\theta)+\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta-\theta]\big)^2 .$$ This is precisely the first line printed on L23 slide 4.
  2. Shifting by a constant does not change a variance. Since $\theta$ is a fixed number (this is where "classical" enters — in the Bayesian setting of G6 §3, $\Theta$ was random and this step would be false), $$\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta-\theta)=\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta).$$
  3. Recognize the second term as the bias. By linearity, $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta-\theta]=\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta]-\theta=b_\theta(\hat\Theta)$.
  4. Combine. $\mathbb{E}[(\hat\Theta-\theta)^2]=\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta)+b_\theta(\hat\Theta)^2$. $\blacksquare$
Both terms are functions of $\theta$ in general, and both are nonnegative — so an unbiased estimator has $\mathrm{MSE}=\operatorname{var}$, and a zero-variance estimator has $\mathrm{MSE}=\mathrm{bias}^2$.
Interpretation — the bias–variance trade-off. Nothing says the best estimator is unbiased. Bias buys variance: shrinking an estimator toward a fixed point (say $0$) reduces its spread while pulling its center off target. Because MSE weighs $\mathrm{bias}^2$ against $\operatorname{var}$, a little bias can be a bargain when it kills a lot of variance — and, since a bias term is proportional to $\theta$ while a variance term is proportional to $1/n$, the bargain is good for small $|\theta|$ and small $n$, and bad otherwise. Example 1.2 makes this exact, and Figure 1.1 draws it.
Example 1.2 (a biased estimator that can beat the sample mean; B&T Example 9.5). Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be i.i.d. with unknown mean $\theta$ and known variance $v$ (B&T writes $v$ for what L23 calls $\sigma^2$). Compare $$M_n=\frac{S_n}{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad T_n=\frac{S_n}{n+1},\qquad S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n .$$ ($T_n$ is not arbitrary: it is the Bayesian LMS/MAP estimator of G6 §4 for a normal prior with mean $0$ and variance $v$, reinterpreted here as a classical estimator.) Take $n=10$ and $v=1$.

Step 1 — the two means. $\mathbb{E}[S_n]=n\theta$ by linearity, so $$\mathbb{E}[M_n]=\frac{n\theta}{n}=\theta \quad(\text{bias }0),\qquad \mathbb{E}[T_n]=\frac{n\theta}{n+1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad b_\theta(T_n)=\frac{n\theta}{n+1}-\theta=-\frac{\theta}{n+1}.$$ At $n=10$ that bias is $-\theta/11$: $-0.045455$, $-0.090909$, $-0.181818$, $-0.272727$ at $\theta=0.5,1,2,3$ (B_bias_th0.5, B_bias_th1.0, B_bias_th2.0, B_bias_th3.0). $T_n$ is asymptotically unbiased, since $\theta/(n+1)\to0$.

Step 2 — the two variances. Independence makes variances add (G2 §3), so $\operatorname{var}(S_n)=nv$, and scaling a random variable by $a$ scales its variance by $a^2$: $$\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\frac{nv}{n^2}=\frac{v}{n}=\frac{1}{10}=0.1,\qquad \operatorname{var}(T_n)=\frac{nv}{(n+1)^2}=\frac{10}{121}=0.082645 .$$ That second value is key B_var_T, and the ratio is $n^2/(n+1)^2=0.826446$ (B_var_ratio): $T_n$ is genuinely less variable, by about 17%.

Step 3 — the two MSEs. By the decomposition, $$\mathrm{MSE}(M_n)=\operatorname{var}(M_n)+0=\frac{v}{n}=0.1\quad\text{(no }\theta\text{ dependence)},$$ $$\mathrm{MSE}(T_n)=\frac{nv}{(n+1)^2}+\frac{\theta^2}{(n+1)^2}=\frac{nv+\theta^2}{(n+1)^2}=\frac{10+\theta^2}{121}.$$ Evaluating (B_mse_T_th*): $0.082645$ at $\theta=0$, $0.084711$ at $\theta=0.5$, $0.090909$ at $\theta=1$, $0.115702$ at $\theta=2$, $0.157025$ at $\theta=3$.

Step 4 — where the crossover sits. Solve $\mathrm{MSE}(T_n)<\mathrm{MSE}(M_n)$: $$\frac{nv+\theta^2}{(n+1)^2}<\frac{v}{n} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; n(nv+\theta^2)<v(n+1)^2 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; n^2v+n\theta^2<n^2v+2nv+v \;\Longleftrightarrow\; n\theta^2<v(2n+1),$$ where the first step multiplies both sides by the positive number $n(n+1)^2$. Hence $$|\theta|<\sqrt{\frac{v(2n+1)}{n}}\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{21}{10}}\;=\;1.449138 .$$ That threshold is key B_crossover. For $|\theta|$ below $1.449$ the shrunk estimator wins on MSE; above it, the sample mean wins. And since you do not know $\theta$, you cannot know which regime you are in — which is why unbiasedness, a guarantee that holds for all $\theta$, remains the safer default.

Practice 1.3. With $X_1,\dots,X_n$ i.i.d. of mean $\theta$ and variance $v$, consider $\hat\Theta_n=cM_n$. (a) Write $\mathrm{MSE}(c)$ and minimize it over $c$. (b) Evaluate at $\theta=1$, $v=1$, $n=10$. (c) Why can you not use the answer?
Solution

(a) $\mathbb{E}[cM_n]=c\theta$ so the bias is $(c-1)\theta$; $\operatorname{var}(cM_n)=c^2v/n$. Hence $$\mathrm{MSE}(c)=c^2\frac{v}{n}+(c-1)^2\theta^2 .$$ This is an upward parabola in $c$; differentiate and set to zero: $$\frac{d}{dc}\mathrm{MSE}=2c\frac{v}{n}+2(c-1)\theta^2=0\;\Longrightarrow\;c\left(\frac{v}{n}+\theta^2\right)=\theta^2\;\Longrightarrow\;c^*=\frac{\theta^2}{\theta^2+v/n}.$$ The second derivative $2(v/n+\theta^2)>0$ confirms a minimum. Note $0<c^*<1$ always: the optimal estimator in this family always shrinks.

(b) $c^*=\dfrac{1}{1+1/10}=\dfrac{10}{11}=0.909091$ (B_cstar), giving $\mathrm{MSE}(c^*)=0.090909$ (B_mse_cstar) versus $\mathrm{MSE}(1)=v/n=0.1$ (B_mse_c1) — a 9.1% improvement. (Here $c^*$ coincides with $n/(n+1)$, which is why $T_n$ of Example 1.2 is optimal exactly when $\theta^2=v$.)

(c) $c^*$ depends on the unknown $\theta$. An "estimator" that requires knowing $\theta$ is not an estimator. This is the recurring classical trap: quantities that are optimal at a $\theta$ are unusable without a prior — and supplying a prior is precisely the Bayesian move of G6 §3–§4.

Practice 1.4. Continuing Practice 1.1 ($X_i$ i.i.d. uniform on $[0,\theta]$, $n=12$), compare the MSE of the moment estimator $\hat\Theta_n=2M_n$ with that of $\max_i X_i$.
Solution

Moment estimator. $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=\theta/2$, so $\mathbb{E}[2M_n]=\theta$: unbiased. $\operatorname{var}(X_i)=\theta^2/12=0.083333\theta^2$ (J_unif_var_X at $\theta=1$), so $$\mathrm{MSE}(2M_n)=\operatorname{var}(2M_n)=4\cdot\frac{\theta^2/12}{n}=\frac{\theta^2}{3n}=\frac{\theta^2}{36}=0.027778\,\theta^2$$ (coefficient J_unif_mse_2M). Maximum. Using the density $nt^{n-1}/\theta^n$ from Practice 1.1, $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n^2]=\frac{n}{n+2}\theta^2$, so $$\operatorname{var}=\frac{n}{n+2}\theta^2-\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^2\theta^2=\frac{n\theta^2}{(n+2)(n+1)^2},\qquad \mathrm{bias}^2=\frac{\theta^2}{(n+1)^2},$$ $$\mathrm{MSE}(\max)=\frac{n+ (n+2)}{(n+2)(n+1)^2}\theta^2=\frac{2\theta^2}{(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac{2\theta^2}{13\cdot 14}=0.010989\,\theta^2$$ (coefficient J_unif_mse_max). The biased maximum beats the unbiased moment estimator by a factor $0.027778/0.010989=2.53$ (J_unif_ratio) — and the gap widens with $n$, since $\mathrm{MSE}(\max)$ decays like $1/n^2$ while $\mathrm{MSE}(2M_n)$ decays like $1/n$. Unbiasedness is not optimality.

1.3 The sample mean as an estimator

L23 slide 5 specializes everything to the most important case: estimating a mean.

Property (the sample mean; L23 slide 5, B&T §9.1). Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be i.i.d. with unknown mean $\theta$ and variance $\sigma^2$. The slide writes the observation model as $$X_i=\theta+W_i,\qquad W_i\ \text{i.i.d., mean }0,\ \text{variance }\sigma^2,$$ i.e. "signal plus noise": $\theta$ is the deterministic quantity being measured and $W_i=X_i-\theta$ is the measurement error. The sample mean estimator is $$\hat\Theta_n\;=\;M_n\;=\;\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n},$$ and it satisfies $$\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]=\theta\ \ (\text{unbiased}),\qquad \operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n},\qquad \mathrm{MSE}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n},\qquad \hat\Theta_n\to\theta\ \ (\text{consistent}).$$ Derivations (the slide asserts three of these — unbiasedness, WLLN consistency, and $\mathrm{MSE}=\sigma^2/n$ — with no computation, and never states the variance at all, even though the MSE claim is exactly that variance).
  1. Unbiased. $\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_n]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n}\right]=\frac1n\big(\mathbb{E}[X_1]+\cdots+\mathbb{E}[X_n]\big)=\frac{n\theta}{n}=\theta,$ using linearity of expectation (no independence needed) and then that all $n$ terms have the same mean $\theta$. This holds for every $n$ and every $\theta$, so $M_n$ is unbiased in the strong "for all $\theta$" sense.
  2. Variance. Independence makes variances of a sum add (G2 §3), and $\operatorname{var}(aY)=a^2\operatorname{var}(Y)$: $$\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\operatorname{var}\!\left(\frac{S_n}{n}\right)=\frac{1}{n^2}\operatorname{var}(S_n)=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{var}(X_i)=\frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.$$ Equivalently in the slide's noise notation, $M_n=\theta+\frac1n\sum_i W_i$, whose randomness is entirely the averaged noise.
  3. MSE. By the decomposition of §1.2 with zero bias, $\mathrm{MSE}=\operatorname{var}(M_n)+0^2=\sigma^2/n$. Notice it does not depend on $\theta$ — a rare and welcome feature.
  4. Consistency. Directly by the weak law of large numbers (G6 §1), $M_n\to\theta$ in probability. If you prefer the explicit rate, Chebyshev gives it: for any $\epsilon>0$, $$\mathbf{P}\big(|M_n-\theta|\ge\epsilon\big)\;\le\;\frac{\operatorname{var}(M_n)}{\epsilon^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\;0 .$$
Interpretation — the $\sqrt{n}$ law and what it costs. The standard error of the sample mean is $\sigma/\sqrt n$, not $\sigma/n$. With $\sigma=1$ it equals $1,\ 0.5,\ 0.2,\ 0.1,\ 0.05,\ 0.025$ at $n=1,4,25,100,400,1600$ (L_se_n1 … L_se_n1600). Reading that list the other way: quadrupling the data halves the error. Every accuracy statement in this section — the width of a confidence interval, the number of voters to poll — inherits this square-root law, and it is why polls of $1000$ people and polls of $4000$ people differ by only a factor of two in precision.
Example 1.3 (L23 slide 5's [UNSOLVED EXAMPLE]: for normal data the sample mean is the ML estimate). The slide states "Sample mean often turns out to also be the ML estimate. E.g., if $X_i\sim N(\theta,\sigma^2)$, i.i.d." and stops. Carry out the maximization. (Maximum likelihood as a method is developed in §2; this is the one place L23 needs it.)

Setup. With $\sigma^2$ known and $\theta$ the unknown mean, the joint density of the data, viewed as a function of $\theta$ (the likelihood), is by independence a product of $n$ normal densities: $$f_X(x;\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left\{-\frac{(x_i-\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\} =\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{n/2}}\exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\theta)^2\right\},$$ where the second form collects the $n$ identical prefactors and adds the exponents.

Step 1 — take logarithms. $\log$ is strictly increasing, so maximizing $f_X$ over $\theta$ is the same as maximizing $$\ell(\theta)=\log f_X(x;\theta)=-\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\theta)^2 .$$ The first term does not involve $\theta$, so maximizing $\ell$ is the same as minimizing $\sum_i(x_i-\theta)^2$ — the ML estimate for normal data is a least-squares estimate.

Step 2 — differentiate. $$\frac{d\ell}{d\theta}=-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\cdot\frac{d}{d\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\theta)^2 =-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}2(x_i-\theta)(-1)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\theta).$$ Set it to zero: $\sum_i x_i-n\theta=0$, hence $$\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=\frac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n}=m_n,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \hat\Theta_{\text{ML}}=M_n .$$

Step 3 — check it is a maximum. $\dfrac{d^2\ell}{d\theta^2}=-\dfrac{n}{\sigma^2}<0$ for every $\theta$, so $\ell$ is strictly concave and the stationary point is the unique global maximum.

A derivative-free proof. Complete the square with $m_n=\frac1n\sum_i x_i$: $$\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\theta)^2=\sum_{i=1}^n\big[(x_i-m_n)+(m_n-\theta)\big]^2=\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-m_n)^2+2(m_n-\theta)\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-m_n)}_{=\,0}+n(m_n-\theta)^2 .$$ The cross term vanishes because $\sum_i(x_i-m_n)=\sum_i x_i-n m_n=0$ by the definition of $m_n$. What is left is $\sum_i(x_i-m_n)^2+n(m_n-\theta)^2$, whose only $\theta$-dependence is the nonnegative term $n(m_n-\theta)^2$, minimized at $\theta=m_n$. Same answer, no calculus.

Numerical confirmation. For the data $2.1,3.4,1.8,2.9,3.3$ the sample mean is $2.7$; a grid search over $400\,001$ candidate values of $\theta$ maximizes the log-likelihood at $2.7$ (K_samplemean, K_grid_argmax, K_matches).

Why it matters here. The sample mean is thus simultaneously the natural moment estimator, the ML estimator under normality, and (from G6 §4) the limit of the Bayesian LMS estimator as the prior becomes uninformative. Three different philosophies converge on $M_n$ — which is why §1.4 can build a confidence interval around it and expect it to be near-optimal.

1.4 Confidence intervals: definition and construction

A single number $\hat\theta_n=0.57$ is "not informative enough" (L23 slide 6): it says nothing about how far off it might be. A confidence interval attaches a guaranteed-probability range.

Definition ($1-\alpha$ confidence interval; L23 slide 6, B&T §9.1). Fix a confidence level $1-\alpha$, where $\alpha\in(0,1)$ is small (L23 lists $\alpha=0.05$, $0.25$, $0.01$; the middle value $0.25$ is an unusual confidence level — $0.1$ or $0.025$ would be more usual — but $0.25$ is what the slide prints). A $1-\alpha$ confidence interval is a pair of estimators $\hat\Theta_n^-\le\hat\Theta_n^+$ — both functions of the data $X_1,\dots,X_n$, hence both random variables — such that $$\mathbf{P}_\theta\big(\hat\Theta_n^-\le\theta\le\hat\Theta_n^+\big)\;\ge\;1-\alpha\qquad\text{for every possible value of }\theta .$$ The probability is over the randomness in the data, with $\theta$ held fixed. Among intervals meeting the requirement, narrower is better.

Now build one for a mean. Everything rests on the central limit theorem of G6 §2.

Construction (CI for a mean with known $\sigma$; L23 slide 6, B&T Example 9.6). Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be i.i.d. with unknown mean $\theta$ and known variance $\sigma^2$, and let $\hat\Theta_n=M_n$. Then for large $n$, $$\left[\hat\Theta_n-\frac{z\sigma}{\sqrt n},\ \hat\Theta_n+\frac{z\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right]\quad\text{is an approximate }1-\alpha\text{ CI, where }z\text{ solves }\Phi(z)=1-\frac{\alpha}{2}.$$ Derivation, step by step (the slide names the CLT and leaves the algebra to the reader).
  1. Standardize the estimator. From §1.3, $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]=\theta$ and $\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_n)=\sigma^2/n$, so the standardized error $$Z_n=\frac{\hat\Theta_n-\theta}{\sigma/\sqrt n}=\frac{M_n-\theta}{\sigma/\sqrt n}=\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n-n\theta}{\sigma\sqrt n}$$ has mean $0$ and variance $1$ for every $n$. (The last form is exactly the CLT normalization of G6 §2.)
  2. Invoke the CLT. The $X_i$ are i.i.d. with finite variance, so the CDF of $Z_n$ converges to the standard normal CDF $\Phi$. Hence, for large $n$ and any $z>0$, $$\mathbf{P}\big(|Z_n|\le z\big)\;\approx\;\mathbf{P}\big(|Z|\le z\big),\qquad Z\sim N(0,1).$$
  3. Compute the normal two-sided probability. By symmetry of the standard normal density about $0$, $$\mathbf{P}(|Z|\le z)=\Phi(z)-\Phi(-z)=\Phi(z)-\big(1-\Phi(z)\big)=2\Phi(z)-1 .$$
  4. Choose $z$ so that this equals $1-\alpha$. Set $2\Phi(z)-1=1-\alpha$ and solve: $\Phi(z)=1-\alpha/2$. This is the "invert $\Phi$" step, done with normal tables. For $\alpha=0.05$: $\Phi(z)=1-0.025=0.975$, and the tables give $z=1.96$. Checking, $\Phi(1.96)=0.975002$ and $2\Phi(1.96)-1=0.9500042$ (C_Phi_196, C_2Phi196_m1); the exact $97.5$th percentile is $1.959964$ (C_z_exact_005), so the familiar $1.96$ is a rounded table value that overshoots the nominal level by $4.2\times10^{-6}$ — utterly negligible next to the CLT approximation itself.
  5. Unfold the absolute value. $|Z_n|\le z$ means $$-z\le\frac{\hat\Theta_n-\theta}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\le z \;\Longleftrightarrow\; -\frac{z\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le \hat\Theta_n-\theta\le\frac{z\sigma}{\sqrt n},$$ multiplying through by the positive number $\sigma/\sqrt n$ (which preserves both inequalities).
  6. Solve for $\theta$. Subtract $\hat\Theta_n$ from all three parts: $$-\hat\Theta_n-\frac{z\sigma}{\sqrt n}\;\le\;-\theta\;\le\;-\hat\Theta_n+\frac{z\sigma}{\sqrt n},$$ then multiply by $-1$, which reverses both inequality signs and hence swaps the two ends: $$\hat\Theta_n-\frac{z\sigma}{\sqrt n}\;\le\;\theta\;\le\;\hat\Theta_n+\frac{z\sigma}{\sqrt n}.$$ This sign flip is the only place students routinely slip.
  7. Read off the conclusion. The three events $\{|Z_n|\le z\}$, $\{|\hat\Theta_n-\theta|\le z\sigma/\sqrt n\}$ and $\{\hat\Theta_n-z\sigma/\sqrt n\le\theta\le\hat\Theta_n+z\sigma/\sqrt n\}$ are the same event, so they have the same probability, namely $\approx 1-\alpha$. Identifying $\hat\Theta_n^\pm=\hat\Theta_n\pm z\sigma/\sqrt n$ completes the construction. $\blacksquare$
For $\alpha=0.05$ this is L23 slide 6's pair of displayed lines: $$\mathbf{P}\!\left(\frac{|\hat\Theta_n-\theta|}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\le 1.96\right)\approx 0.95 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{P}\!\left(\hat\Theta_n-\frac{1.96\,\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le\theta\le\hat\Theta_n+\frac{1.96\,\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right)\approx 0.95 .$$

1.5 What a confidence interval means — and what it does not

L23 slide 6 says only "interpretation is subtle". It is worth four paragraphs.

Gotcha — the classic misreading. After the data arrive, the 95% interval is a concrete pair of numbers, say $[-2.3,\ 4.1]$ (the illustration in B&T §9.1). It is wrong to say

"$\theta$ lies in $[-2.3,\,4.1]$ with probability $0.95$."

That sentence contains no random variable at all: $\theta$ is a fixed unknown constant and $-2.3$, $4.1$ are fixed known constants. The statement "$-2.3\le\theta\le 4.1$" is either true or false; its probability is $1$ or $0$, and we simply do not know which. What is random — before the data are seen — is the interval: $$\mathbf{P}_\theta\big(\underbrace{\hat\Theta_n^-}_{\text{random}}\le\underbrace{\theta}_{\text{fixed}}\le\underbrace{\hat\Theta_n^+}_{\text{random}}\big)\approx 0.95 .$$ The $0.95$ describes the procedure, not this particular interval. Saying "$\theta$ has a 95% chance of being in here" is a Bayesian sentence (G6 §3), and it requires a prior; without one it is not merely imprecise, it is undefined.
Interpretation — the frequency reading, made concrete. Fix $\theta$. Repeat the whole experiment many times: each replication draws its own $n$ observations and builds its own interval by the same recipe. Then about 95% of those intervals contain $\theta$ — whatever $\theta$ is. Figure 1.3 does exactly this for $\mu=10$, $\sigma=2$, $n=25$: the half-width is $1.96\cdot 2/\sqrt{25}=0.784$ (H_halfwidth), the intervals wander because $M_n$ wanders, the vertical line at $\theta=10$ never moves, and $96$ of the $100$ intervals cross it while $4$ miss (H_hits, H_misses). Over $200\,000$ replications the empirical coverage is $0.94983$ (H_longrun) against the exact $2\Phi(1.96)-1=0.950004$ (G_normal_exact). Out of $100$ intervals the expected number of misses is $5$ with standard deviation $\sqrt{100\cdot0.05\cdot0.95}=2.18$, so anything between about $1$ and $9$ misses is unremarkable — $4$ is not evidence of anything.

For Bernoulli data with the plug-in variance at $\theta=0.57$, the true coverage of the nominal 95% interval is $0.8285$ at $n=5$, $0.9305$ at $n=30$, $0.9458$ at $n=100$ and $0.9488$ at $n=1000$ (G_cov_plug_n5, G_cov_plug_n30, G_cov_plug_n100, G_cov_plug_n1000) — approaching but never quite reaching the nominal level, and the approach is not even monotone, because the underlying binomial is a lattice distribution. The pathological case: $\theta=0.03$, $n=20$ gives coverage $0.4565$ (G_cov_plug_n20_th003), because with probability $0.97^{20}=0.5438$ (G_p_allzero_n20_th003) the sample contains no successes at all, $\hat\theta_n=0$, the estimated variance $\hat\theta_n(1-\hat\theta_n)$ is $0$, and the "interval" $[0,0]$ has zero width and certainly misses. Two lessons: a plug-in variance can fail catastrophically when the estimate sits at the edge of the parameter range, and the conservative $1/4$ bound of §1.6 (coverage $0.9854$ at $n=5$, G_cov_bound_n5) is the safe choice at small $n$.

1.6 When $\sigma$ is unknown: three ways out

The construction of §1.4 needs $\sigma$, which in a real problem you do not have. L23 slide 7 lists three fixes, in increasing order of generality.

Option 1 — use an upper bound on $\sigma$ (L23 slide 7). If $X_i$ is Bernoulli($\theta$) then $\sigma^2=\theta(1-\theta)\le 1/4$, so $\sigma\le 1/2$. Proof. A Bernoulli variable has $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=\theta$ and $\mathbb{E}[X_i^2]=1^2\cdot\theta+0^2\cdot(1-\theta)=\theta$, so $\sigma^2=\theta-\theta^2=\theta(1-\theta)$ (G2 §3). Maximize $g(\theta)=\theta-\theta^2$ on $[0,1]$: $g'(\theta)=1-2\theta=0$ at $\theta=1/2$, and $g''=-2<0$, so this is the maximum, with value $g(1/2)=1/4$. Hence $\sigma^2\le 1/4$ and $\sigma\le 1/2$ for every $\theta$ (E_bern_max_var, E_bern_max_sd). Substituting the bound can only widen the interval, so the coverage guarantee is preserved — the resulting interval is conservative: $$\left[\hat\Theta_n-\frac{z}{2\sqrt n},\ \hat\Theta_n+\frac{z}{2\sqrt n}\right].$$
Option 2 — an ad hoc plug-in estimate (L23 slide 7). For Bernoulli data, substitute the estimate into the variance formula: $\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\hat\Theta_n(1-\hat\Theta_n)}$. This is justified by consistency: $\hat\Theta_n\to\theta$ in probability, and $t\mapsto t(1-t)$ is continuous, so $\hat\Theta_n(1-\hat\Theta_n)\to\theta(1-\theta)=\sigma^2$ in probability. Typical values of $\theta(1-\theta)$: $0.09$ at $\theta=0.1$, $0.21$ at $0.3$, $0.25$ at $0.5$, $0.2451$ at $0.57$ (E_bern_var_0.1, E_bern_var_0.3, E_bern_var_0.5, E_bern_var_0.57). Note how flat the function is near its peak — that flatness is why Options 1 and 2 give nearly identical intervals whenever $\hat\theta_n$ is anywhere near $1/2$.
Option 3 — a generic variance estimator (L23 slide 7, B&T §9.1). Start from the definition $\sigma^2=\mathbb{E}[(X_i-\theta)^2]$. Three candidates, of which only the last two can actually be computed from data: $$\hat\sigma_n^2=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\theta)^2 \qquad\text{(needs }\theta\text{, which we do not know)},$$ $$\bar S_n^2=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\hat\Theta_n)^2,\qquad \hat S_n^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-\hat\Theta_n)^2 .$$ The last of these, with divisor $n-1$, is what is universally called the sample variance; it is the only one of the three you can actually compute and that is unbiased. All three converge to $\sigma^2$ in probability. As for bias: $\hat\sigma_n^2$ is unbiased too — it uses the true $\theta$, so $\mathbb{E}[\hat\sigma_n^2]=\frac1n\sum_i\mathbb{E}[(X_i-\theta)^2]=\sigma^2$ exactly — but it is not available in practice. Of the two computable estimators, $\bar S_n^2$ is biased and $\hat S_n^2$ is unbiased; the next two derivations show why.

Why $\hat\sigma_n^2\to\sigma^2$. The terms $Y_i=(X_i-\theta)^2$ are i.i.d. (functions of i.i.d. variables) with common mean $\mathbb{E}[Y_i]=\mathbb{E}[(X_i-\theta)^2]=\sigma^2$. So $\hat\sigma_n^2$ is just the sample mean of the $Y_i$, and the weak law of large numbers (G6 §1) gives $\hat\sigma_n^2\to\sigma^2$ in probability. (This step needs $\mathbb{E}[Y_i^2]=\mathbb{E}[(X_i-\theta)^4]$ finite if you want the Chebyshev proof of the weak law; the law itself holds under the finite-mean assumption alone.)

Why the denominator changes from $n$ to $n-1$ — the derivation L23 slide 7 omits. Replacing the unknown $\theta$ by $\hat\Theta_n=M_n$ is not free: $M_n$ is, by construction, the point that minimizes $\sum_i(x_i-t)^2$ over $t$ (the completing-the-square identity of Example 1.3), so $\sum_i(X_i-M_n)^2$ is systematically smaller than $\sum_i(X_i-\theta)^2$. Quantify it (following B&T §9.1):

  1. Expand the sum of squares. Using $\sum_i X_i=nM_n$, $$\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i-M_n)^2=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2-2M_n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i+nM_n^2=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2-2nM_n^2+nM_n^2=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2-nM_n^2 .$$ Hence $\displaystyle\bar S_n^2=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2-M_n^2$.
  2. Two second moments. For each observation, $\mathbb{E}[X_i^2]=\operatorname{var}(X_i)+(\mathbb{E}[X_i])^2=\sigma^2+\theta^2$. For the sample mean, using $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n$ and $\mathbb{E}[M_n]=\theta$ from §1.3, $$\mathbb{E}[M_n^2]=\operatorname{var}(M_n)+(\mathbb{E}[M_n])^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}+\theta^2 .$$
  3. Take the expectation. By linearity, $$\mathbb{E}\big[\bar S_n^2\big]=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb{E}[X_i^2]-\mathbb{E}[M_n^2] =(\sigma^2+\theta^2)-\left(\frac{\sigma^2}{n}+\theta^2\right)=\sigma^2-\frac{\sigma^2}{n}=\frac{n-1}{n}\,\sigma^2 .$$ So $\bar S_n^2$ understates the variance by the factor $(n-1)/n$: $0.5$ at $n=2$, $0.8$ at $n=5$, $0.9$ at $n=10$, $0.99$ at $n=100$ (F_biasfac_n2 … F_biasfac_n100) — a 50%, 20%, 10%, 1% understatement respectively. It is biased but asymptotically unbiased.
  4. Rescale. Multiplying by $n/(n-1)$ removes the factor exactly: $$\hat S_n^2=\frac{n}{n-1}\bar S_n^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\hat\Theta_n)^2,\qquad \mathbb{E}\big[\hat S_n^2\big]=\frac{n}{n-1}\cdot\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2=\sigma^2 ,$$ for every $n\ge 2$ and every $\theta$ — this is the unbiasedness claim on L23 slide 7. Simulation with $\sigma^2=1$, $n=5$, $400\,000$ replications: $\mathbb{E}[\bar S_n^2]=0.80059$ against the predicted $0.8$, and $\mathbb{E}[\hat S_n^2]=1.00074$ against $1$ (F_sim_div_n, F_sim_div_nm1). Since $n/(n-1)\to 1$, the two estimators are practically identical for large $n$.
Interpretation — "one degree of freedom is spent on the mean." The $n$ deviations $X_i-M_n$ are not free: they always sum to zero, so only $n-1$ of them can be chosen independently. Dividing by $n-1$ rather than $n$ averages over the number of independent deviations, not the number of observations. Notice also that $\hat\sigma_n^2$, which uses the true $\theta$ and therefore spends no degree of freedom, is unbiased with divisor $n$ — the correction is entirely the price of estimating the mean from the same data.

Beyond the syllabus — the $t$-distribution. Even for exactly normal data, replacing $\sigma$ by $\hat S_n$ makes $T_n=\sqrt n(\hat\Theta_n-\theta)/\hat S_n$ non-normal; its exact law is the $t$-distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom (B&T §9.1, its Figure 9.3), which is bell-shaped but heavier-tailed. The L23 reading list explicitly excuses you from $t$-based intervals (B&T pp. 471–473), and for $n\ge 50$ the difference is negligible. For scale: in B&T Example 9.7 ($n=8$ weighings, $\hat\theta_n=0.574675$, $\hat S_n^2/n=0.00032952$, standard error $0.018153$) the $t$ multiplier is $2.364624$ rather than $1.96$, giving $[0.5318,\,0.6176]$ instead of $[0.5391,\,0.6103]$ — an interval $1.2064$ times wider (I_t7, I_width_ratio). Using $z$ at $n=8$ would quietly under-cover; using it at $n=1200$, as in §1.7, is harmless.
Recipe — sample-size design with the conservative bound. For Bernoulli data you can choose $n$ before collecting anything, because $\sigma^2\le 1/4$ needs no data. Requiring a 95% margin of error at most $w$: $$1.96\sqrt{\frac{1/4}{n}}\le w\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\frac{1.96}{2\sqrt n}\le w\;\Longleftrightarrow\;n\ge\left(\frac{1.96}{2w}\right)^{\!2}=\frac{0.9604}{w^2}.$$ Rounding up (E_n_margin*): $n=385$ for $w=5\%$, $n=1068$ for $3\%$, $n=9604$ for $1\%$, $n=38416$ for $0.5\%$. Halving the margin of error quadruples the sample (E_quadruple) — the $\sqrt n$ law once more. A handy mnemonic: at 95% confidence the conservative margin is $0.98/\sqrt n\approx 1/\sqrt n$, which at $n=1200$ reads $0.02829$ against the mnemonic's $0.028868$ (E_hw_c_1200, E_rule_1oversqrtn_1200).
Practice 1.8. A coin is tossed $100$ times and lands heads $40$ times. Give 95% confidence intervals for the probability of heads using (a) the plug-in variance and (b) the conservative bound. Does either exclude a fair coin?
Solution

$\hat\theta_n=40/100=0.4$ (J5_thetahat).

(a) $\hat\theta_n(1-\hat\theta_n)=0.4\cdot 0.6=0.24$ (J5_plug_v), so the standard error is $\sqrt{0.24/100}=0.04899$ and the margin is $1.96\cdot 0.04899=0.09602$ (J5_hw_plug). Interval $[0.30398,\ 0.49602]$.

(b) With $\sigma^2\le 1/4$ the margin is $1.96\cdot\sqrt{0.25/100}=1.96\cdot0.05=0.098$ (J5_hw_bound). Interval $[0.302,\ 0.498]$.

Both intervals lie entirely below $0.5$ (J5_excludes_half_plug, J5_excludes_half_bound), so at the 95% level both are inconsistent with a fair coin — though only barely: the upper endpoints $0.496$ and $0.498$ are a whisker from $0.5$. The formal way to make this decision is the hypothesis test of §4.

Practice 1.9. Explain why $\hat S_n^2$ is undefined at $n=1$, and why that is exactly right.
Solution

At $n=1$ the denominator $n-1$ is zero. Correspondingly, $\sum_{i=1}^1(X_i-M_1)^2=(X_1-X_1)^2=0$: a single observation carries no information about spread at all, since the one deviation from the sample mean is forced to be $0$. The formula's refusal to return a number is the honest answer, whereas $\bar S_1^2=0$ would be a confident and completely wrong claim of zero variance. In the degrees-of-freedom reading: with $n=1$ observation and $1$ parameter estimated, there are $n-1=0$ independent deviations left.

Example 1.4 (rec23 P3 = B&T Example 9.8: a 95% CI for a voter proportion). We wish to estimate the fraction $\theta$ of voters supporting a candidate. We collect $n$ independent responses $X_1,\dots,X_n$, each Bernoulli with $X_i=1$ if voter $i$ supports the candidate, and use $\hat\Theta_n=(X_1+\cdots+X_n)/n$. A poll of $n=1200$ people in North Carolina found $684$ supporters. Using the CLT interval $$\hat\Theta^-=\hat\Theta_n-1.96\sqrt{\frac{v}{n}},\qquad \hat\Theta^+=\hat\Theta_n+1.96\sqrt{\frac{v}{n}},\qquad v=\operatorname{var}(X_i),$$ construct 95% confidence intervals for $\theta$ under three treatments of the unknown $v$: (a) the sample variance $\hat S_n^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i(X_i-\hat\Theta_n)^2$; (b) the plug-in $\hat\Theta_n(1-\hat\Theta_n)$; (c) the most conservative upper bound for the variance.
Solution

Step 0 — the point estimate. $$\hat\theta_n=\frac{684}{1200}=0.57 .$$ This (key D_thetahat) is the number every interval is centered on. Since the $X_i$ are Bernoulli, the sample mean is literally the fraction of supporters in the sample.

Step 1 — the sum of squared deviations, once and for all. The data are $0$s and $1$s: $684$ of them equal $1$ and $1200-684=516$ equal $0$. So $$\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\hat\theta_n)^2=684\,(1-0.57)^2+516\,(0-0.57)^2 =684\,(0.43)^2+516\,(0.57)^2 .$$ Evaluating: $(0.43)^2=0.1849$ and $684\times 0.1849=126.4716$; $(0.57)^2=0.3249$ and $516\times 0.3249=167.6484$. Adding, $$\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\hat\theta_n)^2=126.4716+167.6484=294.12$$ (key D_sumsq). Worth noting the general identity for $0/1$ data: with $k$ ones out of $n$ and $\hat\theta=k/n$, $$k(1-\hat\theta)^2+(n-k)\hat\theta^2=n\hat\theta(1-\hat\theta)^2+n(1-\hat\theta)\hat\theta^2=n\hat\theta(1-\hat\theta)\big[(1-\hat\theta)+\hat\theta\big]=n\hat\theta(1-\hat\theta),$$ which checks out: $1200\times 0.57\times 0.43=1200\times 0.2451=294.12$. This identity is what makes parts (a) and (b) agree so closely.

(a) The sample variance. $$\hat s_n^2=\frac{294.12}{1200-1}=\frac{294.12}{1199}=0.24530442$$ (key D_S2). Standard error: $$\sqrt{\frac{\hat s_n^2}{n}}=\sqrt{\frac{0.24530442}{1200}}=\sqrt{0.000204420}=0.01429756$$ (key D_se_a). Margin of error: $$1.96\times 0.01429756=0.028023$$ (key D_hw_a), so the interval is $$[0.57-0.028023,\ 0.57+0.028023]=[0.541977,\ 0.598023]\;\approx\;[0.542,\ 0.598].$$

(b) The plug-in Bernoulli variance. $$\hat\theta_n(1-\hat\theta_n)=0.57\times 0.43=0.2451$$ (key D_plug). By the identity in Step 1, this differs from $\hat s_n^2$ by exactly the factor $n/(n-1)$: $$\frac{\hat s_n^2}{\hat\theta_n(1-\hat\theta_n)}=\frac{0.24530442}{0.2451}=1.00083403=\frac{1200}{1199}$$ (keys D_S2_over_plug, D_n_over_nm1), a difference of $0.083\%$. Standard error $\sqrt{0.2451/1200}=\sqrt{0.00020425}=0.01429161$ (D_se_b), margin $1.96\times 0.01429161=0.028012$ (D_hw_b), interval $$[0.541988,\ 0.598012]\;\approx\;[0.542,\ 0.598],$$ identical to (a) to three decimals, as B&T notes.

(c) The conservative upper bound. The most conservative admissible value is the maximum of $\theta(1-\theta)$ over $\theta\in[0,1]$, which is $1/4$ at $\theta=1/2$ (Option 1 of §1.6). Standard error $\sqrt{0.25/1200}=\sqrt{0.000208333}=0.01443376$ (D_se_c), margin $1.96\times0.01443376=0.02829$ (D_hw_c), interval $$[0.57-0.02829,\ 0.57+0.02829]=[0.54171,\ 0.59829]\;\approx\;[0.542,\ 0.598].$$ The conservative interval is wider than (b) by $0.02829-0.028012=0.00027862$ (D_hw_c_minus_b) on each side — about $0.03$ percentage points, invisible in any report.

Interpretation — why $n$ and not the population size drives the margin. The margin of error $1.96\sqrt{v/n}$ mentions the sample size $n$ and nothing about how many voters there are in North Carolina. Sampling $1200$ people gives the same $\pm 2.8$ points whether the electorate is $10^6$ or $10^8$, because what governs the accuracy is the number of independent draws, not the fraction of the population sampled. That is a direct consequence of the i.i.d. model, in which each response is an independent Bernoulli draw with the same $\theta$.

Practice 1.10. A second poll, in a different state, samples $n=400$ voters and finds $\hat\theta_n=0.90$. (a) Compute the 95% margin of error by the plug-in and by the conservative bound. (b) Why do they differ so much more than in Example 1.4? (c) Which should you report?
Solution

(a) Plug-in: $v=0.9\times 0.1=0.09$ (J7_plug_v), so the margin is $1.96\sqrt{0.09/400}=1.96\times\frac{0.3}{20}=1.96\times 0.015=0.0294$ (J7_hw_plug). Conservative: $1.96\sqrt{0.25/400}=1.96\times\frac{0.5}{20}=1.96\times 0.025=0.049$ (J7_hw_bound). The conservative margin is $0.049/0.0294=1.667$ times larger (J7_ratio) — a difference of $2$ percentage points, which any reader would notice.

(b) Because $\theta(1-\theta)$ is far from its maximum: $0.09$ versus $0.25$, a factor of $2.777778$ in variance and $\sqrt{2.777778}=1.667$ in width (J7_var_ratio, J7_ratio). In Example 1.4, $\hat\theta_n=0.57$ gave $0.2451$ versus $0.25$, a factor of $1.02$ in variance and $1.01$ in width. The bound $1/4$ is tight only near $\theta=1/2$.

(c) The plug-in interval, provided $n$ is large enough for the CLT to be trustworthy at $\hat\theta_n=0.9$ — here $n\hat\theta_n(1-\hat\theta_n)=400\times0.09=36$ is comfortable (J7_npq), so yes. Use the conservative bound when $n$ is small, when $\hat\theta_n$ is close to $0$ or $1$ (recall the $\theta=0.03$, $n=20$ disaster of §1.5), or when you must fix $n$ before seeing any data.

§1 cheatsheet.
  • Estimator $\hat\Theta_n=g(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ is random; the estimate $\hat\theta_n$ is a number; $\theta$ is a fixed unknown constant. Every property must hold for all $\theta$.
  • Bias $b_\theta(\hat\Theta_n)=\mathbb{E}_\theta[\hat\Theta_n]-\theta$; unbiased if it is $0$ for all $\theta$; consistent if $\hat\Theta_n\to\theta$ in probability for all $\theta$. Neither implies the other.
  • MSE decomposition: $\mathbb{E}_\theta[(\hat\Theta_n-\theta)^2]=\operatorname{var}_\theta(\hat\Theta_n)+b_\theta(\hat\Theta_n)^2$. Bias can buy variance: $S_n/(n+1)$ beats $M_n$ whenever $|\theta|<\sqrt{v(2n+1)/n}$ ($=1.449$ at $n=10$, $v=1$).
  • Sample mean: $\mathbb{E}[M_n]=\theta$, $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n$, $\mathrm{MSE}=\sigma^2/n$, consistent by the weak law. For normal data it is also the ML estimate. Standard error $\sigma/\sqrt n$: quadruple the data to halve the error.
  • $1-\alpha$ CI: random endpoints with $\mathbf{P}_\theta(\hat\Theta_n^-\le\theta\le\hat\Theta_n^+)\ge 1-\alpha$ for all $\theta$. From the CLT: $\hat\Theta_n\pm z\sigma/\sqrt n$ with $\Phi(z)=1-\alpha/2$; $z=1.96$ at 95% ($1.644854$ at 90%, $2.575829$ at 99%).
  • Interpretation: the interval is random, $\theta$ is not. "95%" describes the procedure over repetitions, not this one interval.
  • Unknown $\sigma$, three options: (1) an upper bound — Bernoulli $\sigma^2=\theta(1-\theta)\le 1/4$, always safe; (2) plug in $\hat\Theta_n(1-\hat\Theta_n)$, tightest but fragile near $0$ and $1$; (3) the sample variance $\hat S_n^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i(X_i-\hat\Theta_n)^2$, unbiased for any distribution, because $\mathbb{E}[\frac1n\sum_i(X_i-M_n)^2]=\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$.
  • Poll of 1200 with 684 supporters: $\hat\theta_n=0.57$; all three variance choices give $[0.542,\ 0.598]$; margin $\approx 2.8$ points; design rule $n\ge 0.9604/w^2$ ($385$, $1068$, $9604$ for $w=5\%$, $3\%$, $1\%$).
