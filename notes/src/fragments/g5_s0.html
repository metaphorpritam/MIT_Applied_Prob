§0   Orientation: dependence, compressed into a state

Sources: L16 slides 1–3 · L17 slides 1–2, 5 · L18 slides 1–2 · Bertsekas–Tsitsiklis (cited throughout as B&T) Chapter 7 preamble, §7.1 · numbers from computes/g5_s0.py. A bare "§N.k" always means this note's own subsection; every reference to the textbook carries the "B&T" prefix.

Every process in note G4 was memoryless. In a Bernoulli process each slot is a fresh independent coin flip; in a Poisson process the number of arrivals in disjoint intervals are independent, and the time you have already waited tells you nothing about the time you still must wait. B&T open Chapter 7 by naming exactly this and then dropping it: "The Bernoulli and Poisson processes studied in the preceding chapter are memoryless, in the sense that the future does not depend on the past … In this chapter, we consider processes where the future depends on and can be predicted to some extent by what has happened in the past."

Dependence across time is where realistic models live — a queue that is long now will probably still be long a second from now, a machine that is broken now will probably still be broken tomorrow — but unrestricted dependence is unusable: to predict $X_{n+1}$ you would need the entire history $X_0,X_1,\ldots,X_n$, and the bookkeeping grows without bound. Markov chains are the smallest useful relaxation. They allow the future to depend on the past, but insist that the dependence be routed through one summary variable, the current state. B&T put it as the design principle of the chapter: "we emphasize models where the effect of the past on the future is summarized by a state, which changes over time according to given probabilities."

0.1 The Markov property, in words and in symbols

A discrete-time, finite-state Markov chain is built from three ingredients, and L16 slide 3 lists them in the order you should think of them.

Definition (Markov chain; L16 slide 3, B&T §7.1). Let $X_n$ denote the state after $n$ transitions, a random variable taking values in a finite state space $S=\{1,\ldots,m\}$, where $m$ is the number of states and $n=0,1,2,\ldots$ indexes discrete time. The initial state $X_0$ is either given or itself random. The transition probabilities are the numbers $$p_{ij}=\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i),\qquad i,j\in S,$$ read "from $i$, the chance the next state is $j$". The model is a Markov chain when these numbers satisfy the Markov property: for every time $n$, every pair of states $i,j$, and every possible history $i_0,\ldots,i_{n-1}$ of earlier states, $$\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i,\;X_{n-1}=i_{n-1},\ldots,X_0=i_0)\;=\;\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i)\;=\;p_{ij}.$$ Two things are being asserted at once. First, conditional independence of the future from the past given the present: adding history to the conditioning changes nothing. Second, time-homogeneity: the symbol $p_{ij}$ carries no $n$, so the same number applies at every time step (L17 slide 2 states this as "discrete state, discrete time, time-homogeneous"). Being probabilities of a partition of the possible next states, the $p_{ij}$ must satisfy $$p_{ij}\ge 0\quad\text{for all }i,j,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{ij}=1\quad\text{for each }i,$$ the second because from state $i$ the chain must go somewhere at the next step (total probability over the $m$ mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive events $\{X_{n+1}=j\}$, G1 §1). Note the sum is over the second index: rows sum to one, columns generally do not.

Collecting the $p_{ij}$ into an $m\times m$ array gives the transition probability matrix $P$, whose $(i,j)$ entry is $p_{ij}$. For the chain of Fig. 0.1 (right), $$P=\begin{bmatrix}p_{11}&p_{12}\\ p_{21}&p_{22}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.5&0.5\\ 0.2&0.8\end{bmatrix},$$ and each row does sum to $1$: $0.5+0.5=1$ and $0.2+0.8=1$. The matrix and the transition diagram — circles for states, an arrow $i\to j$ drawn whenever $p_{ij}>0$, labelled with $p_{ij}$ — are two renderings of the same object, and §1 onward will move freely between them. Diagrams are for seeing structure (which states can reach which); matrices are for computing.

Interpretation — what "state" really means. The Markov property is not a property of the physical system; it is a property of the system together with your choice of state variable. The state is whatever you must remember about the past in order to make the future's distribution definite. Choose too little and the property fails; choose enough and it holds, at the price of a larger state space. That is why L16 slide 3 lists "identify the possible states" first: modeling a Markov chain is choosing what to remember. In the checkout-counter example of L16 slide 2 (worked in §1) the choice is the number of customers in the queue — not the individual arrival times, not who is being served, just the count. Everything else about the past turns out to be irrelevant once that number is known.
Recipe — specifying a Markov model (L16 slide 3; B&T §7.1, "Specification of Markov Models").
  1. Identify the possible states. Ask: what is the least I must know at time $n$ to make $\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=\cdot)$ well defined? That answer is the state space $S=\{1,\ldots,m\}$.
  2. Identify the possible transitions — the pairs $(i,j)$ with $p_{ij}>0$. This is the arrow structure of the diagram, and it alone settles every classification question of §2.
  3. Identify the numerical values of those positive $p_{ij}$, checking that each row sums to $1$.
  4. Verify the Markov property. Argue that, given $X_n=i$, the mechanism generating $X_{n+1}$ uses nothing else from the past. If it does use something else, enlarge the state to include it and return to step 1.
Gotcha — four ways to misread the definition.
  • Markov is not independence. $X_{n+1}$ and $X_n$ are in general strongly dependent; what is asserted is that $X_{n+1}$ is conditionally independent of $X_0,\ldots,X_{n-1}$ given $X_n$. Unconditionally, $X_{n+1}$ and $X_0$ are usually dependent — knowing where you started does tell you something about where you are, just nothing extra once $X_n$ is known.
  • $p_{ij}$ is conditional, $\mathbf{P}(X_n=j)$ is not. The rows of $P$ are conditional distributions, one per starting state. They only become a distribution over states after you supply an initial distribution.
  • Order of indices. $p_{ij}$ is "from $i$ to $j$"; the first index is the present, the second the future. Rows sum to $1$, columns need not. Transposing $P$ silently reverses time and is the single most common arithmetic slip in this material.
  • Self-transitions count. $p_{ii}>0$ is allowed and normal. B&T are explicit: "Even though the state does not change, we still view this as a state transition of a special type (a 'self-transition')." A step of the clock always happens, even when nothing visible does.

0.2 One notation to fix now: $p_{ij}$ versus $r_{ij}(n)$

The whole note runs on two symbols, and keeping them apart is worth thirty seconds.

  • $p_{ij}=\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i)$ — one step. Given data of the model.
  • $r_{ij}(n)=\mathbf{P}(X_n=j\mid X_0=i)$ — the $n$-step transition probability, the chance of being in $j$ after $n$ steps having started in $i$ (L16 slide 4). Computed, not given.

They are linked by the key recursion of L16 slide 4, $$r_{ij}(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{m} r_{ik}(n-1)\,p_{kj},$$ which just conditions on where the chain was one step before the end: $r_{ik}(n-1)$ is the probability of being at $k$ at time $n-1$, and $p_{kj}$ is the chance of stepping from there to $j$. Started off by $r_{ij}(0)=1$ if $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise, it generates the entire table; in matrix form it says $R(n)=R(n-1)P$, hence $R(n)=P^{n}$. §1 derives it with every step and states the general Chapman–Kolmogorov identity; here it is quoted only so that the three questions below can be phrased.

Gotcha. $r_{ij}(1)=p_{ij}$, but $r_{ij}(2)\ne p_{ij}^2$ — the two-step probability is a sum over intermediate states, not a product along one path. For the chain above, $r_{11}(2)=0.35$ whereas $p_{11}^2=0.25$; the difference $0.10=p_{12}p_{21}=0.5\times0.2$ is the path $1\to2\to1$ that squaring the entry forgets (all values from computes/g5_s0.py).

0.3 The three questions this note answers

Given a chain, essentially everything asked of it in 6.041 is one of three questions about $r_{ij}(n)$, distinguished by the time horizon. L16 slide 6 poses the last two under the heading "generic convergence questions"; L18 slide 1 poses the third. Each gets one number here as a preview, computed in computes/g5_s0.py; each is developed by a later section.

Question A — the short run: where am I after $n$ steps? Iterate the recursion. For the two-state chain of Fig. 0.1, starting from state 1, $$r_{11}(0)=1,\quad r_{11}(1)=0.5,\quad r_{11}(2)=0.35,\quad r_{11}(3)=0.305,$$ and correspondingly $r_{12}(n)=1-r_{11}(n)$ takes the values $0,\,0.5,\,0.65,\,0.695$. Nothing subtle is happening: this is arithmetic, and §1 supplies the derivation, the matrix reading, and the checkout-counter model that makes it worth doing.

Question B — the long run: does $r_{ij}(n)$ settle down, and does the answer forget where it started? For that same chain it does, quickly. The numbers converge to $\pi_1=2/7=0.285714$ and $\pi_2=5/7=0.714286$ from both starting states — at $n=100$ and $n=101$ every entry of the matrix agrees with these to eight decimals, which is the point L16 slide 5's blank table was built to reveal. The convergence is geometric: the largest deviation $\max_{i,j}|r_{ij}(n)-\pi_j|$ falls $0.214286,\ 0.0642857,\ 0.0192857$ at $n=1,2,3$, each about $0.3$ times the last, and reaches $2.49\times10^{-11}$ by $n=20$. (The ratio $0.3$ is not a coincidence — it is the second-largest eigenvalue modulus of $P$, the first being $1$; §3 explains why $1$ is always an eigenvalue.) But convergence can fail in two distinct ways, which is why §2 must come before §3:

  • Periodicity. For the three-state chain of L16 slide 6 ($p_{12}=1$, $p_{21}=p_{23}=0.5$, $p_{32}=1$), the chain sits on state 2 only at even times: $r_{22}(n)=1,0,1,0,1,0,1,0,1$ for $n=0,\ldots,8$. There is no limit at all — the sequence oscillates forever.
  • Several destinations. For the four-state chain of L16 slide 6 (state 1 absorbing; from state 2, $p_{21}=0.3$, $p_{22}=0.4$, $p_{23}=0.3$; states 3 and 4 forming a closed pair), the limit exists but depends on the start: $r_{11}(n)=1$, $r_{31}(n)=0$, and $r_{21}(n)\to 0.5$. Where you end up is decided by where you began.

Question C — the endgame: when there are several possible destinations, which one, and how long until you get there? That last number, $0.5$, is the preview of §4. It is not obtained by taking powers of a matrix but by a one-line equation: writing $a_i$ for the probability of eventual absorption into state $1$ starting from $i$, condition on the first step out of state 2, $$a_2=0.3\underbrace{a_1}_{=1}+0.4\,a_2+0.3\underbrace{a_3}_{=0}\;\Longrightarrow\;a_2(1-0.4)=0.3\;\Longrightarrow\;a_2=\frac{0.3}{0.6}=0.5,$$ matching the $n\to\infty$ matrix computation exactly. Here $a_1=1$ because state $1$ absorbs immediately and $a_3=0$ because the pair $\{3,4\}$ can never be left. The same first-step idea, applied to times instead of probabilities, gives the expected time to absorption — from state 2 the chain leaves on each step with probability $0.3+0.3=0.6$, so by the mean $1/p$ of a geometric random variable (G2 §3) it lingers $1/0.6=1.666667$ steps on average.

Interpretation — three questions, three kinds of arithmetic. The three questions do not merely differ in horizon; they call for structurally different computations, and recognizing which you face is most of the skill. Question A is forward iteration (multiply by $P$ repeatedly). Question B is a linear system in the unknown long-run frequencies $\pi_j$ — the balance equations, indexed by the recurrent states. Question C is a linear system in unknowns indexed by the transient states — the first-step equations, with boundary values pinned at the absorbing destinations. §5 turns this into an explicit decision procedure; for now, notice that B and C are never both the right tool for the same chain, because B needs a single recurrent class and C needs several.

0.4 Roadmap

  • §1 — The Markov model: states, transition probabilities, $n$-step transitions (L16 slides 2–4, rec18 modeling parts, B&T §7.1–7.2). Building a chain from a verbal description, the transition matrix and diagram, the checkout-counter queue of L16 slide 2 derived in full (including why the queue length is Markov and what $p_{i,i+1}$, $p_{i,i-1}$, $p_{ii}$ are in terms of the arrival rate $p$ and service rate $q$), the key recursion derived rather than asserted, and Chapman–Kolmogorov. Hook: the modeling step — deciding what to remember — is the only step with no formula for it.
  • §2 — Classification of states: recurrent, transient, periodic (L16 slides 6–7, L17 slides 3–4, rec18 classification parts, B&T §7.2). Which states can be reached from which, recurrent classes and transient states, why a transient state is visited only finitely often, periodicity, and the exact conditions under which $r_{ij}(n)$ converges to a limit that forgets the initial state. Hook: everything here is read off the arrow diagram; the numbers on the arrows do not matter, only which of them are positive.
  • §3 — Steady-state probabilities and balance equations (L17 slides 5–8, L18 slide 2, B&T §7.3). The steady-state convergence theorem, the balance equations $\pi_j=\sum_k\pi_k p_{kj}$ together with the normalization $\sum_j\pi_j=1$, the long-run-frequency-of-visits interpretation, and birth–death chains with their cut equations $\pi_i p_i=\pi_{i+1}q_{i+1}$. Hook: the balance equations alone are singular — they fix $\pi$ only up to a scale factor, and the normalization is what makes the answer unique.
  • §4 — Absorption probabilities and expected time to absorption (L18 slides 4–7, rec19, B&T §7.4). First-step analysis for absorption probabilities $a_i$, for the expected time to absorption $\mu_i$, and for mean first-passage and mean recurrence times; the phone-company blocking problem; and analyses conditioned on which class absorbs. Hook: one idea — condition on the first transition — produces every equation system in the section.
  • §5 — Synthesis and checkpoint (all three lectures plus rec16–rec17 review). Choosing between the balance system and the absorption system, the standard failure modes, mixed problems, and a "test yourself" pass over continuous random variables (G3) and the Poisson process (G4). Hook: a Markov-chain problem is usually easy once you have decided which linear system to write; deciding is the exam skill.

Behind you: G1 (conditioning and the total probability theorem, used on literally every page here), G2 (PMFs, expectation, the geometric distribution, which reappears as the time spent in a state), G3 (continuous random variables), G4 (the Bernoulli process, whose arrivals drive the queueing chains, and iterated expectations, which is what first-step analysis silently uses). Ahead: B&T §7.5 extends all of this to continuous time, outside the scope of these lectures.
