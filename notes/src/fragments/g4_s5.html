§5   Synthesis, gotchas, and the bridge to Markov chains

Sources: L12 slides 2–8 · L13 slides 2–8 · L14 slides 2–8 · L15 slides 2–8 · rec12–rec15 · L16 slides 1–3 (forward reference only) · B&T §4.3, §4.5, §6.1–6.3, §7.1. Every number on this page is recomputed in computes/g4_s5.py — closed forms cross-checked by exact Fraction arithmetic, exact convolution of PMFs, scipy.integrate.quad, and (for the two length-biasing results) an independent Monte-Carlo run — and the figure by computes/g4_s5_fig.py.

Four lectures introduced what looks like four topics: conditional expectation as a random variable, sums with a random number of terms, the Bernoulli process, and the Poisson process. They are one topic. Every result in G4 comes from a single move — condition on the piece of information that makes the problem easy, solve the easy problem, then average back over the information — and every arrival process in G4 is the same process viewed on a discrete or a continuous clock. This section is the one page to revise from: the identity list (§5.1), the two-process correspondence table (§5.2), the four mistakes that cost the most points (§5.3), and the single assumption that G4 makes everywhere and G5 drops, which is what makes Markov chains a new subject (§5.4).

Nothing here is proved for the first time. §1 built $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ as a random variable and proved the law of iterated expectations and the law of total variance (L12, rec12); §2 derived the random-sum formulas and the Bernoulli process (L12 end, L13, rec13); §3 built the Poisson process from its three defining properties (L14, rec14); §4 did merging, splitting, the Erlang distribution and random incidence (L15, rec15). Where a result is only stated here, the section that proves it is named — with one exception, the discrete random-incidence formula of §5.2, which no earlier section covers and which is therefore derived here.

5.1 The conditioning toolkit on one page

In every row below, $X$ is the random variable whose mean or variance you want, $Y$ is the random variable you condition on (the "extra information"), $y$ is a value $Y$ might take, $A_1,\dots,A_n$ is a partition of the sample space into events of positive probability, $N$ is a nonnegative-integer-valued random variable (a random count), and $X_1,X_2,\dots$ are i.i.d. random variables independent of $N$, with common mean $\mathbb{E}[X]$ and variance $\operatorname{var}(X)$. The column "type" is the single most useful thing on the page: it says whether the object on the left is a number or a random variable, and almost every error in G4 is a violation of that column.

The two laws are the same statement about two moments, and the random-sum formulas are that statement applied with $Y=N$. Read the last two rows as: the mean of a random sum only needs the two means; the variance needs four quantities, because $Y$ is random for two separate reasons — each term is random, and how many terms there are is random.

Interpretation — within versus between. Both decompositions split randomness the same way. In the class-sections example of L12 slides 4–5 ($\mathbf{P}(Y=1)=10/30=1/3$ students in a section of mean score $90$ and within-section variance $10$; $\mathbf{P}(Y=2)=20/30=2/3$ in a section of mean $60$ and variance $20$), the toolkit gives $\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac13(90)+\frac23(60)=70$, $$\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]=\tfrac13(10)+\tfrac23(20)=\tfrac{50}{3}=16.666667, \qquad \operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)=\tfrac13(90-70)^2+\tfrac23(60-70)^2=200,$$ so $\operatorname{var}(X)=\frac{50}{3}+200=\frac{650}{3}=216.666667$. Reading the two terms: $\frac{50}{3}$ is the average variability within a section, $200$ is the variability between sections, and here the between-section term is $92.3077\%$ of the total — knowing a student's section explains almost all of the spread. (Cross-checked in computes/g4_s5.py against an explicit 30-student roster with exactly these section means and within-section variances, whose pooled variance is $216.666667$.) The identical reading applies to the random sum: in $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)+(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N)$, the first term is the variability of the individual terms averaged over how many there are, the second is the variability caused by the count itself.
Recipe — divide and conquer with conditioning (L12; B&T §4.3, §4.5).
  1. Name the information that simplifies the problem. The right $Y$ is the quantity whose value turns a messy random object into a familiar one: the section a student is in, the number of stores visited, the number of arrivals in an interval, the first step of a sequential experiment.
  2. Compute $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$ and, if variance is wanted, $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)$ — treating $y$ as a fixed constant. This is ordinary G2/G3 work on a conditional distribution.
  3. Replace $y$ by $Y$ to get the random variables $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)$. Write them down explicitly as functions of $Y$; this is the step people skip and then regret.
  4. Average. $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]$. If you want the variance, you need both pieces: $\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]+\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)$.
  5. Sanity-check the units and the sign. Both variance terms are nonnegative, so $\operatorname{var}(X)\ge\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y])$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)\ge\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)]$: conditioning can never make the total variance smaller than either piece.

A worked check of the random-sum pair. Take $N$ Poisson with parameter $2.5$ (so $\mathbb{E}[N]=\operatorname{var}(N)=2.5$) and $X_i$ uniform on $\{1,2,3\}$ dollars, so $\mathbb{E}[X]=2$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}[X])^2=\frac{14}{3}-4=\frac23=0.666667$. The formulas give $\mathbb{E}[Y]=2.5\times 2=5$ and $$\operatorname{var}(Y)=\underbrace{2.5\times 0.666667}_{=1.666667\ \text{(within terms)}} +\underbrace{2^2\times 2.5}_{=10\ \text{(across counts)}}=11.666667 .$$ computes/g4_s5.py builds the exact PMF of $Y$ by convolving the PMF of $X$ with itself $n$ times and mixing over $n$, and recovers $\mathbb{E}[Y]=5$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y)=11.666667$ to machine precision. Note the size of the two terms: dropping the second one — the standard error — would report $1.666667$, which is $14.29\%$ of the truth. When $N$ is at all variable, the randomness of the count dominates.

Practice 5.1 (types, then both variance terms). A help desk has busy days with probability $0.4$ and quiet days with probability $0.6$. Let $B$ be the day type. Given a busy day the number of calls $N$ is Poisson with mean $20$; given a quiet day it is Poisson with mean $8$. (i) Write down the random variable $\mathbb{E}[N\mid B]$ — its values and their probabilities — and $\mathbb{E}[N]$. (ii) Find $\operatorname{var}(N)$. (iii) Is $N$ Poisson?
Solution

(i) The function $g(b)=\mathbb{E}[N\mid B=b]$ takes two values, $g(\text{busy})=20$ and $g(\text{quiet})=8$, because a Poisson mean is its parameter. So $\mathbb{E}[N\mid B]$ is the random variable equal to $20$ with probability $0.4$ and $8$ with probability $0.6$ — a random variable, not the number $12.8$. Averaging it (law of iterated expectations) gives $$\mathbb{E}[N]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[N\mid B]\big]=0.4(20)+0.6(8)=8+4.8=12.8$$ (p51.EN).

(ii) Both terms. Within: for a Poisson, $\operatorname{var}(N\mid B)$ equals the same parameter, so $\operatorname{var}(N\mid B)$ is $20$ with probability $0.4$ and $8$ with probability $0.6$, and $$\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(N\mid B)\big]=0.4(20)+0.6(8)=12.8 .$$ Between: $\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[N\mid B]\big)=0.4(20-12.8)^2+0.6(8-12.8)^2 =0.4(51.84)+0.6(23.04)=20.736+13.824=34.56$. Adding, $$\operatorname{var}(N)=12.8+34.56=47.36$$ (p51.varN, cross-checked in computes/g4_s5.py by summing the exact mixture PMF $0.4\,p_{20}(k)+0.6\,p_8(k)$ directly). Note that the between term is the larger one here: most of the spread in the call count is which kind of day it is, not Poisson noise within a day.

(iii) No. A Poisson random variable has mean $=$ variance, and $47.36\neq 12.8$. A mixture of Poissons is overdispersed — the extra $34.56$ is exactly the variance the mixing adds. This is the Gotcha-2 failure mode in reverse: someone who averages only the conditional variances would report $12.8$, conclude "mean $=$ variance, so $N$ is Poisson", and be wrong twice.

Practice 5.2 (synthesis: Poisson splitting $+$ random sum). Customers enter a shop as a Poisson process of rate $\lambda=10$ per hour. Independently of everything else, each customer is a "big spender" with probability $q=0.2$. Big spenders' purchase amounts are i.i.d. with mean $\$50$ and standard deviation $\$20$, independent of the arrival process. Over a $3$-hour shift, let $Y$ be the total spent by big spenders. Find $\mathbb{E}[Y]$ and $\operatorname{var}(Y)$, and say which piece of the variance dominates.
Solution

Step 1 — split (§4.3). Routing each Poisson arrival by an independent coin gives a Poisson process of rate $q\lambda=0.2\times 10=2$ big spenders per hour. Over $T=3$ hours the count $N$ of big spenders is therefore Poisson with parameter $$q\lambda T=2\times 3=6,\qquad \mathbb{E}[N]=\operatorname{var}(N)=6 .$$ The splitting property is what lets us ignore the other $8$ per hour entirely.

Step 2 — recognize the random sum (§2.2). $Y=X_1+\cdots+X_N$ with $X_i$ i.i.d., $\mathbb{E}[X]=50$, $\operatorname{var}(X)=20^2=400$, and $N$ independent of the $X_i$ (the coin and the amounts are independent of each other). The independence hypothesis is what makes the §5.1 formulas apply; without it, neither one is valid.

Step 3 — apply both formulas. $$\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[N]\,\mathbb{E}[X]=6\times 50=\$300,$$ $$\operatorname{var}(Y)=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)}_{6\times 400=2400} +\underbrace{(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N)}_{50^2\times 6=15000}=17400 ,$$ so the standard deviation is $\sqrt{17400}=131.909060$ dollars (p52; a two-million-shift simulation returns $300.102097$ and $17417.648618$). The count term is $15000/17400=86.206897\%$ of the variance: on a shift this short, the dominant uncertainty is how many big spenders walk in, not how much each one spends. Reporting only the first term would give a standard deviation of $\sqrt{2400}=\$48.989795$ and badly understate the risk.

5.2 Bernoulli and Poisson: one process, two clocks

The Poisson process is what the Bernoulli process becomes when the time slot shrinks to zero while the expected number of arrivals per unit time is held fixed (L14 slides 4 and 7; B&T §6.2). Take slots of length $\delta$, so a window of length $t$ holds $n=t/\delta$ slots, and let the per-slot success probability be $p=\lambda\delta$, where $\lambda$ is the arrival rate in arrivals per unit time. Then $$np=\frac{t}{\delta}\cdot\lambda\delta=\lambda t ,$$ independent of $\delta$ — the expected number of arrivals in the window is pinned while the clock gets finer. Every row of the correspondence table below is a consequence of that one limit.

Fig. 5.1 — The Bernoulli-to-Poisson limit (L14 slides 4, 7; B&T §6.2). (a) The same arrival pattern seen through slots of length $\delta=0.1$, then $\delta=0.04$, then in continuous time; on the coarsest clock two arrivals share a slot and are miscounted as one, which is exactly the error the small-interval assumption $P(k,\delta)\approx 0$ for $k>1$ makes vanish as $\delta\to 0$. (b) With $np=\lambda t=2$ held fixed, the binomial PMF collapses onto Poisson$(2)$: the total-variation distance to the Poisson falls $0.729\to 0.174\to 0.0522\to 0.004572\to 0.0004517$ as $\delta$ runs $0.5,\,0.25,\,0.1,\,0.01,\,0.001$ (computes/g4_s5.py).

The one row §2 does not cover — random incidence on the discrete clock. §2.6 stops at merging and splitting, so the Bernoulli entry above is proved nowhere else in the note. It follows in three lines from two facts we already have. The gap between consecutive Bernoulli$(p)$ arrivals is geometric, $p_T(\ell)=p(1-p)^{\ell-1}$ on $\ell=1,2,\dots$, with $\mathbb{E}[T]=1/p$ (§2.4); and landing in a gap at an arbitrary slot selects that gap with probability proportional to its length, which is the length-biasing rule of §4.4 in its discrete form, $$p_L(\ell)=\frac{\ell\,p_T(\ell)}{\mathbb{E}[T]}=\ell\,p(1-p)^{\ell-1}\cdot p=\ell\,p^2(1-p)^{\ell-1}.$$ Its mean is $\mathbb{E}[L]=\sum_\ell \ell^2 p_T(\ell)\,p=p\,\mathbb{E}[T^2]=p\cdot\frac{2-p}{p^2}=\frac{2-p}{p}$, using $\mathbb{E}[T^2]=\operatorname{var}(T)+(\mathbb{E}[T])^2=(1-p)/p^2+1/p^2=(2-p)/p^2$. At $p=0.2$: typical gap $\mathbb{E}[T]=5$ slots, observed gap $\mathbb{E}[L]=9$ slots — a factor $1.8$, not $2$. The shortfall is the discreteness: the observed gap is at least one slot long no matter what. Under the same limit as the rest of §5.2 ($p=\lambda\delta$, gaps measured in time units) the factor closes, $\delta\,\mathbb{E}[L]=\delta(2-\lambda\delta)/(\lambda\delta)=(2-\lambda\delta)/\lambda\to 2/\lambda$, recovering the Erlang$(2)$ mean in the Poisson column: at $\lambda=2$ and $\delta=10^{-4}$ this is $0.999900$ against the limit $2/\lambda=1$. Note also that $p_L$ is not the Pascal$(2)$ PMF $(\ell-1)p^2(1-p)^{\ell-2}$ — the discrete analogue of Erlang$(2)$ — so the two clocks agree only in the limit, not slot for slot (computes/g4_s5.py).

Two numerical illustrations of the limit, both from computes/g4_s5.py with $\lambda=2$ and $t=1$. First the variance: the binomial variance $np(1-p)$ equals $1.600000$ at $\delta=0.1$ and $1.960000$ at $\delta=0.01$, climbing to the Poisson value $\lambda t=2$ as $p=\lambda\delta\to0$ kills the factor $(1-p)$ — which is why the Poisson has mean and variance equal while the binomial has variance strictly smaller than its mean. Second the interarrival time: at $\delta=0.001$ the geometric mean is $1/p=500$ slots, and $500$ slots is $500\times 0.001=0.5$ time units $=1/\lambda$, the exponential mean.

Recipe — which process, and which formula (L13 s2; L14 s3, s7; L15 s2).
  1. Discrete or continuous clock? "Each day", "each time slot", "each trial", "each packet" $\to$ Bernoulli, parameter $p$ per trial. "At a rate of $\lambda$ per hour", "arrivals in continuous time" $\to$ Poisson, parameter $\lambda$ per unit time. Watch the units: $p$ is dimensionless, $\lambda$ is 1/time.
  2. What is being asked about? A count in a fixed window $\to$ binomial / Poisson. A time until something $\to$ geometric / exponential (first arrival) or Pascal / Erlang ($k$th arrival).
  3. Does the question restart the clock? "Given nothing has happened yet", "additional time" — invoke memorylessness and start over from scratch; the elapsed wait is irrelevant.
  4. Several streams? Independent streams combine by adding rates (Poisson) or by $1-(1-p_1)(1-p_2)$ (Bernoulli); an independently-routed stream splits by multiplying the rate by the routing probability.
  5. Is the observer choosing the interval, or is the interval choosing the observer? If you show up at an arbitrary instant, you are in the length-biased regime — go to §5.3, Gotcha 3.
  6. Random number of terms? If the quantity is a sum over the arrivals ($Y=X_1+\cdots+X_N$ with $N$ the arrival count), use the §5.1 random-sum formulas, not a fixed-$n$ argument.
Practice 5.3 (the unit trap). A detector registers particles as a Poisson process of rate $\lambda=3$ per minute. A student wants to simulate it by checking the detector every $\delta=0.01$ minutes and flipping a coin each time, and writes "$p=3$". (i) What is wrong, and what is the right $p$? (ii) Compute $\mathbf{P}(\text{no particle in }0.5\text{ minutes})$ both exactly and in the student's (corrected) slotted model. (iii) The slotted model says the mean wait to the first particle is $1/p$ slots. Show this is the exponential mean.
Solution

(i) $p$ is a probability and therefore dimensionless; $\lambda$ has units of $1/\text{time}$. So "$p=3$" is both dimensionally wrong and impossible (a probability exceeding $1$). The bridge of §5.2 is $p=\lambda\delta$, which carries the units correctly: $$p=\lambda\delta=3\times 0.01=0.03 ,$$ dimensionless as required. This is the single most common Bernoulli/Poisson error: $\lambda$ is a rate, $p$ is a probability per slot, and only the product $\lambda\delta$ converts between them.

(ii) Exactly, using the Poisson PMF with $\tau=0.5$: $$P(0,0.5)=e^{-\lambda\tau}=e^{-3\times 0.5}=e^{-1.5}=0.223130 .$$ In the slotted model the window holds $n=0.5/0.01=50$ slots, and "no particle" means $50$ independent failures: $$(1-p)^{50}=(0.97)^{50}=0.218065 .$$ The two agree to $0.005065$ (p53). The slotted answer is slightly low because a slot that receives two particles is miscounted as one arrival — exactly the collision effect of Fig. 5.1(a), which vanishes as $\delta\to 0$.

(iii) The number of slots to the first success is geometric$(p)$ with mean $1/p=1/0.03=33.333333$ slots. Each slot lasts $\delta=0.01$ minutes, so in time units the mean wait is $$33.333333\times 0.01=0.333333\text{ minutes}=\frac{1}{3}=\frac1\lambda ,$$ the exponential mean. The conversion is the same $p=\lambda\delta$ read backwards: $1/p$ slots is $\delta/p=\delta/(\lambda\delta)=1/\lambda$ time units, for every $\delta$. Whenever a slotted answer and a continuous answer disagree by a factor that looks like $\delta$, you have failed to multiply a slot count by the slot length.

Practice 5.4 (merging, then conditioning on which came first). Work email arrives as a Poisson process of rate $\lambda_W=5$ per hour, personal email as an independent Poisson process of rate $\lambda_P=3$ per hour. (i) What is the probability that the next email is personal? (ii) Starting from an empty inbox, what is the expected time until you have received at least one of each kind?
Solution

(i) Merging (§4.2): the pooled stream is Poisson with rate $\lambda=\lambda_W+\lambda_P=5+3=8$ per hour, and each arrival is personal, independently, with probability $$\frac{\lambda_P}{\lambda_W+\lambda_P}=\frac{3}{8}=0.375000 .$$

(ii) Condition on the first arrival — the "name the information that simplifies the problem" step of the §5.1 recipe. Let $T$ be the time until you hold one of each. The first email arrives after an $\text{Exp}(8)$ wait with mean $1/8=0.125$ hours, and it is of one kind or the other. Given it was work (probability $5/8$), memorylessness (§3.3) restarts the personal stream from scratch, so the remaining wait is $\text{Exp}(3)$ with mean $1/3$; given it was personal (probability $3/8$), the remaining wait is $\text{Exp}(5)$ with mean $1/5$. Hence, by the total expectation theorem, $$\mathbb{E}[T]=\frac18+\frac58\cdot\frac13+\frac38\cdot\frac15 =0.125000+0.208333+0.075000=0.408333\text{ hours}=24.500000\text{ minutes}$$ (p54; a $400\,000$-run simulation of $\max(T_W,T_P)$ gives $0.407452$). Note the trap this avoids: $\mathbb{E}[T]$ is not $\max(1/5,1/3)=1/3$, and it is not $1/5+1/3$ either — you must average over which stream fires first, because the two cases leave different amounts of waiting to do.

5.3 The four expensive mistakes

Each of the following is a place where a G4 object is confused with a G2/G3 object of a different type. Every number quoted is recomputed in computes/g4_s5.py.

Gotcha 1 — $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ is a random variable, and $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$ is a number. The notation differs by three characters and the type differs completely (L12 slide 2). Symptoms of getting it wrong: writing "$\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]=70$" (a random variable cannot equal a constant unless it is degenerate — in the class example $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ equals $90$ with probability $1/3$ and $60$ with probability $2/3$, and $70$ is its mean); writing "$\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y])$", which is the variance of a number, hence $0$; or trying to take $\mathbb{E}$ of $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$ and finding nothing left to average over. The test that settles it every time: does the expression still contain a lowercase $y$? If yes it is a number (or a function of $y$); if the conditioning symbol is the capital $Y$, it is a random variable and you may take its expectation and its variance. In particular the law of iterated expectations $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]=\mathbb{E}[X]$ only parses at all because the inner object is a random variable — the outer $\mathbb{E}$ averages over the randomness of $Y$ (§1.2).
Gotcha 2 — variance does not average. Expectation does: $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]$, full stop. Variance does not: $\operatorname{var}(X)\neq\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]$ in general, and the missing piece $\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)$ is usually the larger one. In the class example the averaged within-section variance is $\frac{50}{3}=16.666667$ while the true variance is $\frac{650}{3}=216.666667$ — the naive answer is short by a factor of $13$, because it ignores that the two sections sit $30$ points apart. In the random-sum example of §5.1 the same omission reports $1.666667$ instead of $11.666667$, i.e. $14.29\%$ of the truth. The mirror-image error is dropping $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)]$ instead, which is what you get if you forget that $X$ is still random once $Y$ is known. Both terms, every time — and note the asymmetry of the two: $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{var}(\cdot)]$ takes the expectation of a variance, while $\operatorname{var}(\mathbb{E}[\cdot])$ takes the variance of an expectation; swapping them is a different (wrong) quantity. The special case worth memorizing is the random sum: the count's variance enters multiplied by $(\mathbb{E}[X])^2$, so a random $N$ inflates $\operatorname{var}(Y)$ enormously whenever the individual terms are large on average (L12 slide 8).
Gotcha 3 — random incidence: the interval you land in is not a typical interval. The interarrival times of a Poisson process are exponential with mean $1/\lambda$. If you show up at an arbitrary instant $t^*$ and measure the gap $L$ containing it, $L$ is Erlang of order $2$ with mean $2/\lambda$ — twice as long (L15 slide 7; B&T §6.2 and its Figure 6.7). The reason is not a paradox but arithmetic: long intervals cover more of the time axis, so an arbitrary instant is more likely to fall in one. Formally the observed length has the length-biased density $$f_L(\ell)=\frac{\ell\,f_T(\ell)}{\mathbb{E}[T]},$$ which for $f_T(\ell)=\lambda e^{-\lambda\ell}$ and $\mathbb{E}[T]=1/\lambda$ collapses to $\lambda^2\ell e^{-\lambda\ell}$, the Erlang$(2)$ density; quadrature confirms it integrates to $1$ and has mean $2/\lambda$ — at $\lambda=0.5$ per minute, the typical gap is $2$ minutes and the observed gap is $4$ minutes. The same bias applies to any renewal process, exponential or not (L15 slide 8). With bus gaps equally likely to be $5$ or $10$ minutes, the plain mean gap is $\frac{5+10}{2}=7.5$ minutes, but an arriving passenger lands in a $5$-minute gap with probability $\frac{5}{5+10}=\frac13=0.333333$ and in a $10$-minute gap with probability $\frac{10}{15}=\frac23=0.666667$, so the observed gap averages $\frac13(5)+\frac23(10)=\frac{25}{3}=8.333333$ minutes and the expected wait to the next bus is $\frac13\cdot\frac52+\frac23\cdot\frac{10}{2}=\frac{25}{6}=4.166667$ minutes, not the naive $7.5/2=3.75$. A 400 000-gap simulation gives $8.336675$ and $4.172885$. The tell: whenever the problem says "you arrive at a random time" rather than "consider the $k$th interval", the selection mechanism is biased and the unbiased distribution is the wrong one to use.
Gotcha 4 — split streams: independent for Poisson, dependent for Bernoulli. Splitting looks like the same operation in both worlds — route each arrival to stream 1 or stream 2 by an independent coin of bias $q$ — and in both worlds each output stream is again a process of the same kind, with rate multiplied by $q$ or $1-q$ (L13 slide 7; L15 slide 6). But the two outputs are related differently. For a Poisson process the outputs are genuinely independent processes: with $\lambda=4$ and $q=0.25$ the split rates are $1$ and $3$, and the joint PMF of the two counts over a window factors exactly, $\max|\mathbf{P}(N_1=j,N_2=k)-\mathbf{P}(N_1=j)\mathbf{P}(N_2=k)|=4\times10^{-17}$, i.e. zero to machine precision. For a Bernoulli process they are not: a slot holds at most one arrival, so an arrival routed up is an arrival not routed down. With $p=0.5$ and $q=0.5$ the per-slot marginals are $\mathbf{P}(A=1)=pq=0.25$ and $\mathbf{P}(B=1)=p(1-q)=0.25$, but $\mathbf{P}(A=1,B=1)=0\neq 0.0625=\mathbf{P}(A=1)\mathbf{P}(B=1)$. The same asymmetry runs through merging: merged Poisson rates simply add ($\lambda_1+\lambda_2$, so with $\lambda_1=2$ and $\lambda_2=3$ the merged rate is $5$ and the next arrival is from the first stream with probability $\frac{2}{5}=0.4$), while merged Bernoulli probabilities do not: with $p_1=0.2$ and $p_2=0.3$ the merged $p$ is $1-(0.8)(0.7)=0.44$, not $0.5$ — the shortfall $0.06=p_1p_2$ is exactly the probability of a collision, counted once (L13 slide 8). Collisions have probability zero in continuous time, which is why the Poisson arithmetic is the clean one.
Interpretation — a four-line self-check for any G4 answer. (i) Is every conditional object the type I think it is — number where a number belongs, random variable where I am about to take an expectation or a variance? (ii) If I computed a variance by conditioning, did I write down both terms? (iii) If the problem involves a sum, is the number of terms fixed or random — and if random, is it independent of the terms? (iv) If an observer or an interval was "chosen at random", did I ask by what mechanism? Gotchas 1–4 each fail exactly one of these four checks.
Practice 5.5 (spot the biased selection rule). A college runs classes of two sizes: $80\%$ of its classes hold $20$ students and $20\%$ hold $100$. The registrar picks a class uniformly at random and reports the mean class size; a journalist stops a student uniformly at random and asks how big that student's class is. (i) What does each report? (ii) Which one is the length-biased quantity, and why does the registrar's answer not describe anybody's experience?
Solution

(i) Registrar (unbiased over classes). The size $T$ of a uniformly chosen class has $p_T(20)=0.8$, $p_T(100)=0.2$, so $$\mathbb{E}[T]=0.8(20)+0.2(100)=16+20=36\ \text{students}.$$ Journalist (biased by size). A uniformly chosen student is not a uniformly chosen class: a class contributes as many students as it holds. Take $C$ classes; they hold $0.8C(20)=16C$ students in small classes and $0.2C(100)=20C$ in large ones, $36C$ in all. So the sampled student is in a large class with probability $$\mathbf{P}(L=100)=\frac{20C}{36C}=\frac59=0.555556,\qquad \mathbf{P}(L=20)=\frac{16C}{36C}=\frac49 ,$$ and the reported size averages $$\mathbb{E}[L]=\tfrac49(20)+\tfrac59(100)=\frac{80+500}{9}=\frac{580}{9}=64.444444\ \text{students}$$ (p55). The registrar says $36$; the students say $64.4$; both are right about different questions.

(ii) The journalist's is the length-biased one, and it is the same formula as Gotcha 3 with class size playing the role of interval length: $$p_L(\ell)=\frac{\ell\,p_T(\ell)}{\mathbb{E}[T]}\quad\Rightarrow\quad p_L(100)=\frac{100\times 0.2}{36}=\frac{20}{36}=\frac59 ,$$ matching the count above. The tell is exactly the one in §5.3: the selection is made by picking a point inside the object (a student inside a class, an instant inside an interarrival gap) rather than by picking the object itself, so large objects are oversampled in proportion to their size. The registrar's $36$ describes the average class; no student has the average class, and the average student's class is nearly twice as big. Same arithmetic, three costumes: buses (§5.3), Poisson interarrival gaps (§4.4), class sizes.

5.4 Bridge to G5: where the independence goes

Everything in G4 rests on one assumption, stated in a different dialect in each lecture but always the same: disjoint stretches of time are independent. For the Bernoulli process it is "a sequence of independent Bernoulli trials" (L13 slide 2); for the Poisson process it is the axiom "numbers of arrivals in disjoint time intervals are independent" (L14 slide 3, L15 slide 2). Memorylessness is a corollary, not an extra assumption: if the past tells you nothing about the future, then knowing that you have already waited $m$ trials or $s$ time units cannot change the remaining wait, which is why the geometric and the exponential — and only they — appear as interarrival times. Independence is also what licenses the merging and splitting arithmetic of §4 and the random-sum formulas of §2, whose derivation used $\mathbb{E}[X_1+\cdots+X_n\mid N=n]=\mathbb{E}[X_1+\cdots+X_n]$ — a step that is legal only because $N$ carries no information about the $X_i$ (L12 slide 7).

The next group removes exactly that assumption, and nothing else. A Markov process is a sequence $X_0,X_1,X_2,\dots$ of random variables — the state at each time — in which the future does depend on the past, but only through the present: $$\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i,\ X_{n-1}=i_{n-1},\dots,X_0=i_0)=\mathbf{P}(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i)=p_{ij},$$ the transition probability from state $i$ to state $j$ (L16 slides 2–3; B&T §7.1). Read the equation as a statement about information: the whole history $i_0,\dots,i_{n-1}$ may be deleted once $i_n$ is known, because the current state summarizes everything about the past that matters for the future. That is strictly weaker than independence — under independence you may delete $i_n$ too — and strictly stronger than nothing.

The continuity is unusually direct. L16's opening model is a checkout counter in which arrivals are Bernoulli$(p)$ and service times are geometric$(q)$, and the state $X_n$ is the number of customers at the counter (those waiting plus the one in service, so state $1$ means one customer being served and none waiting): both G4 processes appear as ingredients, and the memorylessness of the geometric is what makes the resulting state sequence Markov in the first place. Three threads carry straight over:

  • Conditioning as the engine. The $n$-step transition probabilities satisfy $r_{ij}(n)=\sum_k r_{ik}(n-1)p_{kj}$ — the total probability theorem applied to the state at time $n-1$. The law of iterated expectations reappears as the tool for expected times to absorption: condition on the first step, average over it (G5).
  • Type discipline. $\mathbb{E}[\text{time to absorption}\mid X_0=i]$ is a number for each state $i$ and a random variable when the starting state is random — precisely the distinction of Gotcha 1, now indexed by states.
  • The processes themselves. A Bernoulli process is the trivial Markov chain in which the transition probabilities do not depend on the current state at all; the Poisson process is its continuous-time counterpart. Adding state-dependence is the single new idea.

One thing does not carry over, and it is worth flagging now: the closed-form distributions. Binomial, geometric, Pascal, Poisson, exponential and Erlang exist because independence makes the algebra collapse. Once the future depends on the present, the distribution of the state at time $n$ is generally obtained by iterating a matrix rather than by a formula, and the interesting questions change accordingly — not "what is $\mathbf{P}(X_n=j)$?" but "where does the chain end up in the long run, and how long does it take to get there?"

§5 in one box.
  • Types. $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y=y)$ are numbers; $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)$ are random variables (functions of $Y$). If a lowercase $y$ survives, it is a number.
  • Two laws. $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big]=\mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)=\mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)\big]+\operatorname{var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big)$ — within-group plus between-group. Class example: $70=\frac13(90)+\frac23(60)$ and $\frac{650}{3}=\frac{50}{3}+200$, the between term being $92.3077\%$ of the total.
  • Random sums. $\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[N]\mathbb{E}[X]$; $\operatorname{var}(Y)=\mathbb{E}[N]\operatorname{var}(X)+(\mathbb{E}[X])^2\operatorname{var}(N)$ (requires $X_i$ i.i.d. and independent of $N$). Worked check: $N\sim$ Poisson$(2.5)$, $X_i$ uniform on $\{1,2,3\}$ give $\mathbb{E}[Y]=5$, $\operatorname{var}(Y)=1.666667+10=11.666667$.
  • One process, two clocks. $n=t/\delta$, $p=\lambda\delta$, $np=\lambda t$. Counts: binomial $\to$ Poisson. Interarrivals: geometric $\to$ exponential. $k$th arrival: Pascal $\to$ Erlang$(k)$. Memorylessness on both sides. Fig. 5.1.
  • Merging / splitting. Poisson: rates add, next arrival from stream 1 with probability $\lambda_1/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$; split streams are independent. Bernoulli: $p=p_1+p_2-p_1p_2$ ($0.44$, not $0.5$, for $p_1=0.2,p_2=0.3$); split streams are dependent.
  • Random incidence. $f_L(\ell)=\ell f_T(\ell)/\mathbb{E}[T]$. Poisson: observed gap is Erlang$(2)$ with mean $2/\lambda$, twice the typical gap. Buses of $5$ or $10$ minutes: land in the long gap with probability $\frac23$, observed gap $\frac{25}{3}=8.333333$ min, expected wait $\frac{25}{6}=4.166667$ min, not $3.75$. Bernoulli side (derived in §5.2, nowhere else): $p_L(\ell)=\ell p^2(1-p)^{\ell-1}$, mean $(2-p)/p$.
