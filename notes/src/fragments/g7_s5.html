G7 asked one question in four ways. §1 asked how to judge a rule that turns data into a number, and answered with bias, variance and consistency; §2 asked how to build such a rule and answered with maximum likelihood; §3 asked the same question when the unknown is a straight line rather than a point, and answered with least squares; §4 asked how to choose between two stories about the world and answered with the likelihood ratio. This section puts all four on one page, sets them beside their Bayesian counterparts from G6, lists the five errors that cost the most marks and the most money, and then — because this is the last section of the last note — draws the whole of 6.041 as a single map.

5.1 The classical-inference cheatsheet

Fix the notation once; all six rows share it. $\theta$ is the unknown parameter: a constant, not a random variable, and this single decision is what separates every row below from every row of G6 §5.2. Because $\theta$ is not random it has no distribution, so the model is written $p_X(x;\theta)$ (discrete data) or $f_X(x;\theta)$ (continuous data) — one distribution per candidate value of $\theta$, with a semicolon rather than a conditioning bar (L23 slide 2, in bold on the slide: "These are NOT conditional probabilities"). $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are the observations, i.i.d. under each fixed $\theta$; $x_1,\dots,x_n$ are the numbers actually seen. $\hat\Theta_n=g(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ is an estimator — a random variable, because it is a function of the data — and $\hat\theta_n=g(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is the estimate, a number. $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ denote the mean and variance of a single $X_i$; $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF, and $z$ is the number with $\Phi(z)=1-\alpha/2$. Finally $\alpha$ is used twice in classical statistics, with different meanings, and you must keep them apart: $1-\alpha$ is a confidence level in row 3, while $\alpha$ alone is a false-rejection probability in row 6.

Read the table down the "what it guarantees" column and the classical character of the subject appears: every guarantee is a statement about a procedure repeated over hypothetical data sets, and every one carries the clause "for all $\theta$". That clause is the price of having no prior. Without a distribution on $\theta$ there is no way to average over it, so a claim can only be defended by holding for every value it might take.

Fig. 5.1 — Which classical method? The branch is decided by what you owe the reader, not by what the data look like (L23 slides 2–7, L24 slides 2–8, L25 slide 2; B&T §9.1–9.3).
Recipe — answering any classical-inference question (L23 slides 2, 4; B&T §9.1).
  1. Write the model with a semicolon. Before anything else, put down $p_X(x;\theta)$ or $f_X(x;\theta)$ and say what $\theta$ ranges over. If you find yourself writing $f_\Theta(\theta)$ you have silently gone Bayesian — decide deliberately, not by accident (Figure 5.1, footer).
  2. Name the deliverable. A point? A point with a range? A line? A decision? This selects the column of Figure 5.1 and nothing else does.
  3. Write the likelihood as a product. Under independence $p_X(x;\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n p_{X_i}(x_i;\theta)$; take logs immediately, so the product becomes a sum and the derivative is tractable.
  4. Check the estimator, do not assume it. Compute $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]$ (bias) and $\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_n)$ (spread) as functions of $\theta$. Consistency usually follows from the weak law applied to a sample average inside the estimator.
  5. Attach an interval, always. A point estimate with no spread hides whether the data did any work. Use $\hat\Theta_n\pm z\hat\sigma/\sqrt n$ and say which of the three $\sigma$-handling options you used (L23 slide 7).
  6. For a test, fix $\alpha$ first, then find $\xi$, then report $\beta$. The order is not negotiable: $\xi$ is chosen to meet the false-alarm budget, and the missed-detection rate is whatever that choice implies (L25 slide 2).
  7. State the assumption you are least sure of. Independence, normality, large $n$, "the model family contains the truth". Every guarantee above is conditional on these, and the guarantee is worth exactly what the assumption is worth.

5.2 Bayesian and classical: the master comparison

G6 and G7 compute with the same likelihoods and frequently produce the same arithmetic, yet they answer different questions. The difference is one modeling decision — whether $\theta$ gets a distribution — and everything else follows from it.

Line up the two columns and three matching pairs appear: not coincidences, but the same computation with the prior switched off. Each pair is worth working through numerically once, because the numbers make the relationship exact rather than suggestive.

Example 5.1 (matching pair 1: MAP becomes ML when the prior goes flat — L21 slide 5, L24 slide 2; B&T §8.2, §9.1). A coin is tossed $n$ times and $k$ heads appear; the bias is the unknown. Show that with a uniform prior the MAP estimate equals the ML estimate exactly, and measure how fast a non-uniform prior is washed out as $n$ grows. Use $k/n=0.3$ throughout.
Solution

The classical side. The number of heads $K$ is binomial, so the likelihood is $$p_K(k;\theta)=\binom{n}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k},\qquad 0\le\theta\le1 .$$ The binomial coefficient does not involve $\theta$, so it cannot affect the location of the maximum. Take logs — legitimate because $\log$ is strictly increasing, so it preserves the argmax: $$\ell(\theta)=\log\binom{n}{k}+k\log\theta+(n-k)\log(1-\theta).$$ Differentiate and set to zero: $$\frac{d\ell}{d\theta}=\frac{k}{\theta}-\frac{n-k}{1-\theta}=0 \ \Longrightarrow\ k(1-\theta)=(n-k)\theta \ \Longrightarrow\ k=n\theta \ \Longrightarrow\ \hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=\frac{k}{n}.$$ The second derivative $-k/\theta^2-(n-k)/(1-\theta)^2$ is negative wherever $0<\theta<1$, so this stationary point is the maximum, not a minimum. With $k=3$, $n=10$: $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=3/10=0.3$.

The Bayesian side with a uniform prior. Take $f_\Theta(\theta)=1$ on $[0,1]$, the Beta$(1,1)$ density. The posterior is proportional to prior $\times$ likelihood, and MAP maximizes that product: $$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\max_\theta\ 1\cdot\binom{n}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k} =\arg\max_\theta\ \theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}=\frac{k}{n}=0.3 .$$ Identical, and identical for a reason: a constant factor cannot move an argmax. Maximum likelihood is MAP with the prior deleted (or, equivalently, with a flat one).

A non-uniform prior, and how fast it dies. Take the Beta$(2,2)$ prior $f_\Theta(\theta)\propto\theta(1-\theta)$, a mild pull toward $\theta=1/2$. Multiplying by the likelihood gives a Beta$(2+k,\,2+n-k)$ posterior, whose mode is $$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\frac{(2+k)-1}{(2+k)+(2+n-k)-2}=\frac{k+1}{n+2},$$ the "add one success and one failure" rule. Evaluating at a fixed ratio $k/n=0.3$:

  • $n=10$, $k=3$: $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=4/12=0.333333$, versus ML $0.3$ — gap $0.033333$.
  • $n=100$, $k=30$: $31/102=0.303922$ — gap $0.003922$.
  • $n=1000$, $k=300$: $301/1002=0.300399$ — gap $0.000399$.

The gap falls by a factor of about ten each time $n$ rises by a factor of ten, i.e. like $1/n$: the prior contributes a fixed two pseudo-observations while the data contribute $n$ real ones. Reading: the Bayesian/classical dispute is loud when data are scarce and nearly empty when data are plentiful. It is at $n=10$, not $n=1000$, that you must decide whether you believe your prior.

Example 5.2 (matching pairs 2 and 3: the posterior mean becomes the sample mean, and the credible interval becomes the CI — L22 slide 8, L23 slide 6; B&T §8.3, §9.1). Ten i.i.d. observations $X_i\sim N(\theta,\sigma^2)$ with known $\sigma=2$ have sample mean $\bar x=5$. A Bayesian puts a $N(\mu_0,\sigma_0^2)$ prior on $\Theta$ with $\mu_0=0$. Compute the posterior mean and standard deviation for $\sigma_0=1,5,20$ and in the limit $\sigma_0\to\infty$, and compare the resulting $95\%$ credible interval with the classical $95\%$ confidence interval.
Solution

The posterior. For a normal prior and normal observations the posterior is normal with precision (reciprocal variance) equal to the sum of precisions, and mean equal to the precision-weighted average (G6 §4; L22 slide 8): $$\frac{1}{\sigma_{\text{post}}^2}=\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}+\frac{n}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \mu_{\text{post}}=\frac{\mu_0/\sigma_0^2+n\bar x/\sigma^2}{1/\sigma_0^2+n/\sigma^2} = w\mu_0+(1-w)\bar x,\quad w=\frac{1/\sigma_0^2}{1/\sigma_0^2+n/\sigma^2}.$$ Here $n=10$, $\sigma^2=4$, so the data precision is $n/\sigma^2=10/4=2.5$, and $\bar x=5$.

Sweeping the prior width.

  • $\sigma_0=1$: prior precision $1$, so $w=1/(1+2.5)=0.285714$ and $\mu_{\text{post}}=0.285714(0)+0.714286(5)=3.571429$, with $\sigma_{\text{post}}=\sqrt{1/3.5}=0.534522$. The prior has dragged the answer $28.6\%$ of the way back to $0$.
  • $\sigma_0=5$: prior precision $0.04$, $w=0.04/2.54=0.015748$, so $\mu_{\text{post}}=4.92126$ and $\sigma_{\text{post}}=\sqrt{1/2.54}=0.627456$.
  • $\sigma_0=20$: prior precision $0.0025$, $\mu_{\text{post}}=4.995005$, $\sigma_{\text{post}}=0.63214$.
  • $\sigma_0\to\infty$ (a "flat" prior): prior precision $\to0$, $w\to0$, so $\mu_{\text{post}}\to\bar x=5$ exactly and $\sigma_{\text{post}}\to\sqrt{\sigma^2/n} =2/\sqrt{10}=0.632456$.

Matching pair 2. In the limit the LMS estimate $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ is the sample mean, and its posterior standard deviation is exactly the classical standard error $\sigma/\sqrt n=0.632456$. The two frameworks have converged on the same number by different routes: the Bayesian by averaging over $\Theta$, the classicist by computing $\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_n)=\sigma^2/n$ over repeated data.

Matching pair 3. The classical $95\%$ confidence interval is $$\bar x\pm 1.959964\cdot\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}=5\pm1.959964(0.632456)=5\pm1.23959 =[3.76041,\ 6.23959],$$ and the $95\%$ credible interval under the $\sigma_0=5$ prior is $$4.92126\pm1.959964(0.627456)=[3.691469,\ 6.151051].$$ They differ by about $0.07$ at the low end and $0.09$ at the high end — visually the same interval, logically different objects. The classical one says: the procedure that produced me covers the true $\theta$ in $95\%$ of repetitions. The Bayesian one says: given these data and my prior, $\Theta$ lies in me with probability $0.95$. Only the second is a probability statement about $\theta$, and it is one only because a prior was supplied. Figure 5.2 draws the distinction: on the left the probability is spread along the $\theta$ axis; on the right $\theta$ is a single fixed vertical line and the probability is spread across the twenty intervals.

Interpretation — the same arithmetic, two different promises. Nothing above says one framework is correct. Both compute with $p_X(x;\theta)$; they differ only in what they average over. The Bayesian averages over the unknown with the data held at what you saw; the classicist averages over hypothetical data with the unknown held at whatever it truly is. Choose Bayesian when a prior is genuinely available and you must answer "what is $\theta$, given what I saw?"; choose classical when a prior would be an invention and you must answer "does my procedure behave well no matter what $\theta$ is?" The one thing you may never do is compute one and interpret it as the other — which is Gotcha 1 below.

5.3 The five gotchas that cost the most

Gotcha 1 — a confidence interval is not a probability statement about $\theta$. "$[3.76,\,6.24]$ is a $95\%$ confidence interval" does not mean $\mathbf{P}(3.76\le\theta\le6.24)=0.95$. Once the data are in, both endpoints are numbers and $\theta$ is a number; the statement "$\theta$ lies between them" is either true or false, with no probability attached, because in the classical framework $\theta$ has no distribution to put probability on. What is $0.95$ is the coverage of the procedure: with $\theta=5$, $\sigma=2$, $n=25$, the half-width is $1.959964(2)/\sqrt{25}=0.783986$, and over $400\,000$ simulated repetitions the interval covered $\theta$ a fraction $0.950283$ of the time ✓ — matching the nominal $0.95$ to three decimals. Figure 5.2 (right) shows twenty of those repetitions: nineteen cover, one (the sixteenth) misses entirely, and there is nothing pathological about the one that missed. Note also that a "$95\%$" CLT-based interval is only approximately $95\%$: the guarantee inherits the CLT's error, so at small $n$ or a skewed $X_i$ the true coverage can differ noticeably from the nominal one (L23 slide 6; B&T §9.1).
Gotcha 2 — the likelihood is not a probability distribution in $\theta$. $p_X(x;\theta)$ is a legitimate PMF/PDF in $x$ for each fixed $\theta$: it sums or integrates to $1$ over $x$. Viewed as a function of $\theta$ with $x$ fixed — which is exactly how maximum likelihood uses it — it is not normalized and generally cannot be. Concretely, for one observation from an exponential with parameter $\theta$, $f_X(x;\theta)=\theta e^{-\theta x}$, so $$\int_0^\infty \theta e^{-\theta x}\,d\theta=\frac{1}{x^2},$$ which equals $0.25$ when $x=2$ and $4.0$ when $x=0.5$ — a different number for every $x$, and never $1$ except by accident at $x=1$. For $n=3$ observations with $\sum_i x_i=6$ the likelihood is $\theta^3e^{-6\theta}$ and $\int_0^\infty\theta^3e^{-6\theta}d\theta=3!/6^4=0.00462963$. Three consequences. (i) You may not report "the probability that $\theta=\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}$" — no such quantity exists without a prior. (ii) You may scale the likelihood by any positive constant without changing $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}$, which is why dropping $\binom{n}{k}$ in Example 5.1 was legal. (iii) The ML estimate is invariant under reparametrization — if $\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=0.5$ then the ML estimate of $\theta^2$ is $0.25$, because the maximum of a function is carried along by any one-to-one relabeling — whereas the MAP estimate is not, since a change of variable drags a Jacobian into the prior density (L24 slide 2; B&T §9.1).
Gotcha 3 — the ML estimate of a variance is biased low, by exactly the factor $(n-1)/n$. For $X_i\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with both unknown, maximizing the likelihood gives $\hat\mu_{\text{ML}}=M_n$ and, in §1.6's symbols, $$\bar S_n^2=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-M_n)^2, \qquad \mathbb{E}\big[\bar S_n^2\big]=\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2 ,$$ because one degree of freedom has been spent estimating the mean: the deviations are taken from $M_n$, which is itself pulled toward the data, so they are systematically too small. The factor is $0.5$ at $n=2$, $0.8$ at $n=5$, $0.9$ at $n=10$ and $0.99$ at $n=100$ — a $50\%$ understatement of the variance with two data points. Monte Carlo with $\sigma^2=1$, $n=5$, two million replications returns $0.79961$ ✓ against the predicted $0.8$, while the $n-1$ version returns $0.999512$ ✓ against $1$. The repair is L23 slide 7's $\hat S_n^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i(X_i-\hat\Theta_n)^2$, which is unbiased for every $n$ ($\mathbb{E}[\hat S^2_n]=\sigma^2$; simulated at $n=25$, $\sigma^2=4$: $4.003219$ ✓ versus $3.843091$ for the ML version, itself matching the predicted $(24/25)(4)=3.84$ ✓). General lesson: ML optimizes fit, and unbiasedness is a separate property that it does not promise. Also note that unbiasedness is not preserved by nonlinear transformation — $\hat S_n^2$ is unbiased for $\sigma^2$ but $\hat S_n$ is not unbiased for $\sigma$, by Jensen's inequality (B&T §9.1).
Gotcha 4 — regression measures association; it cannot manufacture causation. The slope $\hat\theta_1$ is a statement about how $y$ and $x$ co-vary in the data you happen to have, and L24 slide 6 lists "sometimes misused to conclude causal relations" among the standard concerns. Concretely, let $Z\sim N(0,1)$ be an unobserved common cause and let $X=Z+U$, $Y=Z+V$ with $U,V\sim N(0,1)$ independent of $Z$ and of each other. Then $X$ has no effect at all on $Y$; yet $$\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=\operatorname{var}(Z)=1,\qquad\operatorname{var}(X)=1+1=2 \ \Longrightarrow\ \text{slope}=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)}{\operatorname{var}(X)}=0.5,$$ and $\rho=1/\sqrt{2\cdot2}=0.5$. Fitting a line to $200\,000$ simulated pairs returns slope $0.504693$ and $\rho=0.502511$ ✓ — a solid, highly significant, entirely non-causal relationship. Now intervene: set $X$ yourself, from an independent generator that ignores $Z$. The fitted slope collapses to $-0.001634$ ✓, i.e. zero. The regression coefficient changed because the question changed, from "how do they co-vary?" to "what happens if I move it?", and only a randomized intervention answers the second. Two companion traps from the same slide: multicollinearity (nearly proportional explanatory variables make individual coefficients wildly unstable even when the fit is fine) and heteroskedasticity (non-constant noise variance leaves $\hat\theta_1$ usable but ruins the reported standard errors) (L24 slide 6; B&T §9.2).
Gotcha 5 — the $\alpha/\beta$ trade-off has no free lunch at fixed $n$.

Shrinking the rejection region lowers $\alpha=\mathbf{P}(\text{reject }H_0;H_0)$ and simultaneously raises $\beta=\mathbf{P}(\text{accept }H_0;H_1)$; the threshold $\xi$ slides along a single curve and you may pick a point on it, not leave it. Take $H_0: X_i\sim N(0,1)$ against $H_1: X_i\sim N(1,1)$ with $n=10$ i.i.d. observations; the LRT reduces (L25 slide 3) to rejecting when $\sum_i X_i>\xi'$, and under $H_0$ the sum is $N(0,10)$, so $\xi'=z_{1-\alpha}\sqrt{10}$:

$\alpha$threshold $\xi'$$\beta$
$0.20$$2.6614$$0.010153$

Going from $\alpha=0.05$ to $\alpha=0.001$ — a fifty-fold tightening of the false-alarm rate — multiplies the missed-detection rate by $0.471283/0.064580=7.3$, from one miss in fifteen to nearly one in two. The only way to improve both at once is to buy more data: holding $\alpha=0.05$ and raising $n$ from $10$ to $40$ to $160$ drives $\beta$ from $6.458\times10^{-2}$ to $1.436\times10^{-6}$ to $1.823\times10^{-28}$. One last piece of vocabulary: "$H_0$ is not rejected at the $5\%$ level" means the data were not extreme enough, not that $H_0$ is true — in the L25 coin example, $S=472$ heads in $n=1000$ tosses gives a deviation $|472-500|=28$ against the threshold $1.959964\sqrt{1000(0.25)}=30.99\approx31$, so $H_0$ survives, even though the exact two-sided binomial probability of a deviation this large or larger is $0.081939$ — uncomfortably close to the line (L25 slide 5; worked in full in §4).

5.4 The whole course on one page

Seven notes, twenty-five lectures, and a single trajectory: outcomes $\to$ numbers $\to$ numbers that evolve $\to$ numbers that tell you about the model.

  • Phase 1 — models (G1). A sample space, three axioms, and two operations: additivity over disjoint pieces, and renormalization inside a conditioning event. Everything downstream is these two applied to a particular decomposition. Total probability and Bayes' rule appear here, and never leave (G1 §5).
  • Phase 2 — random variables (G2, G3). Replace outcomes by numbers, $X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$. Discrete first: PMFs, the four families, expectation, linearity, variance, and the fact that will matter most in the end, $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n$ (G2 §5). Then continuous: $p_X\to f_X$, $\sum_x\to\int dx$, and the identity table survives unchanged (G3 §5) — plus normal distributions, derived distributions, convolution, covariance and the correlation coefficient.
  • Phase 3 — processes in time (G4, G5). First conditioning becomes an object: $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ is a random variable, iterated expectations and the variance decomposition follow, and random sums are computed with them (G4 §5). Then two arrival processes, Bernoulli and Poisson, are one process on two clocks (G4 §2–§4). Finally Markov chains keep dependence but restrict it to "the present summarizes the past", and three questions — $n$-step, steady state, absorption — answer everything (G5 §5).
  • Phase 4 — what the data says (G6, G7). Averages concentrate: Markov, Chebyshev, the weak law, the CLT (G6 §5). Then inference, twice: with a prior (posterior, MAP, LMS, linear LMS) and without one (bias, consistency, confidence intervals, maximum likelihood, regression, the likelihood ratio test).

Across the top, four phases; in the middle, the seven notes and what each contributes; below them, the three threads that run end to end; at the bottom, the two ideas and the one fact everything reduces to. Sources: the six prior cheatsheets G1 §5–G6 §5 plus L23–L25.

The three threads in Figure 5.4 are worth stating in words, because each is the same idea wearing four costumes.

  1. Condition and renormalize. $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B)=\mathbf{P}(A\cap B)/\mathbf{P}(B)$ (G1 §2) becomes the conditional PDF $f_{X\mid Y}$ (G3 §2), becomes the posterior $f_{\Theta\mid X}(\theta\mid x)=f_\Theta(\theta)f_{X\mid\Theta}(x\mid\theta)/f_X(x)$ (G6 §3), becomes — with the prior and the denominator both deleted, because neither survives when $\theta$ is a constant — the likelihood ratio $p_X(x;H_1)/p_X(x;H_0)$ (G7 §4). The Bayes denominator and the LRT threshold play the same structural role: they are the thing that does not depend on the hypothesis.
  2. Averages concentrate. $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n$ (G2 §3) is a two-line consequence of independence; feed it to Chebyshev and you get the weak law, add the CLT and you get a number instead of a bound (G6 §1–§2); and those two results are precisely what makes $\hat\Theta_n$ consistent and what puts the $1.96\hat\sigma/\sqrt n$ in every confidence interval and every significance threshold in G7. One variance formula, three chapters.
  3. Conditional expectation as an object. $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ as a random variable (G4 §1) is what makes $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ an estimator rather than a number (G6 §4) — and the least-squares slope of G7 §3 is its linear version with sample moments substituted for population ones.
Interpretation — you learned three things, then applied them twenty times. Additivity over a partition (total probability $\to$ total expectation $\to$ balance equations $\to$ the Bayes denominator), renormalization inside a conditioning event (conditional probability $\to$ conditional densities $\to$ posteriors $\to$ likelihood ratios), and the fact that averaging $n$ independent things divides the variance by $n$. Every formula in seven cheatsheets is one of those three applied to a particular decomposition. If you remember nothing else in five years, remember which decomposition to make — the algebra can be re-derived, the modeling instinct cannot be looked up.

5.5 Where to go next

6.041 stops at the point where each of its four phases turns into a subject of its own, and B&T §9.4–9.5 and L25 slide 8 ("What else is there?") point at all four doors. Stochastic processes continues Phase 3: continuous-time Markov chains and queueing (the Poisson process of G4 §3–§4 with servers attached), martingales and the optional stopping theorem, Brownian motion as the continuous-time limit of the random walks of G5, and renewal theory as the mature version of the random-incidence paradox of G4 §4.4–§4.5. Statistics continues Phase 4 and fills in what this course could only sketch: composite hypotheses and the generalized likelihood ratio, the chi-square and Kolmogorov–Smirnov goodness-of-fit tests that L25 slides 6–7 introduce without derivation (B&T §9.4), the Cramér–Rao bound that says how small an unbiased estimator's variance can possibly be, multiple regression and model selection, and the resampling methods that replace the CLT approximation with brute-force simulation. Information theory reinterprets the likelihood ratio: $\log\frac{p_X(x;H_1)}{p_X(x;H_0)}$ accumulated over i.i.d. data grows at a rate equal to a Kullback–Leibler divergence, which is why $\beta$ in Figure 5.3 falls geometrically in $n$ — and the same quantity governs how many bits a source needs and how fast a channel can carry them. Beyond those three, the modern descendants are visible in the same slide: signal processing (efficient and recursive estimation — the Kalman filter is linear LMS, G6 §4, run once per time step), machine learning (regression with many variables plus model-complexity selection), and causal inference (Gotcha 4, taken seriously). All of them assume, without re-teaching, exactly what is in Figure 5.4.

G7 in twelve lines.
  • Classical framing: $\theta$ is an unknown constant; the model is $p_X(x;\theta)$, a family, not a conditional (L23 slide 2; B&T §9.1).
  • Estimator properties must hold for all $\theta$: unbiased $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_n]=\theta$; consistent $\hat\Theta_n\to\theta$ in probability; $\mathrm{MSE}=\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_n)+\text{bias}^2$.
  • Sample mean: unbiased, $\operatorname{var}=\sigma^2/n$, consistent by the WLLN, and the ML estimate when $X_i\sim N(\theta,\sigma^2)$.
  • CI: $\hat\Theta_n\pm z\hat\sigma/\sqrt n$ with $z=1.644854/1.959964/2.575829$ at $90/95/99\%$; handle $\sigma$ by bound, ad hoc estimate, or $\hat S_n^2$ (L23 slide 7).
  • ML: maximize $\log p_X(x;\theta)=\sum_i\log p_{X_i}(x_i;\theta)$; it is MAP with the prior deleted — uniform prior gives $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\hat\theta_{\text{ML}}=k/n$ exactly (Example 5.1).
  • Regression: $\hat\theta_1=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)/\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2$, $\hat\theta_0=\bar y-\hat\theta_1\bar x$; equals ML under i.i.d. normal noise (L24 slide 4).
  • LRT: reject $H_0$ iff $p_X(x;H_1)/p_X(x;H_0)>\xi$; fix $\alpha$, solve for $\xi$, then read off $\beta$ (L24 slides 7–8, L25 slide 2).
  • Matching pairs: MAP $\leftrightarrow$ ML; LMS $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$ $\leftrightarrow$ sample mean ($5.0$ in the flat-prior limit, posterior sd $0.632456=\sigma/\sqrt n$); credible interval $[3.691469,6.151051]$ $\leftrightarrow$ CI $[3.76041,6.23959]$ (Example 5.2).
  • Gotcha 1: a CI is a claim about the procedure — simulated coverage $0.950283$ over $400\,000$ repetitions, nineteen of twenty in Figure 5.2.
  • Gotcha 2: the likelihood is not a density in $\theta$ — $\int_0^\infty\theta e^{-2\theta}d\theta=0.25$, not $1$. Gotcha 3: $\mathbb{E}[\bar S_n^2]=\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$ ($0.8$ at $n=5$; simulated $0.79961$).
  • Gotcha 4: a pure confounder gives slope $0.5$ and $\rho=0.5$ with zero causal effect; under intervention the slope is $-0.001634\approx0$. Gotcha 5: $\alpha=0.05\to0.001$ raises $\beta$ from $0.064580$ to $0.471283$; only more data moves the curve.
  • The arc: outcomes $\to$ numbers $\to$ numbers that evolve $\to$ numbers that tell you about the model, held together by additivity over a partition, renormalization inside a conditioning event, and $\operatorname{var}(M_n)=\sigma^2/n$ (Figure 5.4).
