Everything so far in G7 has estimated a parameter of one distribution: the mean of the $X_i$ in §1, the $\theta$ inside a likelihood $p_X(x;\theta)$ in §2. Regression asks a different question. You have pairs — a voltage and a current, a year and a tilt, hours of study and an exam score — and you want a rule that turns the first number into a prediction of the second. The astonishing thing, and the reason regression is the single most used procedure in all of applied statistics, is that this apparently much harder problem collapses into two lines of algebra and a closed-form answer.

Three warnings up front, because they shape everything below. First, we are firmly in the classical world of §0: the coefficients $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$ are unknown constants, not random variables, so there is no prior and no posterior — contrast G6 §3–§4, where $\Theta$ was random. Second, the $x_i$ are not random either; they are numbers you chose or recorded exactly. Only the $y_i$ carry noise. Third, least squares is at bottom a curve-fitting recipe that needs no probability at all — the probability enters afterwards, to tell you how much to trust the answer.

3.1 The setup: what is fixed, what is random, and what "$\approx$" means

Definition (the regression problem; L24 slide 3, B&T §9.2). You are given $n$ data pairs $$(x_1,y_1),\ (x_2,y_2),\ \ldots,\ (x_n,y_n),$$ where $x_i$ is the explanatory variable (also called the regressor, the independent variable, or the input) for the $i$-th observation and $y_i$ is the response (the dependent variable, the output). You postulate the model $$y \approx \theta_0 + \theta_1 x,$$ in which $\theta_0$ is an unknown constant called the intercept — the value the line predicts at $x=0$ — and $\theta_1$ is an unknown constant called the slope — the change in the predicted $y$ per unit change in $x$. Fitting the model means producing numbers $\hat\theta_0$ and $\hat\theta_1$ from the data. The line $y=\hat\theta_0+\hat\theta_1 x$ is the fitted or regression line.

The symbol "$\approx$" is doing real work and deserves unpacking. It does not say that the true relationship between the two quantities is a straight line. It says: among all straight lines, find the one that comes closest to these points, in a sense to be made precise in §3.2. B&T (§9.2, p. 477) put it exactly this way: "the postulated linear model may or may not be true… The linear least squares approach aims at finding the best possible linear model, and involves an implicit hypothesis that a linear model is valid."

Definition (residual; L24 slide 3). Given any candidate pair of coefficients $(\theta_0,\theta_1)$, the residual at the $i$-th data point is the vertical gap between the observed response and the value the line predicts there: $$\tilde y_i \;=\; y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1 x_i .$$ It is positive when the point lies above the line and negative when it lies below. Evaluated at the fitted coefficients we write $r_i=y_i-\hat\theta_0-\hat\theta_1 x_i$ and call these the residuals.
Fig. 3.1 — the picture on L24 slide 3, redrawn. The quantity least squares minimizes is the sum of the squares of the dashed vertical gaps. Note that the gaps are measured vertically, not perpendicular to the line: $y$ is the thing being predicted and $x$ is taken as exact, so errors are charged in the $y$ direction only.
Interpretation (what is random here). Three different objects are easy to confuse. (i) The parameters $\theta_0,\theta_1$: unknown constants — the thing we want. (ii) The estimates $\hat\theta_0,\hat\theta_1$: specific numbers computed from the data in hand. (iii) The estimators $\hat\Theta_0,\hat\Theta_1$: the same formulas regarded as random variables, because they are functions of the random responses $Y_1,\ldots,Y_n$. Sections 3.2–3.4 never leave level (ii) — they are pure algebra and need no probability model at all. Section 3.5 introduces a probability model for the $Y_i$ and moves to level (iii), which is what makes standard errors and confidence intervals possible.
Gotcha. The $x_i$ are treated as exact, non-random constants. If your $x$ values are themselves noisy measurements — you weighed the samples with a sloppy balance — then least squares is systematically biased toward a slope that is too shallow ("attenuation"), and no amount of data fixes it. This is a modelling assumption, not a technicality; B&T's justification (b), reviewed in §3.4, is the one case where random $x_i$ are legitimate, and even there the formulas are only being reinterpreted, not rederived.
Practice 3.1. Suppose all the $x_i$ happen to be equal, say $x_i=c$ for every $i$. What goes wrong, and what is the geometric picture?
Solution

The denominator in the slope formula of §3.2 is $\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2$. If every $x_i$ equals $c$ then $\bar x = c$ and every term is $0$, so the denominator is $0$ and $\hat\theta_1$ is undefined. Geometrically: all the data sit on one vertical line, and every straight line that passes through the point $(c,\bar y)$ gives exactly the same sum of squared residuals, namely $\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2$. Check that directly: with $x_i=c$, the criterion is $\sum_i(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1 c)^2$, which depends on $(\theta_0,\theta_1)$ only through the single number $a=\theta_0+\theta_1 c$; minimizing over $a$ gives $a=\bar y$, and infinitely many $(\theta_0,\theta_1)$ produce that same $a$. So the minimizer exists but is not unique. The moral: you can only learn a slope if your $x$ values vary, and the more they vary — the larger $\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2$ — the sharper the slope estimate. Section 3.5 makes that precise: $\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_1)=\sigma^2/\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2$.

Practice 3.2. Without redoing the minimization, predict what happens to $\hat\theta_0$ and $\hat\theta_1$ if (a) every $y_i$ is increased by $10$; (b) every $x_i$ is doubled. Then verify on the dataset of Example 3.1 below, whose fit is $\hat\theta_0=55,\ \hat\theta_1=5$.
Solution

(a) Shifting the responses shifts the whole cloud vertically by $10$, so the best line shifts vertically by $10$: the slope is unchanged and the intercept rises by $10$. Algebraically, $\bar y$ becomes $\bar y+10$ while every $y_i-\bar y$ is unchanged, so $S_{xy}$ and hence $\hat\theta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}$ are unchanged, and $\hat\theta_0=(\bar y+10)-\hat\theta_1\bar x$ rises by $10$. Computation confirms $\hat\theta_1=5$, $\hat\theta_0=65$ (P32_shift_th1, P32_shift_th0).

(b) Doubling the $x_i$ stretches the horizontal axis by $2$, so the same cloud is described by a line of half the slope, with the intercept unchanged (the point $x=0$ is still $x=0$). Algebraically $\bar x\mapsto 2\bar x$, each $x_i-\bar x\mapsto 2(x_i-\bar x)$, hence $S_{xy}\mapsto 2S_{xy}=420$ and $S_{xx}\mapsto 4S_{xx}=168$ (P32_double_Sxy, P32_double_Sxx), so $\hat\theta_1\mapsto \tfrac{2}{4}\hat\theta_1=5/2$ and $\hat\theta_0=\bar y-\tfrac{5}{2}(9)=77.5-22.5=55$, unchanged (P32_double_th1, P32_double_th0). This is why regression coefficients always carry units: $\hat\theta_1$ here is "points of exam score per hour", and it must change when you switch from hours to half-hours.

3.2 The least-squares criterion and the normal equations

Definition (least squares; L24 slide 3, equation $(*)$). The least-squares estimates are the minimizers of the sum of squared residuals $$S(\theta_0,\theta_1)\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1 x_i\big)^2 , \qquad (\hat\theta_0,\hat\theta_1)=\arg\min_{\theta_0,\theta_1} S(\theta_0,\theta_1).$$ Here $S$ is a function of the two candidate coefficients only — the data $(x_i,y_i)$ are fixed numbers inside it. The minimized value $S(\hat\theta_0,\hat\theta_1)$ is written $\mathrm{SSE}$ ("error sum of squares").

Why squares? Three reasons, in increasing order of depth. (i) Squaring makes positive and negative misses count alike, whereas $\sum_i(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)$ can be driven to zero by lines that miss everything. (ii) $S$ is a smooth quadratic, so calculus gives a closed form — minimizing $\sum_i|y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i|$ instead is a perfectly sensible criterion but has no closed-form solution. (iii) Under a normal noise model the least-squares answer is the maximum likelihood answer, which is L24 slide 3's second half and our §3.5.

Property (the normal equations; L24 slide 4, "set derivatives to zero"). The minimizers satisfy the two simultaneous linear equations $$(\mathrm{E}_0)\quad n\,\theta_0+\Big(\sum_i x_i\Big)\theta_1=\sum_i y_i, \qquad (\mathrm{E}_1)\quad \Big(\sum_i x_i\Big)\theta_0+\Big(\sum_i x_i^2\Big)\theta_1=\sum_i x_iy_i .$$ Provided $\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2>0$, they have the unique solution $$\boxed{\ \hat\theta_1=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2}, \qquad \hat\theta_0=\bar y-\hat\theta_1\bar x\ }$$ where $\bar x=\frac1n\sum_i x_i$ and $\bar y=\frac1n\sum_i y_i$ are the two sample means.

Derivation, every step. The slide says only "set derivatives to zero"; here is the whole computation.

Step 1 — the first partial derivative. Differentiate $S$ with respect to $\theta_0$, treating $\theta_1$ as fixed. Each summand is $(\,\cdot\,)^2$ with inner function $y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i$ whose derivative in $\theta_0$ is $-1$, so by the chain rule $$\frac{\partial S}{\partial\theta_0} =\sum_{i=1}^n 2\big(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i\big)\cdot(-1) =-2\sum_{i=1}^n\big(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i\big).$$ Setting this to zero and dividing by $-2$: $$\sum_{i=1}^n\big(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i\big)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_i y_i-n\theta_0-\theta_1\sum_i x_i=0,$$ which is exactly $(\mathrm{E}_0)$ — the sum of $n$ copies of the constant $\theta_0$ is $n\theta_0$. Dividing $(\mathrm{E}_0)$ through by $n$ gives the memorable form $$\bar y=\theta_0+\theta_1\bar x \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \theta_0=\bar y-\theta_1\bar x. \tag{3.1}$$ Read (3.1) as a statement about the picture: whatever the slope turns out to be, the fitted line must pass through the centroid $(\bar x,\bar y)$ of the data cloud.

Step 2 — the second partial derivative. Now differentiate with respect to $\theta_1$; the inner derivative is $-x_i$, so $$\frac{\partial S}{\partial\theta_1} =\sum_{i=1}^n 2\big(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i\big)\cdot(-x_i) =-2\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\big(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i\big).$$ Setting to zero and dividing by $-2$, $$\sum_i x_iy_i-\theta_0\sum_i x_i-\theta_1\sum_i x_i^2=0,$$ which is $(\mathrm{E}_1)$.

Step 3 — eliminate $\theta_0$. Substitute (3.1) into $(\mathrm{E}_1)$, using $\sum_i x_i=n\bar x$: $$\sum_i x_iy_i-\big(\bar y-\theta_1\bar x\big)\,n\bar x-\theta_1\sum_i x_i^2=0 .$$ Expand the middle product and collect the two $\theta_1$ terms on one side: $$\sum_i x_iy_i-n\bar x\bar y \;=\; \theta_1\Big(\sum_i x_i^2-n\bar x^{\,2}\Big). \tag{3.2}$$

Step 4 — recognize both sides. Two standard identities turn (3.2) into the boxed formula. For the left side, $$\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)=\sum_i\big(x_iy_i-x_i\bar y-\bar xy_i+\bar x\bar y\big) =\sum_i x_iy_i-\bar y\underbrace{\sum_i x_i}_{n\bar x}-\bar x\underbrace{\sum_i y_i}_{n\bar y}+n\bar x\bar y =\sum_i x_iy_i-n\bar x\bar y,$$ the last step because $-n\bar x\bar y-n\bar x\bar y+n\bar x\bar y=-n\bar x\bar y$. Taking $y=x$ in that identity gives the right side: $\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2=\sum_i x_i^2-n\bar x^{\,2}$. So (3.2) reads $$\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)=\theta_1\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2 ,$$ and dividing by $\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2$ — legitimate exactly when it is nonzero, i.e. when the $x_i$ are not all equal (Practice 3.1) — gives $\hat\theta_1$. Then (3.1) gives $\hat\theta_0$. $\blacksquare$

Notation. From here on we abbreviate the three sums that do all the work: $$S_{xx}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2,\qquad S_{xy}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y),\qquad S_{yy}=\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2 .$$ $S_{xx}$ measures how spread out the inputs are, $S_{yy}$ how spread out the responses are, and $S_{xy}$ how much they move together. The whole of simple linear regression is these three numbers plus $n$, $\bar x$, $\bar y$. In this notation $\hat\theta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}$.

Step 5 — is it really a minimum? The slide does not check, so we will. $S$ is a quadratic in $(\theta_0,\theta_1)$, and its matrix of second derivatives (the Hessian) is constant: $$\frac{\partial^2S}{\partial\theta_0^2}=2n,\qquad \frac{\partial^2S}{\partial\theta_1^2}=2\sum_i x_i^2,\qquad \frac{\partial^2S}{\partial\theta_0\partial\theta_1}=2\sum_i x_i, \qquad H=2\begin{pmatrix} n & \sum_i x_i\\[2pt] \sum_i x_i & \sum_i x_i^2\end{pmatrix}.$$ Its determinant is $$\det H=4\Big(n\sum_i x_i^2-\big(\textstyle\sum_i x_i\big)^2\Big) =4n\Big(\sum_i x_i^2-n\bar x^{\,2}\Big)=4n\,S_{xx},$$ using $\sum_i x_i=n\bar x$ and then the Step-4 identity. Since $n>0$ and $S_{xx}>0$, we have $\det H>0$; and the leading entry $2n>0$. A symmetric $2\times2$ matrix with positive leading entry and positive determinant is positive definite, so $S$ is a strictly convex paraboloid and the stationary point is its unique global minimum. For the dataset of Example 3.1, $\det H=1344$ and $4nS_{xx}=4\cdot8\cdot42=1344$ (Hessian_det), as it must be.

Practice 3.3 (regression through the origin). Some models must predict $y=0$ when $x=0$ — an extension proportional to a force, say. Then the criterion is $\min_{\theta_1}\sum_i(y_i-\theta_1x_i)^2$ with no intercept. Derive $\hat\theta_1$, apply it to Example 3.1's data, and comment.
Solution

One unknown, so one derivative: $$\frac{d}{d\theta_1}\sum_i(y_i-\theta_1x_i)^2=\sum_i 2(y_i-\theta_1x_i)(-x_i)=0 \ \Longrightarrow\ \sum_i x_iy_i=\theta_1\sum_i x_i^2 \ \Longrightarrow\ \hat\theta_1=\frac{\sum_i x_iy_i}{\sum_i x_i^2},$$ the second derivative being $2\sum_i x_i^2>0$, so it is a minimum. Note that the formula uses raw sums, not deviations from the means: there is no $\bar x$ or $\bar y$ anywhere, because the line is no longer forced through the centroid.

On Example 3.1's data, $\sum_i x_iy_i=3000$ and $\sum_i x_i^2=204$, so $\hat\theta_1=3000/204=250/17=14.705882$ (P34_theta_origin) and the sum of squared residuals is $85720/17=5042.35$ (P34_SSE_origin) — 84.04 times the $\mathrm{SSE}=60$ of the two-parameter fit (P34_SSE_ratio). Forcing the line through the origin here is a disaster, because the data live around $x\in[1,8]$ with $y\approx 60$–$100$ and a line through $(0,0)$ can only reach that cloud by tilting absurdly steeply. Lesson: only drop the intercept when the physics genuinely demands it, and never merely because $\hat\theta_0$ "looks insignificant".

3.3 What the fitted line always satisfies: residuals, and $R^2$

The two normal equations are not merely a step in the derivation; read at the solution they are two identities that the residuals of any least-squares fit obey, whatever the data.

Property (residual identities). With $r_i=y_i-\hat\theta_0-\hat\theta_1x_i$: $$\text{(i) } \sum_{i=1}^n r_i=0, \qquad \text{(ii) } \sum_{i=1}^n x_ir_i=0, \qquad \text{(iii) } \sum_{i=1}^n \hat y_i r_i=0,\qquad \text{(iv) the line passes through }(\bar x,\bar y).$$

Derivation. (i) and (ii) are $(\mathrm{E}_0)$ and $(\mathrm{E}_1)$ rewritten: Step 1 showed $\partial S/\partial\theta_0=0$ is $\sum_i(y_i-\hat\theta_0-\hat\theta_1x_i)=0$, and Step 2 showed $\partial S/\partial\theta_1=0$ is $\sum_i x_i(y_i-\hat\theta_0-\hat\theta_1x_i)=0$. (iii) follows by linearity: $\sum_i \hat y_ir_i=\sum_i(\hat\theta_0+\hat\theta_1x_i)r_i =\hat\theta_0\sum_i r_i+\hat\theta_1\sum_i x_ir_i=\hat\theta_0\cdot0+\hat\theta_1\cdot0=0$. (iv) is (3.1): dividing (i) by $n$ gives $\bar y-\hat\theta_0-\hat\theta_1\bar x=0$. $\blacksquare$

Interpretation (why this is the right notion of "best"). Identities (i)–(iii) say the residual vector $r=(r_1,\ldots,r_n)$ is orthogonal to the all-ones vector and to the vector $(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$, hence to every vector of the form $a\mathbf{1}+bx$ — that is, to the entire two-dimensional plane of fitted values that lines can produce. Least squares is a projection: it drops a perpendicular from the data vector $y$ onto that plane. This is the same orthogonality principle that G6 §4 established for the linear LMS estimator, where the error $\hat\Theta_L-\Theta$ was uncorrelated with the constant $1$ and with the observation $X$ — exactly the two directions $\mathbf{1}$ and $x$ here — and the sample-average inner product replaces the expectation. What does not carry over is orthogonality to every function of the observation: that is the property of the full LMS estimator $\mathbb{E}[\Theta\mid X]$, and least squares emphatically does not deliver it — residuals can be perfectly predictable from a nonlinear function of $x$, which is exactly the phenomenon of Practice 3.4. Nothing that a line can express is left in the residuals — which is precisely why a pattern in a residual plot is evidence that your model is wrong (§3.8).
Property (variance decomposition and $R^2$). Write $\mathrm{SST}=S_{yy}=\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2$ (total), $\mathrm{SSR}=\sum_i(\hat y_i-\bar y)^2$ (explained) and $\mathrm{SSE}=\sum_i r_i^2$ (residual). Then $$\mathrm{SST}=\mathrm{SSR}+\mathrm{SSE},\qquad \mathrm{SSR}=\hat\theta_1^2S_{xx} =\frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}},$$ and the coefficient of determination $$R^2\;=\;\frac{\mathrm{SSR}}{\mathrm{SST}}\;=\;1-\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{\mathrm{SST}} \;=\;\frac{S_{xy}^2}{S_{xx}S_{yy}}\;=\;\hat\rho^{\,2}$$ is the square of the sample correlation coefficient $\hat\rho=S_{xy}/\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}$, and lies in $[0,1]$.

Derivation. Split each deviation from the mean into an explained part and a residual, using $\bar y=\hat\theta_0+\hat\theta_1\bar x$ from (iv): $$y_i-\bar y=(\hat y_i-\bar y)+(y_i-\hat y_i)=(\hat y_i-\bar y)+r_i .$$ Square and sum: $$\mathrm{SST}=\sum_i(\hat y_i-\bar y)^2+2\sum_i(\hat y_i-\bar y)r_i+\sum_i r_i^2 .$$ The cross term vanishes: $\sum_i(\hat y_i-\bar y)r_i=\sum_i\hat y_ir_i-\bar y\sum_ir_i=0-0=0$ by (iii) and (i). That leaves $\mathrm{SST}=\mathrm{SSR}+\mathrm{SSE}$. For the second formula, use (iv) to write $\hat y_i-\bar y=\hat\theta_1(x_i-\bar x)$, so $\mathrm{SSR}=\hat\theta_1^2\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2=\hat\theta_1^2S_{xx}=(S_{xy}/S_{xx})^2S_{xx} =S_{xy}^2/S_{xx}$. Dividing by $\mathrm{SST}=S_{yy}$ gives $R^2=S_{xy}^2/(S_{xx}S_{yy})$, which is $\hat\rho^{\,2}$ by definition. Both $\mathrm{SSR}$ and $\mathrm{SSE}$ are sums of squares, hence nonnegative, and they add to $\mathrm{SST}$; therefore $0\le R^2\le1$. $\blacksquare$

For Example 3.1. $\mathrm{SSR}=\hat\theta_1^2S_{xx}=25\cdot42=1050$ and $\mathrm{SSE}=60$, and indeed $1050+60=1110=S_{yy}$ (SSR, SST_decomp_check). Hence $$R^2=\frac{1050}{1110}=\frac{35}{37}=0.945946,\qquad \hat\rho=\frac{210}{\sqrt{42\cdot1110}}=0.972598$$ (R2_exact, R2, r_corr; scipy.stats.linregress reports $r^2=0.945945945946$, scipy_rvalue2). Cross-check with the correlation form: $210^2/(42\cdot1110)=44100/46620=35/37$. $\checkmark$

Practice 3.4. A colleague reports $R^2=0$ for a fit and concludes "$x$ tells us nothing about $y$". Give a dataset where that conclusion is flatly wrong.
Solution

Take $x_i$ symmetric about $0$ — say $x=-2,-1,0,1,2$ — and $y_i=x_i^2$, i.e. $y=4,1,0,1,4$. Then $\bar x=0$ and $$S_{xy}=\sum_i(x_i-0)(y_i-\bar y)=\sum_i x_iy_i-\bar y\sum_ix_i=\sum_i x_i^3-0 =(-8)+(-1)+0+1+8=0,$$ so $\hat\theta_1=0$, $\hat\theta_0=\bar y=2$, and $R^2=S_{xy}^2/(S_{xx}S_{yy})=0$. Yet $x$ determines $y$ exactly. $R^2$ measures the strength of the linear association only; it is blind to any relation that is symmetric about $\bar x$. This is precisely the phenomenon G6 §4 flagged for the linear LMS estimator, where $\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)=0$ made the best linear estimator throw the data away even though $X$ determined $\Theta$. The fix is the same in both places: change the variable (fit $y\approx\theta_0+\theta_1h(x)$ with $h(x)=x^2$, §3.7) — and always look at the scatter plot before trusting a single summary number.

3.4 Where the formulas come from: the linear LMS reading

L24 slide 4 spends its second half on the "interpretation of the form of the solution", and this is the bridge back to G6. The claim is that the least-squares formulas are not an accident of calculus: they are the sample version of the best linear estimator you already derived in a Bayesian setting.

Property (population version; L24 slide 4). Let $X$ and $Y$ be random variables related by $$Y=\theta_0+\theta_1X+W,$$ where $W$ is independent of $X$ with $\mathbb{E}[W]=0$. Then $$\theta_1=\frac{\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)}{\operatorname{var}(X)},\qquad \theta_0=\mathbb{E}[Y]-\theta_1\mathbb{E}[X].$$
Example 3.2 (L24 slide 4 — the slide's own [UNSOLVED EXAMPLE], "check that…"). Verify the two displayed formulas.
Solution

The covariance. Use bilinearity of covariance (G3 §4): for constants $a,b,c$ and random variables $U,V$, $\operatorname{cov}(X,\,a+bU+cV)=b\operatorname{cov}(X,U)+c\operatorname{cov}(X,V)$, because adding a constant to an argument does not change a covariance and constants factor out. Applying it with $Y=\theta_0+\theta_1X+W$: $$\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=\operatorname{cov}(X,\theta_0)+\theta_1\operatorname{cov}(X,X) +\operatorname{cov}(X,W) =0+\theta_1\operatorname{var}(X)+\operatorname{cov}(X,W).$$ Here $\operatorname{cov}(X,\theta_0)=0$ because $\theta_0$ is a constant, and $\operatorname{cov}(X,X)=\operatorname{var}(X)$ by definition.

The noise term drops out. $W$ is independent of $X$, so $\mathbb{E}[XW]=\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[W]$ (G3 §4), and therefore $$\operatorname{cov}(X,W)=\mathbb{E}[XW]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[W] =\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[W]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[W]=0 .$$ (Zero mean alone is not enough here — independence is what is used; but note that once $\operatorname{cov}(X,W)=0$ holds, that is all the argument needs, so uncorrelatedness would suffice.) Hence $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=\theta_1\operatorname{var}(X)$, and dividing by $\operatorname{var}(X)>0$ gives $\theta_1=\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)/\operatorname{var}(X)$, which is the first claim.

The intercept. Take expectations across the model and use linearity (G2 §2): $$\mathbb{E}[Y]=\mathbb{E}[\theta_0]+\theta_1\mathbb{E}[X]+\mathbb{E}[W] =\theta_0+\theta_1\mathbb{E}[X]+0,$$ so $\theta_0=\mathbb{E}[Y]-\theta_1\mathbb{E}[X]$. $\blacksquare$

Numerical confirmation with deliberately non-normal ingredients (MC_*, $4\times10^6$ samples): take $X$ exponential with mean $3$ (so $\operatorname{var}(X)=9$), $W$ Laplace with mean $0$ and scale $2$, independent, and $\theta_0=55,\theta_1=5$. Simulation gives $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=44.945$, $\operatorname{var}(X)=8.989$, ratio $4.99985\approx5=\theta_1$, and $\mathbb{E}[Y]-\theta_1\mathbb{E}[X]=55.0015\approx55=\theta_0$. Neither variable is normal and neither needs to be: the property is a covariance identity, not a distributional one.

Interpretation (the same formula twice; L24 slide 4, G6 §4, B&T §8.4). G6 §4 derived the linear LMS estimator of $\Theta$ given $X$, $$\hat\Theta_L=\mathbb{E}[\Theta]+\frac{\operatorname{cov}(\Theta,X)}{\operatorname{var}(X)} \big(X-\mathbb{E}[X]\big),$$ the best straight-line predictor in mean squared error. Rewrite the regression line the same way, using $\hat\theta_0=\bar y-\hat\theta_1\bar x$: $$\hat y(x)=\hat\theta_0+\hat\theta_1x=\bar y+\hat\theta_1(x-\bar x) =\bar y+\frac{S_{xy}/n}{S_{xx}/n}(x-\bar x).$$ The two expressions are identical in form, with $$\mathbb{E}[Y]\to\bar y,\qquad \mathbb{E}[X]\to\bar x,\qquad \operatorname{cov}(X,Y)\to\frac{S_{xy}}{n},\qquad \operatorname{var}(X)\to\frac{S_{xx}}{n}.$$ The right-hand quantities are the sample moments — exactly the natural estimates of the left-hand population moments, and exactly what the slide means by "the solution formula for $\hat\theta_1$ uses natural estimates of the variance and covariance". For Example 3.1 the sample covariance is $S_{xy}/n=210/8=26.25$ and the sample variance of $x$ is $S_{xx}/n=42/8=5.25$, whose ratio is $26.25/5.25=5=\hat\theta_1$ (samp_cov, samp_var_x, samp_cov_over_var). The $n$'s cancel, which is why they never appear in the boxed formula.

B&T (§9.2, pp. 478–480) turn this observation into two of their three formal justifications of least squares, both worth knowing because they assume much less than the normal model of §3.5.

  • Justification (b): approximate linear LMS, no linear model assumed. Suppose the pairs $(X_i,Y_i)$ are i.i.d. draws from some unknown joint distribution, and you want to predict the $Y$ of a fresh independent pair $(X_0,Y_0)$ from its $X$ by a linear rule $\hat Y_0=\theta_0+\theta_1X_0$. G6 §4 says the optimal coefficients are $\theta_1=\operatorname{cov}(X_0,Y_0)/\operatorname{var}(X_0)$ and $\theta_0=\mathbb{E}[Y_0]-\theta_1\mathbb{E}[X_0]$. You do not know those four moments, so you plug in their sample counterparts — and out come the regression formulas. Nothing in this argument assumes the true relation is linear; it only assumes you have decided to use a line.
  • Justification (c): approximate LMS under a linear model, via the WLLN. If the pairs are i.i.d. and genuinely satisfy $Y_i=\theta_0+\theta_1X_i+W_i$ with zero-mean noise independent of the $X_i$, then $\mathbb{E}[Y_0\mid X_0]=\theta_0+\theta_1X_0$, so by the LMS theorem of G6 §4 the true $(\theta_0,\theta_1)$ minimize $\mathbb{E}\big[(Y_0-\theta_0'-\theta_1'X_0)^2\big]$ over all $(\theta_0',\theta_1')$. By the weak law of large numbers (G6 §1), for each fixed candidate pair $$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big(Y_i-\theta_0'-\theta_1'X_i\big)^2 \;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\;\mathbb{E}\big[(Y_0-\theta_0'-\theta_1'X_0)^2\big] \quad\text{in probability},$$ so minimizing the sample average — which is $\frac1n S(\theta_0',\theta_1')$, the least-squares criterion divided by the constant $n$ — approximates minimizing the true objective. Least squares is therefore a consistent way of finding the true coefficients, in the sense of §1.

3.5 Why least squares? Maximum likelihood under normal noise, and what it buys

Sections 3.2–3.4 contain no probability. L24 slide 3's "one interpretation" supplies it, and in doing so connects regression to the maximum likelihood machinery of §2.

Definition (the linear–normal model; L24 slide 3, B&T §9.2 justification (a)). The numbers $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ are known constants. The responses are random variables $$Y_i=\theta_0+\theta_1x_i+W_i,\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,$$ where $W_1,\ldots,W_n$ are i.i.d. $N(0,\sigma^2)$ — independent normal errors of mean zero and common variance $\sigma^2$ — and $\theta_0,\theta_1$ (and possibly $\sigma^2$) are the unknown constants to be estimated. Consequently each $Y_i$ is normal with mean $\theta_0+\theta_1x_i$ and variance $\sigma^2$, and the $Y_i$ are independent (though not identically distributed — their means differ).
Theorem (least squares $=$ ML; L24 slide 3). Under the linear–normal model, the maximum likelihood estimates of $(\theta_0,\theta_1)$ are exactly the least-squares estimates, and the ML estimate of the noise variance is $\hat\sigma^2_{\text{ML}}=\mathrm{SSE}/n$.

Derivation, filling the slide's "Take logs, same as $(*)$".

Step 1 — write the likelihood. Since the $Y_i$ are independent, the joint density factorizes; since $Y_i\sim N(\theta_0+\theta_1x_i,\sigma^2)$, each factor is a normal density evaluated at $y_i$: $$f_Y(y;\theta_0,\theta_1)=\prod_{i=1}^n\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left\{-\frac{(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\}.$$ Collect the $n$ identical prefactors and add the exponents (the product of exponentials is the exponential of the sum): $$f_Y(y;\theta_0,\theta_1)=\underbrace{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}}_{=\,c} \exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^2\right\},$$ which is the slide's expression, with its unexplained constant $c$ now identified as $(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}$. Note that $c$ does not involve $\theta_0$ or $\theta_1$.

Step 2 — take logs. The logarithm is strictly increasing, so it does not move the location of a maximum (§2): $$\ln f_Y(y;\theta_0,\theta_1)=-\frac n2\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^2 =-\frac n2\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}S(\theta_0,\theta_1).$$ Step 3 — read off the maximizer. The first term is a constant in $(\theta_0,\theta_1)$. In the second, the factor $-1/(2\sigma^2)$ is a negative constant, so maximizing the whole expression over $(\theta_0,\theta_1)$ is the same as minimizing $S(\theta_0,\theta_1)$ — which is the least-squares criterion $(*)$. Hence the ML estimates are the least-squares estimates: $\hat\theta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}$, $\hat\theta_0=\bar y-\hat\theta_1\bar x$. Notice that they do not depend on $\sigma^2$ at all. $\blacksquare$

Step 4 — the variance. Now maximize over $v=\sigma^2$ with $(\theta_0,\theta_1)$ at their optima, so that $S(\hat\theta_0,\hat\theta_1)=\mathrm{SSE}$ is a fixed number: $$\ell(v)=-\frac n2\ln(2\pi v)-\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{2v},\qquad \frac{d\ell}{dv}=-\frac{n}{2v}+\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{2v^2}=0 \ \Longrightarrow\ nv=\mathrm{SSE}\ \Longrightarrow\ \hat v=\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{n},$$ where $\frac{d}{dv}\ln(2\pi v)=1/v$ and $\frac{d}{dv}v^{-1}=-v^{-2}$. For $v$ below $\mathrm{SSE}/n$ the derivative is positive and above it negative, so this is the maximum. For Example 3.1, $\hat\sigma^2_{\text{ML}}=60/8=7.5$ (sigma2_ML).

Interpretation (what the normal assumption is really doing). The slide's last line, "Least sq. $\leftrightarrow$ pretend $W_i$ i.i.d. normal", is exactly right — and the word pretend is exactly right too. Three things follow. (i) Squares, not absolute values, come from the square in the normal exponent; Laplace noise $\propto e^{-|w|/b}$ would give least absolute deviations instead. (ii) All the residuals are weighted equally because all the $W_i$ were given the same $\sigma^2$; if the spread varies with $i$ the correct ML criterion is the weighted sum $\sum_i\alpha_i(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^2$ with $\alpha_i\propto1/\sigma_i^2$ (L24 slide 6's "heteroskedasticity", §3.8). (iii) The independence of the $W_i$ is what made the likelihood a product; correlated errors — a time series, say — call for a different criterion entirely.
Property (sampling properties of the estimators; B&T §9.2, p. 479, and the end-of-chapter problems). Under the linear–normal model, with the $x_i$ fixed: $$\hat\Theta_1=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)Y_i}{S_{xx}},\qquad \mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_1]=\theta_1,\qquad \operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_1)=\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}},$$ $$\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_0]=\theta_0,\qquad \operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_0)=\sigma^2\left(\frac1n+\frac{\bar x^{\,2}}{S_{xx}}\right),\qquad \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SSE}]=(n-2)\sigma^2 .$$ Both estimators are unbiased, and both are normal (being linear combinations of independent normals).

Derivation.

(a) The linear form. Because $\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)=0$, $$\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(Y_i-\bar Y)=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)Y_i-\bar Y\underbrace{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)}_{0} =\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)Y_i,$$ so $\hat\Theta_1=\sum_i c_iY_i$ with the fixed weights $c_i=(x_i-\bar x)/S_{xx}$. This is the key structural fact: the slope estimator is a fixed linear combination of the responses.

(b) Unbiasedness of $\hat\Theta_1$. Take expectations, using linearity and $\mathbb{E}[Y_i]=\theta_0+\theta_1x_i$: $$\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_1]=\sum_i c_i(\theta_0+\theta_1x_i) =\theta_0\underbrace{\sum_i c_i}_{=0}+\theta_1\sum_i c_ix_i .$$ The first sum vanishes because $\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)=0$. For the second, $$\sum_i c_ix_i=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)x_i}{S_{xx}} =\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(x_i-\bar x)}{S_{xx}}=\frac{S_{xx}}{S_{xx}}=1,$$ where the middle equality again subtracts $\bar x\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)=0$. Hence $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_1]=\theta_1$: unbiased for every $n$, not just asymptotically.

(c) Variance of $\hat\Theta_1$. The $Y_i$ are independent with variance $\sigma^2$ each, so variances of a linear combination add with squared weights (G2 §3): $$\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_1)=\sum_i c_i^2\sigma^2 =\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}^2}\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2=\frac{\sigma^2 S_{xx}}{S_{xx}^2} =\frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}} .$$

(d) The intercept. $\hat\Theta_0=\bar Y-\hat\Theta_1\bar x$, so $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_0]=\mathbb{E}[\bar Y]-\bar x\theta_1 =\big(\theta_0+\theta_1\bar x\big)-\theta_1\bar x=\theta_0$, using $\mathbb{E}[\bar Y]=\frac1n\sum_i(\theta_0+\theta_1x_i)=\theta_0+\theta_1\bar x$. For its variance, first note the two pieces are uncorrelated: $$\operatorname{cov}(\bar Y,\hat\Theta_1)=\operatorname{cov}\Big(\tfrac1n\sum_iY_i,\sum_jc_jY_j\Big) =\frac1n\sum_i c_i\sigma^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\cdot\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)}{S_{xx}}=0,$$ because independent $Y_i$ contribute only their own variances. Therefore $$\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_0)=\operatorname{var}(\bar Y)+\bar x^{\,2} \operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_1)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}+\frac{\bar x^{\,2}\sigma^2}{S_{xx}} .$$

(e) $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SSE}]$. The count $n-2$ (rather than $n$) is the price of having estimated two parameters: the residual vector lives in the $(n-2)$-dimensional subspace orthogonal to $\mathbf{1}$ and to $x$ (the identities of §3.3), and each remaining dimension contributes $\sigma^2$. Monte Carlo confirms it: with $n=8$ and $\sigma=5$, $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SSE}]$ should be $6\cdot25=150$, and $4\times10^5$ simulated datasets give $150.029$ (WID_n8_s5_ESSE_theory, WID_n8_s5_ESSE_MC); with $\sigma=12$, theory $864$, simulation $863.36$. $\blacksquare$

Gotcha. $\hat\sigma^2_{\text{ML}}=\mathrm{SSE}/n$ is biased low: its expectation is $\frac{n-2}{n}\sigma^2$, which for $n=8$ is $0.75\sigma^2$ (sigma2_ML_bias_factor) — a $25\%$ underestimate on average, and therefore confidence intervals that are $13\%$ too narrow. Always report $$\hat\sigma^2=\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{n-2},\qquad \hat\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{n-2}},$$ the unbiased version; $\hat\sigma$ is what software calls the standard error of the regression and what L24 slide 6 lists as "'Standard error' (estimate of $\sigma$)". This is the same "ML can be biased" phenomenon as in §2, and the same $n$ versus $n-1$ story as the sample variance in §1 — here the correction is $-2$ because two coefficients were fitted.
Recipe (confidence intervals for the coefficients; L24 slide 6, using §1's machinery). To get a $1-\alpha$ confidence interval for $\theta_1$:
  1. Fit: compute $\hat\theta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}$ and $\mathrm{SSE}$.
  2. Estimate the noise: $\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\mathrm{SSE}/(n-2)}$.
  3. Standard error of the slope: $\operatorname{se}(\hat\theta_1)=\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}$ — the estimated version of $\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_1)}=\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}$.
  4. Quantile: if $\sigma$ were known, use $z$ with $\Phi(z)=1-\alpha/2$ and the interval $\hat\theta_1\pm z\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}$ is exact, because $\hat\Theta_1$ is exactly normal. With $\sigma$ estimated from $n$ points and $2$ fitted coefficients, use instead the $t$ quantile with $n-2$ degrees of freedom (§1).
  5. Report $\hat\theta_1\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-2}\operatorname{se}(\hat\theta_1)$. Same recipe for $\theta_0$ with $\operatorname{se}(\hat\theta_0)=\hat\sigma\sqrt{1/n+\bar x^{\,2}/S_{xx}}$.

With $t_{0.975,6}=2.446912$ (t_crit_6df_975), the $95\%$ confidence intervals are $$\theta_1:\ 5\pm2.446912(0.487950)=5\pm1.193971=[3.806029,\ 6.193971],$$ $$\theta_0:\ 55\pm2.446912(2.464027)=55\pm6.029257=[48.970743,\ 61.029257]$$ (ci_th1, ci_th0). 3.6 Reporting a regression, and a real dataset

Recipe (a complete simple-regression analysis; L24 slides 4 and 6).
  1. Plot the data first. If the cloud curves, or fans out, or has one point far from the rest, stop and read §3.8 before fitting anything.
  2. Compute the six numbers $n,\ \bar x,\ \bar y,\ S_{xx},\ S_{xy},\ S_{yy}$. Everything else is a function of these.
  3. Estimate: $\hat\theta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}$, $\hat\theta_0=\bar y-\hat\theta_1\bar x$.
  4. Assess spread: $\mathrm{SSE}=S_{yy}-S_{xy}^2/S_{xx}$, $\hat\sigma=\sqrt{\mathrm{SSE}/(n-2)}$.
  5. Assess fit: $R^2=S_{xy}^2/(S_{xx}S_{yy})$.
  6. Quantify uncertainty: $\operatorname{se}(\hat\theta_1)=\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}$, $\operatorname{se}(\hat\theta_0)=\hat\sigma\sqrt{1/n+\bar x^{\,2}/S_{xx}}$, and the $t_{n-2}$ intervals of §3.5.
  7. Plot the residuals against $x$ and look for structure. No structure, no complaint.
  8. State the scope: the fit describes the range of $x$ in the data, and describes association, not causation.

Steps 4–5. $\mathrm{SSE}=1.923\times10^{-6}$ m² and $R^2=0.98798$ (pisa_SSE, pisa_R2): the straight line accounts for $98.8\%$ of the variation in the lean.

Interpretation. $\hat\theta_1=0.00093187$ metres per year is $0.9319$ millimetres per year (pisa_mm_per_year) — a number with a clear physical meaning, unlike $\hat\theta_0$, which is the lean the line predicts "in the year $0$" and is a pure artifact of where the $x$ axis has its origin. This is a general point: an intercept far outside the data range is not interpretable. Had we recentred the years, fitting $y$ against $x-1981$, the slope would be unchanged at $0.00093187$ while the intercept would become $\bar y=2.969369$, the lean in the middle year — a far more useful parametrization (pisa_centred_th1, pisa_centred_th0; the identity $\hat\theta_0^{\text{centred}}=\bar y$ is (3.1) with $\bar x=0$).

The residuals (Fig. 3.4, right) are at most $0.74$ mm in absolute value and form $9$ runs of constant sign in $13$ points (pisa_resid_absmax_mm, pisa_sign_runs) — the sequence of signs is $+,-,-,+,-,+,+,-,+,-,-,+,+$, which breaks into $9$ blocks, i.e. $8$ sign changes. Step 8. The fit says nothing about why the tower tilts, and it applies to 1975–1987. Practice 3.8. Using Example 3.1's fit $y=55+5x$: (a) what score does the model predict for a student who works $10$ hours a week? (b) For $20$ hours? (c) What is wrong with each answer?
Solution

(a) $55+5(10)=105$ (pred_x10). (b) $55+5(20)=155$ (pred_x20).

(c) Both are impossible: an exam is out of $100$. The data covered $x\in[1,8]$, and the fitted line carries no information whatever about what happens outside that window — it was never asked to. Whatever the true relation is, it must flatten out as the score approaches its ceiling, and a straight line cannot flatten. This is the extrapolation failure of §3.8, and it is worth noticing how loudly the model announces its own invalidity here: a prediction outside the physically possible range is a proof that the model has been pushed too far. It is much more dangerous when the extrapolated value merely looks plausible.

3.7 The world of regression: more variables, better variables, priors

L24 slides 5 and 6 sketch the extensions. All of them keep the same three moving parts — a sum of squared residuals, derivatives set to zero, linear equations to solve — and this is the reason regression scales.

Definition (multiple linear regression; L24 slide 5, B&T §9.2). With data $(x_i,x_i',x_i'',y_i)$, $i=1,\ldots,n$, and model $y\approx\theta_0+\theta x+\theta'x'+\theta''x''$, the estimates minimize $$\min_{\theta_0,\theta,\theta',\theta''}\ \sum_{i=1}^n \big(y_i-\theta_0-\theta x_i-\theta'x_i'-\theta''x_i''\big)^2 .$$

Setting the four partial derivatives to zero gives four linear equations in four unknowns — exactly the same argument as Steps 1–2 of §3.2, once per coefficient. The pattern is worth seeing once in general: differentiating with respect to the coefficient of a variable $u$ gives $-2\sum_i u_i(y_i-\text{fitted}_i)=0$, i.e. the residuals are orthogonal to every explanatory variable (and to the constant $1$, from $\theta_0$) — the identities of §3.3, one per column. The system always has a unique solution unless some explanatory variable is an exact linear combination of the others, which is the extreme case of the multicollinearity of §3.8.

Definition (choosing the variables; L24 slide 5). For a model $y\approx\theta_0+\theta_1h(x)$ with $h$ a chosen function, minimize $$\min_{\theta_0,\theta_1}\ \sum_{i=1}^n\big(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1h(x_i)\big)^2 .$$ More generally $y\approx\theta_0+\sum_{j=1}^m\theta_jh_j(x)$.
Interpretation (why "linear" regression fits curves). Substituting $u_i=h(x_i)$ turns the problem into ordinary simple regression on the pairs $(u_i,y_i)$: run the §3.2 formulas with $u$ in place of $x$. So fitting $y\approx\theta_0+\theta_1x^2$, or $y\approx\theta_0+\theta_1\ln x$, or a cubic $y\approx\theta_0+\theta_1x+\theta_2x^2+\theta_3x^3$, is still linear regression. The word "linear" refers to linearity in the unknown parameters $\theta_j$, not in $x$ — that is what makes the derivative equations linear and the solution closed-form. By contrast $y\approx\theta_1e^{\theta_2x}$ is genuinely nonlinear in the parameters and has no closed form (B&T's "Nonlinear Regression", p. 483) — though Practice 3.9 shows the standard trick for that particular case.
Interpretation (Bayesian linear regression, for contrast; B&T §9.2, pp. 480–482). Everything above is classical: $\theta_0,\theta_1$ are constants. If instead you model them as random variables $\Theta_0,\Theta_1$ — independent, normal, mean $0$, variances $\sigma_0^2$ and $\sigma_1^2$ — and keep $Y_i=\Theta_0+\Theta_1x_i+W_i$ with $W_i\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$, then by Bayes' rule the posterior is proportional to $$\exp\left\{-\frac{\theta_0^2}{2\sigma_0^2}\right\} \exp\left\{-\frac{\theta_1^2}{2\sigma_1^2}\right\} \prod_{i=1}^n\exp\left\{-\frac{(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\},$$ and maximizing it (MAP, G6 §3) means minimizing $$\frac{\theta_0^2}{2\sigma_0^2}+\frac{\theta_1^2}{2\sigma_1^2} +\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^2 .$$ This is the least-squares criterion plus two penalty terms that pull the coefficients toward the prior mean $0$ — "shrinkage", known in machine learning as ridge regression. Two limits are worth holding on to: as $\sigma_0^2,\sigma_1^2\to\infty$ (no prior information) the penalties vanish and the MAP estimates become the classical least-squares estimates; while if $\sigma^2$ is huge compared with the prior variances, the data are drowned out and the estimates collapse to the prior means. The classical answer is the no-prior-information corner of the Bayesian one — precisely the relationship §0 promised between the two philosophies.

Practice 3.9 (linearizing an exponential). Six measurements of a growing quantity: $x=0,1,2,3,4,5$ and $y=2.0,\ 3.4,\ 5.5,\ 9.1,\ 14.8,\ 24.6$. The suspected model is $y\approx ae^{bx}$, which is nonlinear in its parameters. Fit it anyway.
Solution

The trick. Take logarithms of the model: $\ln y\approx \ln a+bx$. Setting $z=\ln y$, $\theta_0=\ln a$, $\theta_1=b$, this is simple linear regression of $z$ on $x$ — the "choosing the right variables" idea of L24 slide 5 applied to the response rather than the explanatory variable.

The fit. The transformed responses are $z=0.693147,\ 1.223775,\ 1.704748,\ 2.208274,\ 2.694627,\ 3.202746$ (P312_lny), with $\bar x=2.5$, $\bar z=1.954553$, $S_{xx}=17.5$ and $S_{xz}=8.732039$ (P312_Sxx, P312_Sxz). Hence $$\hat b=\hat\theta_1=\frac{8.732039}{17.5}=0.498974,\qquad \widehat{\ln a}=\hat\theta_0=1.954553-0.498974(2.5)=0.707119,$$ $$\hat a=e^{0.707119}=2.028140$$ (P312_b1, P312_b0, P312_a). The fitted curve is $y\approx2.02814\,e^{0.498974x}$ — recognizably $2e^{x/2}$, which is indeed how the data were built. Fitted values $2.0281,\ 3.3404,\ 5.5018,\ 9.0615,\ 14.9246,\ 24.5813$ against the observed $2.0,3.4,5.5,9.1,14.8,24.6$ leave a total squared error of $0.021709$ on the original scale (P312_fitted_y, P312_SSE_original_scale), and $R^2=0.999863$ on the log scale (P312_R2_log_scale).

The catch, and it is a real one. You have minimized squared errors in $\ln y$, not in $y$. Under the ML reading of §3.5, that corresponds to assuming multiplicative lognormal noise, $Y_i=ae^{bx_i}\cdot e^{W_i}$ — errors proportional to the size of $y$ — rather than additive noise of constant size. Which is right is a modelling judgement about your measurement process, not a mathematical question; and for growth data, proportional error is usually the more plausible of the two. Note the payoff either way: a nonlinear fit with no iteration, obtained by a change of variable.

3.8 What a fitted line does not claim

L24 slide 6 lists the "common concerns" in four words and moves on. They deserve more, because every one of them is a way that a technically correct computation produces a false conclusion. B&T's "Practical Considerations" (pp. 484–485) is the reference.

Fig. 3.6 — three of the failures of L24 slide 6, each drawn from data where the truth is known. (a) The fit is excellent over $x\in[1,8]$ and useless at $x=15$. (b) The noise variance grows with $x$, so the right-hand points, having the biggest errors, exert the biggest pull — the assumption of a common $\sigma^2$ in §3.5 has failed. (c) The nine-point fit is the Practice 3.10 computation: one mis-recorded observation moves the slope from $5$ to $1/3$.
Gotcha (the big one: correlation is not causation). B&T, p. 485: "a strong relation between $x$ and $y$ should not be mistaken for a discovery of a causal relation". Their example is exact and worth memorizing: let $x_i$ be the wealth of the first child and $y_i$ the wealth of the second child in family $i$. The regression will show a strong positive slope. It would be absurd to conclude that giving money to the first child enriches the second; both are driven by a common cause, the wealth of the parents. Three distinct explanations always compete for a nonzero $\hat\theta_1$: (i) $x$ causes $y$; (ii) $y$ causes $x$; (iii) a third variable causes both. Least squares cannot tell them apart, because the arithmetic is symmetric in the data — recall Practice 3.6, where regressing $x$ on $y$ was just as easy. Only an intervention (a randomized experiment, in which the experimenter sets $x$) can, and that is exactly why the $x_i$ in the model are described as chosen, exogenous constants.
Gotcha (extrapolation). Fig. 3.6(a): the fitted line and the true relation are nearly indistinguishable over the range of the data and disagree by more than the whole range of $y$ well outside it. A straight line always extrapolates to $\pm\infty$; almost nothing in the world does. Practice 3.8 predicted an exam score of $105$. The tower of B&T Example 9.9 was later stabilized. Confidence intervals do not save you here: they quantify noise, not the possibility that the model shape is wrong outside the data.
Gotcha (heteroskedasticity, multicollinearity, overfitting). Three more from L24 slide 6 and B&T pp. 484–485.
  • Heteroskedasticity — the noise variance is not constant across $i$ (Fig. 3.6(b): the variance of household consumption is much larger for rich households than poor ones). The least-squares estimator remains unbiased, but the noisiest points get equal weight and so dominate the fit, and the standard errors of §3.5 — which were derived from a single $\sigma^2$ — are simply wrong. Remedy: weighted least squares, $\min\sum_i\alpha_i(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^2$ with $\alpha_i$ small where $\sigma_i$ is large (this is the correct ML criterion when the $W_i$ have different variances; redo Step 1 of §3.5's derivation with $\sigma_i^2$ inside each exponent to see $\alpha_i=1/\sigma_i^2$ fall out).
  • Multicollinearity — in multiple regression, two explanatory variables that move together cannot have their effects separated. B&T's extreme case: if $y=2x+1$ is the truth and $z=2x$ always holds in the data, then $y=z+1$ fits identically well, and no procedure can choose between them. Symptom: coefficients with huge standard errors that swing wildly when a data point is added.
  • Overfitting — with enough explanatory variables you can drive $\mathrm{SSE}$ to zero and $R^2$ to $1$ while learning nothing; $n$ points can be fitted exactly by a polynomial of degree $n-1$. B&T's rule of thumb: have several times (say ten times) as many data points as parameters. Note the arithmetic warning built into §3.5: with $p$ fitted coefficients the unbiased variance estimate divides by $n-p$, which blows up as $p\to n$ — the formula itself is telling you that you have no information left.
Practice 3.10 (one bad point). A ninth student is added to Example 3.1's data, recorded as $x_9=15$ hours, $y_9=60$ points (the hours were mis-entered). Refit and report the damage.
Solution

Now $n=9$, $\sum_ix_i=36+15=51$ so $\bar x=51/9=17/3=5.666667$, and $\sum_iy_i=620+60=680$ so $\bar y=680/9=75.555556$ (P313_xbar, P313_ybar). Recomputing the two sums gives $S_{xx}=140$ and $S_{xy}=140/3$ (P313_Sxx, P313_Sxy), hence $$\hat\theta_1=\frac{140/3}{140}=\frac13=0.333333,\qquad \hat\theta_0=\frac{680}{9}-\frac13\cdot\frac{17}{3}=\frac{680-17}{9}=\frac{221}{3}=73.666667$$ (P313_th1, P313_th0). The slope has fallen from $5$ to $1/3$ — the fitted line is essentially flat (Fig. 3.6(c)) — and $R^2$ has collapsed from $0.945946$ to $0.011254$ (P313_R2), while $\mathrm{SSE}$ has risen from $60$ to $1366.67$ (P313_SSE).

Why one point can do this. The new point has $x_9-\bar x=15-17/3=28/3$, far larger in magnitude than any other deviation, and $\hat\Theta_1=\sum_ic_iY_i$ with $c_i=(x_i-\bar x)/S_{xx}$ — so its weight $c_9$ dwarfs the others. A point that is extreme in $x$ is called high leverage: it can move the line a lot. Its own residual is only $-18.67$ (P313_resid_outlier), which is not even the largest in the refit, precisely because the line has swung over to chase it. This is why the residual plot of Recipe step 7 is mandatory — and why you plot the data before fitting (step 1), since here the bad point is obvious to the eye.

Practice 3.11. A study regresses annual medical spending $y$ on number of hospital visits $x$ across $10{,}000$ people and finds $\hat\theta_1>0$ with a narrow confidence interval and $R^2=0.61$. A newspaper reports "hospital visits cause higher medical bills — reduce visits to save money". Diagnose every error.
Solution

(1) Reverse causation and common cause. The obvious explanation is that being ill causes both more visits and more spending. Illness is the third variable of the Gotcha above; the regression cannot see it. (2) Policy conclusions require intervention. The estimated slope describes people who chose different numbers of visits, not what happens when you forbid visits — a comparison the data never made. (3) Precision is not validity. The narrow interval says only that with $n=10{,}000$ the sampling noise in $\hat\Theta_1$ is small ($\operatorname{se}\propto1/\sqrt{S_{xx}}$, which grows with $n$); it says nothing about bias from an omitted variable, which does not shrink with $n$ — more data makes a confounded estimate more precisely wrong. (4) $R^2$ measures spread accounted for, not truth. $R^2=0.61$ would be just as high if a third variable drove everything. (5) Untested linearity and probable heteroskedasticity. Spending is bounded below by $0$ and heavily right-skewed, so the constant- $\sigma^2$ normal model of §3.5 is implausible and the reported interval is suspect on its own terms. The defensible statement is the boring one: in this sample, people with more hospital visits spent more, by about $\hat\theta_1$ per visit.

§3 cheatsheet.
  • Setup (L24 slide 3): data $(x_i,y_i)$; the $x_i$ are exact constants, $\theta_0,\theta_1$ are unknown constants (classical, no prior), only the $y_i$ are noisy. Residual $y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i$, measured vertically.
  • Criterion: $\min_{\theta_0,\theta_1}S=\sum_i(y_i-\theta_0-\theta_1x_i)^2$.
  • Normal equations: $n\theta_0+(\sum x_i)\theta_1=\sum y_i$ and $(\sum x_i)\theta_0+(\sum x_i^2)\theta_1=\sum x_iy_i$; Hessian determinant $4nS_{xx}>0$, so the stationary point is the unique global minimum.
  • Solution (L24 slide 4): $\hat\theta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}$, $\hat\theta_0=\bar y-\hat\theta_1\bar x$, with $S_{xx}=\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$, $S_{xy}=\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)$, $S_{yy}=\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2$; equivalently $S_{xy}=\sum x_iy_i-n\bar x\bar y$.
  • Always true: $\sum_ir_i=0$, $\sum_ix_ir_i=0$, $\sum_i\hat y_ir_i=0$, line through $(\bar x,\bar y)$; $\mathrm{SST}=\mathrm{SSR}+\mathrm{SSE}$ with $\mathrm{SSE}=S_{yy}-S_{xy}^2/S_{xx}$ and $R^2=S_{xy}^2/(S_{xx}S_{yy})=\hat\rho^{\,2}\in[0,1]$.
  • Linear LMS reading (L24 slide 4, G6 §4): under $Y=\theta_0+\theta_1X+W$ with $W$ independent of $X$ and zero mean, $\theta_1=\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)/\operatorname{var}(X)$ and $\theta_0=\mathbb{E}[Y]-\theta_1\mathbb{E}[X]$; the regression formulas are these with sample moments $S_{xy}/n$, $S_{xx}/n$, $\bar x$, $\bar y$ in place of population moments.
  • ML reading (L24 slide 3): $Y_i=\theta_0+\theta_1x_i+W_i$, $W_i$ i.i.d. $N(0,\sigma^2)$ gives likelihood $(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\exp\{-S(\theta_0,\theta_1)/(2\sigma^2)\}$; logs turn maximizing into minimizing $S$. Also $\hat\sigma^2_{\text{ML}}=\mathrm{SSE}/n$, biased low.
  • Sampling: $\hat\Theta_1=\sum_i c_iY_i$ with $c_i=(x_i-\bar x)/S_{xx}$; $\mathbb{E}[\hat\Theta_1]=\theta_1$, $\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_1)=\sigma^2/S_{xx}$; $\operatorname{var}(\hat\Theta_0)=\sigma^2(1/n+\bar x^2/S_{xx})$; $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SSE}]=(n-2)\sigma^2$, so use $\hat\sigma^2=\mathrm{SSE}/(n-2)$, and $\hat\theta_1\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-2}\,\hat\sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}$.
  • Extensions (L24 slide 5): multiple regression (one normal equation per coefficient); $y\approx\theta_0+\theta_1h(x)$ is still linear regression — "linear" means linear in $\theta$; Bayesian regression $=$ least squares $+$ shrinkage penalties, and reduces to least squares as the prior variances grow.
  • Failures (L24 slide 6): causation, extrapolation, heteroskedasticity (use weights), multicollinearity, overfitting, high-leverage points. Always plot the data, then the residuals.
