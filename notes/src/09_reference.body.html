This page is the ninth and last, and it is the only one that teaches nothing. The seven notes G1–G7 are a course: they build the subject in order, across 28 numbered teaching sections, deriving every result before using it. Reading them is a linear activity. Looking something up is not — and that is the gap this page fills. It has two halves, and they serve two different readers.

The lookup half (§1) is for the reader who already did the course and now wants one number, one density, or one identity, fast. An audit of these notes against a standard one-page cheatsheet reached a blunt verdict: coverage is strong — of 189 checked items only 3 were in the 6.041 syllabus and genuinely absent — but "the distribution facts exist but are scattered across four tables in four different notes." That is a retrieval problem, not a content problem, and §1 solves it by consolidation. §1.1–§1.2 are one table of every named distribution in the course — PMF or PDF, range, mean, variance, the mechanism that generates it, and the note that derives it. §1.4 is the master formula sheet: the expectation, variance, conditioning, transform, limit, and inference identities of all seven notes on one surface, grouped by what you are trying to do rather than by which week they were taught.

The enrichment half (§2–§3) is for a different reader: someone arriving from Harvard's Stat 110 or from self-study, who has met machinery that 6.041 simply does not use. The same audit classified 32 cheatsheet items as out-of-syllabus rather than missing — chiefly moment generating functions, order statistics, and a zoo of named distributions and identities wider than B&T needs. Those are collected here, stated properly and connected back to the in-course machinery they generalize, so that a reader coming the other way is not left guessing whether an unfamiliar symbol was something these notes forgot.

Nothing on this page is derived from scratch. Every in-syllabus entry carries a pointer to the note section that does the work — that is where the argument, the intuition, the worked examples, and the figures live. Treat an entry here as an index card, and the cited section as the thing the card indexes. (The enrichment entries of §2–§3 have no such parent section by construction, so they carry short self-contained justifications instead, plus a B&T or cheatsheet citation.)

Recipe — looking something up.
  1. You want a distribution. Its PMF or PDF, its range, its mean or variance, or "which family is this mechanism?" → §1.1–§1.2, the consolidated distribution table. It covers the discrete families of G2 §1 and G2 §3, the continuous families of G3 §1, the beta of G3 §3 and G6 §3, the Erlang and the arrival-process families of G4 §3–§4, and the normal in the form used by the CLT in G6 §2 — plus two rows, chi-square and Student-$t$, that are flagged beyond 6.041 because no section of these notes derives them.
  2. You want an identity. An expectation or variance rule, a conditioning law, a convolution, a bound, an estimator or a test statistic → §1.4, the master formula sheet. Each line names its home section: linearity and variance rules in G2 §2–§3, joint and derived distributions in G3 §2 and G3 §4, iterated expectation and random sums in G4 §1–§2, steady-state and absorption equations in G5 §3–§4, inequalities and the CLT in G6 §1–§2, estimators, confidence intervals and tests in G7 §1–§4.
  3. You do not know which tool you need. Do not start here. Start at the note itself — its §0 orientation says what the note is for, and its §5 summary is a one-screen recap. This page assumes you already know the name of the thing you are hunting.
  4. You met a symbol these notes never used. a moment generating function $M_X(t)$, an order statistic $X_{(k)}$, a named family that appears nowhere in G1–G7 → §2 and §3, the beyond-syllabus half.
  5. You want to practise instead of look up. That is the previous page, Q · Question Bank: 170 original questions with fully worked solutions, filtered by difficulty and by section.
Gotcha — §2 and §3 are not part of MIT 6.041. They are outside the syllabus of this course and outside B&T's treatment of it. Nothing in them is examinable in 6.041, nothing in the seven notes depends on them, and no exercise in the question bank needs them. They are here because this site is public and some of its readers are taking a different course (Stat 110 covers this material) or reading on their own with a broader cheatsheet in hand — and because it is genuinely useful to be told "this is a real topic, it is just not this course's topic" rather than to wonder whether you missed a lecture. Interpretation — why consolidation is worth a page of its own. A teaching note and a reference table are optimized against different costs. A note minimizes the effort of understanding a result the first time, which means derivation, motivation, figures, and one idea at a time, spread over the week it belongs to. A table minimizes the time to re-find a result you have already understood, which means everything comparable placed side by side, with the derivations stripped out. The seven notes are excellent at the first job and structurally bad at the second — the geometric mean $1/p$ is derived in G2 §3 and the exponential mean $1/\lambda$ in G3 §1, two notes and several weeks apart, even though the whole point is that they are the same fact at two time resolutions (G4 §3). Putting the two on adjacent rows of one table is not a summary of the notes; it is a different, complementary object.

§1   The distribution table and the master formula sheet

Everything in this section already exists somewhere in the seven notes. That is exactly the problem it solves. The distribution facts are scattered: the audit behind §0 counts seven fragments carrying a table of distribution facts (g2_s1, g2_s5, g3_s5, g5_s5, g6_s2, g6_s3, g7_s0 — rf_s0.json, key fragments_with_distribution_tables), of which four are the full zoos a reader would actually look a family up in: the discrete zoo at the end of G2 (G2 §5.2), the continuous zoo at the end of G3, the Bernoulli-versus-Poisson correspondence at the end of G4, and the hypergeometric, stranded in the counting note at G1 §4. The other three are local, single-purpose tables. Each of the four has its own column headings, its own notation for the parameters, and its own idea of what belongs in a table. When you are mid-problem and want to know whether the Pascal variance carries a factor $k$ or a factor $k^2$, four tables in four files is three tables too many. So this section reprints all of it once, with a single column scheme, in first-appearance order of usefulness, with a pointer back to the note that derives each row. Nothing here is new mathematics; if a row and its home note ever disagree, the home note wins and this page is wrong.

Read the tables in two directions. Left to right is lookup: you know the family, you want the variance. Right to left is modeling, and it is the harder and more valuable direction: you have a sentence in a problem statement, and the column headed "the story" tells you which row that sentence is describing. Most errors in this course are made before any algebra begins, by picking the wrong row — counting successes with a Pascal, or calling a without-replacement draw binomial. The story column is where you check yourself.

1.1 The discrete distributions

Notation, fixed once for the whole table. $p\in(0,1]$ is the success probability of a single trial and $q=1-p$ its failure probability; $n$ is a fixed number of trials; $k$ is a count of successes, except in the Pascal row where $k$ is the fixed order and $t$ is the (random) trial index; $a\le b$ are integers bounding a uniform range, all integers between them included; $\lambda$ is a Poisson arrival rate and $\tau$ the length of the observation window, so that $\lambda\tau$ — never $\lambda$ alone — is the Poisson parameter; and in the hypergeometric row the urn holds $n$ balls of which $m$ are red, $k$ are drawn without replacement, and $i$ of the drawn balls are red. Interpretation — the finite-population correction. The last row is worth one extra sentence, because it is the only row whose variance is not a formula you could guess. Its mean $km/n$ is exactly the binomial mean with $p=m/n$: linearity of expectation does not care that the draws are dependent, so drawing with or without replacement gives the same expected number of red balls. The variance does care. The with-replacement (binomial) variance would be $k\frac{m}{n}(1-\frac{m}{n})=1.2$ for the instance above; the hypergeometric shaves it by the factor $\frac{n-k}{n-1}=\frac{15}{19}=0.789474$, giving $0.947368$. The shave is the whole content of "without replacement": each ball you remove tells you something about what is left, so the total is less variable than free sampling. The factor is $1$ when $k=1$ (one draw cannot be affected by dependence) and $0$ when $k=n$ (drawing every ball leaves nothing random at all). Derived in G1 §4.
Interpretation — why every multinomial covariance is negative. There are only $n$ trials to go around. A trial that lands in category $i$ is a trial that did not land in category $j$, so learning that $N_i$ came out high is evidence that $N_j$ came out low: the categories compete for a fixed budget. The formula $\operatorname{cov}(N_i,N_j)=-n\theta_i\theta_j$ puts a number on that competition, and the constraint $\sum_kN_k=n$ audits it. Since a constant has zero variance, the variance-of-a-sum row of §1.4 block B must give $$0=\operatorname{var}\Big(\sum_kN_k\Big)=\sum_kn\theta_k(1-\theta_k)+\sum_{i\ne j}(-n\theta_i\theta_j),$$ and it does: for $(10;0.5,0.3,0.2)$ the variances sum to $2.5+2.1+1.6=6.2$ and the six ordered cross terms to $2(-1.5-1.0-0.6)=-6.2$, total $0$ to machine precision (F_mn_var_of_total). That identity is the fastest check that you have written the covariance with the right sign. The correlations themselves are modest — $\rho_{12}=-0.654654$, $\rho_{13}=-0.5$, $\rho_{23}=-0.327327$ — and shrink toward $0$ as $r$ grows and each category's share falls.

1.2 The continuous distributions

Notation. $a<b$ bound a uniform range; $\lambda>0$ is a rate, with units of $1/\text{time}$, so $1/\lambda$ is a mean waiting time; $k$ is a positive integer order; $\mu$ and $\sigma>0$ are a normal's mean and standard deviation, and $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF; $\alpha,\beta>0$ are beta shape parameters with normalizing constant $B(\alpha,\beta)=\int_0^1q^{\alpha-1}(1-q)^{\beta-1}dq$. Recall throughout that a PDF is a density, not a probability: $f_X(x)$ may exceed $1$, and only $\int_a^bf_X$ is a probability (G3 §1).

Interpretation — the Gamma family, of which Erlang is the integer slice. The Erlang density $\lambda^ky^{k-1}e^{-\lambda y}/(k-1)!$ only makes sense for integer $k$, because $(k-1)!$ does. Replacing $(k-1)!$ by the gamma function $\Gamma(a)=\int_0^\infty u^{a-1}e^{-u}du$, which satisfies $\Gamma(k)=(k-1)!$ at integers, extends it to any real shape $a>0$: $$f(y)=\frac{\lambda^{a}y^{\,a-1}e^{-\lambda y}}{\Gamma(a)},\qquad y\ge0,\qquad \mathbb{E}[Y]=\frac{a}{\lambda},\qquad \operatorname{var}(Y)=\frac{a}{\lambda^{2}} .$$ The mean and variance formulas are the same two expressions with $k$ replaced by $a$ — checked in the computes at $a=2.5$, $\lambda=2$: mean $1.25$, variance $0.625$, and at $a=3$ it reproduces the Erlang mean $1.5$ exactly. This general Gamma is not used anywhere in 6.041 (every arrival-time question here asks for the $k$th arrival, and $k$ is a count), but you will meet it immediately in any Bayesian course, where it is the conjugate prior for a Poisson rate, and it is what the $\chi^2_\nu$ row above secretly is — $\chi^2_\nu=\Gamma(\nu/2,\,1/2)$, which is exactly where the half-integer shapes earn their keep.
Practice 1.1 (reading the story column backwards). For each sentence, name the distribution and write its mean, using only the tables above. (a) "A machine produces defective parts independently with probability $0.02$; a batch of $500$ is inspected — how many defectives?" (b) "…how many parts must be inspected to find the first defective?" (c) "…to find the third defective?" (d) "A crate holds $500$ parts of which $10$ are defective; $20$ are drawn without replacement — how many defectives among them?" (e) "Defects appear along a wire at a rate of $0.02$ per metre — how many in $500$ metres?" (f) "…how far to the third defect?"
Solution

(a) Binomial$(500,0.02)$ — a fixed number of independent identical trials, count the successes. Mean $np=500\times0.02=10$.

(b) Geometric$(0.02)$ — wait for the first success, unbounded. Mean $1/p=50$ parts. Note this is the trials-until convention of §1.3; a source using failures-before would say $49$.

(c) Pascal of order $3$ at $p=0.02$ — the trial index of the third success. Mean $k/p=3/0.02=150$ parts, variance $k(1-p)/p^2=3(0.98)/0.0004=7350$.

(d) Hypergeometric with $n=500$, $m=10$, $k=20$ — without replacement, so not binomial. Mean $km/n=20\times10/500=0.4$, the same as the binomial with $p=10/500$; the variance is smaller by the factor $(n-k)/(n-1)=480/499=0.961924$.

(e) Poisson with parameter $\lambda\tau=0.02\times500=10$ — a count in a continuous window, mean and variance both $10$. Compare (a): same mean, but the variance is $10$ here against $np(1-p)=9.8$ there, because the binomial's $(1-p)$ factor has been killed by the limit.

(f) Erlang of order $3$ at $\lambda=0.02$ — the position of the third arrival in continuous space. Mean $k/\lambda=150$ metres, variance $k/\lambda^2=7500$. Set (c) beside (f): the same story told on the two clocks of block D, with the Pascal variance $7350$ approaching the Erlang $7500$ as the slot shrinks.

1.3 The one convention clash: which "geometric"?

Gotcha — two incompatible definitions share one name. This course, following B&T §2.2, counts trials up to and including the first success. So $X\in\{1,2,\ldots\}$, $p_X(k)=(1-p)^{k-1}p$, and $$\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac1p .$$ A great many other sources — including the Chen–Blitzstein Probability Cheatsheet, the standard companion to Harvard's Stat 110 — define the geometric as the number of failures before the first success. That is $Y=X-1\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ with $p_Y(j)=(1-p)^jp$ and $$\mathbb{E}[Y]=\frac1p-1=\frac{1-p}{p} .$$ (That cheatsheet reserves the separate name "First Success" for our $X$.) These are the same distribution shifted by one trial, so:
  • The means differ by exactly $1$, always. At $p=0.3$: $3.333333$ against $2.333333$. At $p=0.2$: $5$ against $4$. At $p=0.5$: $2$ against $1$.
  • The variances are identical, $(1-p)/p^2$, because adding or subtracting a constant does not change a variance (G2 §3). At $p=0.3$ both are $7.777778$; at $p=0.2$ both are $20$.
  • Every downstream formula inherits the shift. The Pascal/negative-binomial has the same split — "trial of the $k$th success", mean $k/p$ as in §1.1, versus "failures before the $k$th success", mean $k(1-p)/p$.
The software follows this course, not the cheatsheet. scipy.stats.geom has $\texttt{pmf}(0,p)=0$, $\texttt{pmf}(1,0.3)=0.3$ and mean $1/p=3.333333$; to get the failures-before version you must shift deliberately, scipy.stats.geom(p, loc=-1) (mean $2.333333$) or scipy.stats.nbinom(1, p) (also $2.333333$). All five values verified in computes/rf_s1.py block C. Whenever you import a geometric or negative-binomial formula from outside these notes, read its support first: if $0$ is in the support, it is the failures convention and its mean is one less than ours. This matches the gotcha in G2 §3, where the convention is first flagged.
Practice 1.2 (spotting the convention in a quoted formula). A reference you are reading defines its geometric by $p_Y(j)=(1-p)^jp$ for $j=0,1,2,\ldots$ and quotes "mean $q/p$, variance $q/p^2$", with $q=1-p$. (i) Which convention is that, and what is the one-second test? (ii) Your problem asks for the expected number of tosses of a fair coin up to and including the first head — what do you report? (iii) The same reference gives the negative binomial mean as $kq/p$; you need the trial index of the third success at $p=0.2$ — what is it? (iv) Do you have to adjust the quoted variance as well?
Solution

(i) Failures-before-the-first-success, i.e. our $Y=X-1$. Two tests, either instantly decisive: is $0$ in the support? — it is, and our $X$ starts at $1$; or set $p=1$ — their mean is $q/p=0$, while a sure success still costs one trial, so ours must be $1$. Whenever $0$ is in the support the quoted mean is one less than ours (§1.3).

(ii) $\mathbb{E}[X]=1/p=2$ tosses. Their formula returns $q/p=1$ — the number of failures before the head — so add the successful toss back: $1+1=2$ (E_p12conv_trials, E_p12conv_failures).

(iii) Add $k$, not $1$: the Pascal of order $k$ counts $k$ successes as well as the failures, so $\mathbb{E}[T_k]=k/p=3/0.2=15$ trials, against their $kq/p=3(0.8)/0.2=12$ failures; $12+3=15$ ✓ (E_p12conv_pascal_trials, E_p12conv_pascal_failures). This is the shift of §1.3 applied $k$ times, once per geometric gap.

(iv) No. $Y=X-1$ is a shift, and a shift never changes a variance (affine-map row, block B), so both conventions give $(1-p)/p^2$ — at $p=0.2$, $20$ (E_p12conv_var_both). Only means need repair, which is exactly why the mistake survives a variance check.

Practice 1.3 (which row, and does it need independence?). You have $X$ and $Y$ with $\mathbb{E}[X]=3$, $\mathbb{E}[Y]=5$, $\operatorname{var}(X)=4$, $\operatorname{var}(Y)=9$, $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=-3$. Find (i) $\mathbb{E}[2X-Y+1]$, (ii) $\operatorname{var}(2X-Y)$, (iii) $\rho$, (iv) $\mathbb{E}[XY]$, and (v) the MSE of the best linear estimator of $Y$ from $X$. Solution

(i) Linearity (block B, no independence): $2(3)-5+1=2$.

(ii) Variance of a sum with the covariance term, on $2X$ and $-Y$. Using $\operatorname{cov}(aX,bY)=ab\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)$ and the affine rule, $$\operatorname{var}(2X-Y)=4\operatorname{var}(X)+\operatorname{var}(Y)+2(2)(-1)\operatorname{cov}(X,Y) =16+9-4(-3)=37 .$$ No independence — and note that assuming it would have given $25$, a $32.4\%$ understatement.

(iii) $\rho=\operatorname{cov}/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)=-3/(2\times3)=-0.5$, comfortably inside $[-1,1]$ as the bound requires.

(iv) $\mathbb{E}[XY]=\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)+\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]=-3+15=12$. This uses the covariance identity, which is unconditional; the product rule $\mathbb{E}[XY]=\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]=15$ would need independence and is wrong here — the covariance is nonzero, so they are not independent.

(v) Linear LMS with the roles $\Theta=Y$, observation $X$: $\text{MSE}=(1-\rho^2)\operatorname{var}(Y)=(1-0.25)(9)=6.75$. The straight line removed a quarter of $Y$'s variance and no more. No independence — indeed if they were independent, $\rho=0$ and the MSE would be the full $9$: independence is exactly the case where the data are useless.

2.1 Universality of the uniform

Throughout this subsection $X$ is a continuous random variable whose CDF $F(x)=\mathbf{P}(X\le x)$ is continuous everywhere and strictly increasing on the interval where $0<F(x)<1$. Strict monotonicity is what makes $F$ invertible there, so that $F^{-1}(u)$ — the unique $x$ with $F(x)=u$, called the $u$-quantile of $X$ — is well defined for every $u\in(0,1)$. The exponential, the normal, and the uniform on an interval all qualify; a density with a gap in its support, where $F$ is flat, does not, and is handled by the gotcha below.

Theorem (universality of the uniform; both directions). Let $F$ be as above.
  1. Forward. If $X$ has CDF $F$, then $U:=F(X)$ is uniform on $(0,1)$.
  2. Backward. If $U$ is uniform on $(0,1)$, then $X:=F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$.
So $F$ converts any such random variable into a standard uniform, and $F^{-1}$ converts a standard uniform into any such random variable.

Proof of the forward direction. Fix $u\in(0,1)$ and compute the CDF of $U=F(X)$ by the CDF method of G3 §4 — express the event in terms of $X$, then read off a probability of $X$: $$\mathbf{P}(U\le u)=\mathbf{P}\big(F(X)\le u\big)=\mathbf{P}\big(X\le F^{-1}(u)\big),$$ where the second equality holds because $F$ is strictly increasing, so the inequality $F(x)\le u$ holds for exactly the same $x$ as the inequality $x\le F^{-1}(u)$ (a strictly increasing function preserves order, and so does its inverse). The right-hand probability is by definition the CDF of $X$ evaluated at $F^{-1}(u)$: $$\mathbf{P}\big(X\le F^{-1}(u)\big)=F\big(F^{-1}(u)\big)=u,$$ the last step being the definition of an inverse function. Thus $\mathbf{P}(U\le u)=u$ for every $u\in(0,1)$, and $\mathbf{P}(U\le u)$ is $0$ for $u\le 0$ and $1$ for $u\ge 1$ because $F$ takes values in $[0,1]$. That is exactly the CDF of $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ (G3 §1), and a CDF determines a distribution, so $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$. $\blacksquare$

Proof of the backward direction. Now let $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ and set $X=F^{-1}(U)$. Fix any real $x$ and again take the CDF: $$\mathbf{P}(X\le x)=\mathbf{P}\big(F^{-1}(U)\le x\big)=\mathbf{P}\big(U\le F(x)\big),$$ applying $F$ — increasing, hence order-preserving — to both sides of the inequality inside. Since $F(x)\in[0,1]$ and the uniform CDF is $\mathbf{P}(U\le u)=u$ on that range, $$\mathbf{P}\big(U\le F(x)\big)=F(x).$$ So $X$ has CDF $F$, which is what was claimed. $\blacksquare$

Interpretation. The forward direction says that a CDF is a universal flattener: whatever shape $f_X$ has, plotting $F(X)$ instead of $X$ redistributes the probability perfectly evenly over $(0,1)$, because $F(x)$ is by construction "the fraction of the probability lying to the left of $x$", and a randomly chosen point is equally likely to have any fraction to its left. The backward direction is the same statement read as a manufacturing instruction: all the information in a distribution is packed into the shape of $F^{-1}$, which stretches the parts of $(0,1)$ where the density is low and compresses the parts where it is high. Two consequences worth carrying away. Quantiles: $F^{-1}(0.5)$ is the median, $F^{-1}(0.95)$ the $95$th percentile, and the theorem says a random draw's percentile rank is itself uniform. And, at the edge of G7 §4: the p-value of a test with a continuous test statistic is of the form $F(\text{statistic})$, so under the null hypothesis a p-value is uniform on $(0,1)$ — which is precisely why rejecting when $p\le\alpha$ has false-rejection probability $\alpha$.
Gotcha — both hypotheses are doing work. Continuity is essential in the forward direction. If $X$ is discrete, $F(X)$ takes only finitely or countably many values and cannot be uniform: for a fair coin scored $X\in\{0,1\}$, $F(X)\in\{0.5,1\}$, each with probability $1/2$. Strict monotonicity is essential for $F^{-1}$ to be a function at all; where $F$ is flat — an interval carrying no probability — many $x$ share one value of $u$. The standard repair covers every case at once: define the generalized inverse $$F^{-1}(u)=\min\{x:\ F(x)\ge u\},$$ which exists for any CDF whatever, discrete ones included. The backward direction survives verbatim with this definition (the equivalence $F^{-1}(u)\le x\iff u\le F(x)$ still holds), so inverse-CDF sampling works for discrete distributions too — that is how one simulates a geometric, by walking up the PMF until the cumulative sum passes $U$. The forward direction does not survive, and no repair makes it: no function of a discrete random variable is ever continuous.
Recipe — inverse-CDF (inverse transform) sampling. To generate a draw from a target density $f$ using only a uniform random number generator:
  1. Get the CDF. $F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt$, in closed form.
  2. Invert it. Solve $u=F(x)$ for $x$, giving $x=F^{-1}(u)$. This is algebra, not probability.
  3. Draw and map. Draw $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ from the generator and return $X=F^{-1}(U)$.
  4. Check. The map must be increasing, and it must carry $(0,1)$ onto the support of $f$: $F^{-1}(0^{+})$ should be the left end of the support and $F^{-1}(1^{-})$ the right end. Then compare a simulated mean and variance against the theoretical ones.
If step 1 or step 2 has no closed form — the normal is the standard offender, since $\Phi^{-1}$ is not elementary — this recipe stalls, and one uses a transformation trick instead (Example 2.2 below) or a numerical approximation of $\Phi^{-1}$.
Example 2.1 — inverse-CDF sampling of an exponential.

Statement. Using only a stream of $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ numbers, generate draws from $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$, and verify both directions of the theorem numerically at $\lambda=1.5$.

Step 1 — the CDF. For the exponential, $f(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ on $x\ge0$, so $$F(x)=\int_0^{x}\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt=\big[-e^{-\lambda t}\big]_0^{x}=1-e^{-\lambda x}, \qquad x\ge 0,$$ which is continuous and strictly increasing on $(0,\infty)$ — the theorem applies (G3 §1 derives this CDF).

Step 2 — invert. Set $u=1-e^{-\lambda x}$ and solve: $e^{-\lambda x}=1-u$, then take logarithms of both sides (legal since $1-u>0$ for $u<1$), giving $-\lambda x=\ln(1-u)$ and $$x=F^{-1}(u)=-\frac{\ln(1-u)}{\lambda}.$$ Sanity check: $u\to0^{+}$ gives $x\to0$, $u\to1^{-}$ gives $x\to\infty$, so $(0,1)$ is carried onto $(0,\infty)$, the correct support.

Step 3 — the sampler. Draw $U\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ and return $X=-\ln(1-U)/\lambda$. A common simplification: $1-U$ is also uniform on $(0,1)$ (it is the reflection of $U$ about $1/2$), so $X=-\ln(U)/\lambda$ is an equally valid sampler — the two differ draw by draw but not in distribution.

Step 4 — verify by simulation. With $\lambda=1.5$ and $N=2{,}000{,}000$ draws (u_lambda, u_N): the sample mean is $0.667013$ against the theoretical $1/\lambda=0.666667$, the sample variance is $0.445479$ against $1/\lambda^{2}=0.444444$, and the simulated tail probability $\mathbf{P}(X>2)$ is $0.049993$ against the exact $e^{-1.5\cdot2}=0.049787$. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance — the largest vertical gap between the empirical CDF and the true $\text{Exp}(1.5)$ CDF — is $0.000614$, with p-value $0.438$ (u_ks_stat, u_ks_pvalue): no discrepancy at all. Fig. 2.1(a) is the histogram against the target density.

The result has sample mean $0.499949$ (target $1/2$) and sample variance $0.083353$ (target $1/12=0.083333$), with KS distance $0.000317$ to $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ and p-value $0.988$ (u_fwd_ks_stat). Practice 2.1 — sampling a Cauchy, and a warning about the law of large numbers. The standard Cauchy density is $f(x)=\dfrac{1}{\pi(1+x^{2})}$ on the whole line. (a) Find $F$ and invert it to get a sampler. (b) Simulate and check the median and the interquartile range. (c) What does the sample mean do as $n$ grows, and why?
Solution

(a) Integrate: $\displaystyle F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{dt}{\pi(1+t^{2})} =\frac{1}{\pi}\big[\arctan t\big]_{-\infty}^{x}=\frac{1}{\pi}\Big(\arctan x+\frac{\pi}{2}\Big) =\frac12+\frac{\arctan x}{\pi},$ using $\int dt/(1+t^2)=\arctan t$ and $\arctan(-\infty)=-\pi/2$. Setting $u=F(x)$ and solving: $\arctan x=\pi\big(u-\tfrac12\big)$, so $$X=F^{-1}(U)=\tan\Big(\pi\Big(U-\frac12\Big)\Big).$$ As $U$ approaches $0$ or $1$ the tangent blows up in either direction, which is the heavy tail appearing.

(b) Over $N=2{,}000{,}000$ draws the sample median is $0.000454$ (true median $0$, by symmetry) and the sample interquartile range is $1.998035$ against the exact $F^{-1}(0.75)-F^{-1}(0.25)=\tan(\pi/4)-\tan(-\pi/4)=2$ (c_iqr_sim, c_iqr_true). The tail is genuinely fat: $\mathbf{P}(|X|>10)=2\big(\tfrac12- \arctan(10)/\pi\big)=0.063451$ exactly, simulated $0.063201$ — six percent of draws exceed $10$ in magnitude, where a standard normal would give about $1.5\times10^{-23}$.

(c) The running sample mean never settles. At $10^{4}$, $10^{5}$ and $2\times10^{6}$ draws it reads $0.6030$, $-0.1148$ and $-0.0897$ (c_runmean_1e4 and friends), wandering rather than converging. The reason is that $\mathbb{E}[|X|]=\int|x|f(x)\,dx$ diverges logarithmically, so the Cauchy has no mean — and the weak law of large numbers of G6 §1 assumes a finite mean and variance. Take this as the standing caution about simulation: a simulated average always prints a number, whether or not the quantity it estimates exists.

Practice 2.2. The logistic distribution has CDF $F(x)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$ on the whole line. Give the inverse-CDF sampler and evaluate it at $u=0.9$.
Solution

Solve $u=1/(1+e^{-x})$: invert both sides to get $1+e^{-x}=1/u$, so $e^{-x}=(1-u)/u$, and taking logarithms, $-x=\ln\frac{1-u}{u}$, i.e. $$X=F^{-1}(U)=\ln\frac{U}{1-U},$$ the log-odds, or logit, of $U$. At $u=0.9$ this is $\ln 9=2.197225$ (log_q): the $90$th percentile of the logistic sits at $2.197$. The function is increasing and carries $(0,1)$ onto $(-\infty,\infty)$, as required. (Reading the same identity backwards: applying the logistic CDF to a logistic random variable returns a uniform — the forward direction of the theorem.)

2.2 The two-dimensional change of variables: the Jacobian

G3 §4 transforms one random variable at a time. When two random variables are transformed into two new ones — $(X,Y)\mapsto(U,V)$ — the correction factor for the density is no longer a derivative but a determinant, for the same reason that a substitution in a double integral picks up a determinant: it is the local area-scaling factor of the map.

Theorem (change of variables in two dimensions). Let $X,Y$ have joint density $f_{X,Y}$, and let $(U,V)=g(X,Y)$ be a transformation that is one-to-one and continuously differentiable on the region where $f_{X,Y}>0$, with inverse $x=x(u,v)$, $y=y(u,v)$. Define the Jacobian matrix of the inverse map $$J=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u}&\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v}\\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u}&\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det J=\frac{\partial x}{\partial u}\frac{\partial y}{\partial v} -\frac{\partial x}{\partial v}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}.$$ Then the joint density of $(U,V)$ is $$\boxed{\,f_{U,V}(u,v)=f_{X,Y}\big(x(u,v),\,y(u,v)\big)\;\big|\det J\big|\,}$$ on the image of that region, and $0$ elsewhere. Equivalently, one may compute the Jacobian of the forward map and divide by the absolute value of its determinant, since the two matrices are inverses and $\det(J^{-1})=1/\det J$.
Interpretation — why a determinant. Probability is conserved: the chance of landing in a small rectangle around $(u,v)$ of area $du\,dv$ equals the chance of landing in its preimage around $(x,y)$, a small parallelogram. The linear map $J$ sends the rectangle to that parallelogram, and the absolute determinant of a $2\times2$ matrix is exactly the factor by which it scales area. So $f_{U,V}(u,v)\,du\,dv=f_{X,Y}(x,y)\cdot|\det J|\,du\,dv$. In one dimension the "parallelogram" is an interval and $|\det J|$ collapses to $|dh/dy|$ — which is precisely the formula of G3 §4, $f_Y(y)=f_X(h(y))\,|dh/dy|$. The absolute value plays the same role as there: it merges the increasing and decreasing cases, since a density cannot be negative. The same "probability is conserved" reading explains the Jacobian warnings in G6 §3 and G7 §2 about reparametrizing a posterior: a density transforms with this factor, and a likelihood does not.

The polar case, which the notes already use twice. Take $u=r$, $v=\theta$ with the inverse map $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ for $r\ge0$ and $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$. Differentiating each coordinate with respect to each of $r$ and $\theta$, $$J=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det J=\cos\theta\cdot r\cos\theta-(-r\sin\theta)\cdot\sin\theta =r\big(\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta\big)=r,$$ using the Pythagorean identity in the last step. Hence $$f_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)=f_{X,Y}(r\cos\theta,\,r\sin\theta)\cdot r .$$ That factor $r$ is the whole content of two computations already in these notes: the polar evaluation of the Gaussian integral in G3 §1 ("the extra factor $r$ is what rescues the computation") and the polar decomposition of a standard normal pair in G4 §1 ("the extra factor $r$ is the Jacobian and is the whole content of the computation"). Both are correct; neither names the general rule, because 6.041 never states it. It is the theorem above with $\det J=r$.

Example 2.2 (the Box–Muller transform) — generating normals from uniforms.

Statement. The normal CDF $\Phi$ has no elementary inverse, so the recipe of §2.1 cannot produce normal draws directly. Use the polar Jacobian to build a pair of independent standard normals out of a pair of independent uniforms.

Step 1 — the joint density in polar form. Let $X,Y$ be independent standard normals. By independence their joint density is the product of the marginals (G3 §2), $$f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^{2}/2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-y^{2}/2} =\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(x^{2}+y^{2})/2}.$$ Substituting $x^{2}+y^{2}=r^{2}$ and multiplying by the Jacobian $r$, $$f_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-r^{2}/2}\cdot r =\underbrace{\Big(r\,e^{-r^{2}/2}\Big)}_{\text{depends on }r\text{ only}}\cdot \underbrace{\frac{1}{2\pi}}_{\text{depends on }\theta\text{ only}},\qquad r\ge0,\ \theta\in[0,2\pi).$$

Step 2 — read off two facts. The joint density factors into a function of $r$ times a function of $\theta$ on a rectangular region, which by the criterion of G3 §2 means $R$ and $\Theta$ are independent. Moreover $\Theta$ is uniform on $[0,2\pi)$ (its factor is constant), and $R$ has the Rayleigh density $f_R(r)=re^{-r^{2}/2}$ — which integrates to $1.000000$ over $[0,\infty)$ (ray_norm, by numerical quadrature), confirming the bookkeeping. This is the result G4 §1 obtains; here it is one line of the general rule.

Step 3 — invert the radial CDF. $F_R(r)=\int_0^{r}se^{-s^{2}/2}ds=\big[-e^{-s^{2}/2} \big]_0^{r}=1-e^{-r^{2}/2}$ (substituting $w=s^2/2$, $dw=s\,ds$). Setting $u=1-e^{-r^{2}/2}$ and solving as in Example 2.1: $r^{2}=-2\ln(1-u)$, so $R=\sqrt{-2\ln(1-U_1)}$.

Step 4 — the sampler. Draw independent $U_1,U_2\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ and return $$X=\sqrt{-2\ln(1-U_1)}\,\cos(2\pi U_2),\qquad Y=\sqrt{-2\ln(1-U_1)}\,\sin(2\pi U_2),$$ a pair of independent standard normals — two for the price of two uniforms.

Step 5 — verify. Over $M=1{,}000{,}000$ pairs: sample means $-0.001677$ and $-0.000730$ (true $0$), sample variances $1.001003$ and $0.998087$ (true $1$), and sample correlation $-0.000455$ (true $0$, as they are independent). The radius has sample mean $1.253020$ against the exact Rayleigh mean $\sqrt{\pi/2}=1.253314$ (ray_mean_true). The KS distance of $X$ to $N(0,1)$ is $0.001484$; the $5\%$ critical distance at this sample size is $1.36/\sqrt{M}=0.001360$ (bm_ks_crit5), so the test lands marginally on the reject side, with p-value $0.024$ — an ordinary one-in-forty fluctuation of a test that is razor-sharp at a million draws, not evidence of a defect; the moments above are exact to three decimals.

Example 2.3 — splitting a sum from its share: $S=X+Y$ and $V=X/(X+Y)$.

Statement. $X$ and $Y$ are independent $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ — say two service times. Find the joint density of the total $S=X+Y$ and the first job's share $V=X/(X+Y)$.

Step 1 — invert the map. From $V=X/S$ we get $x=sv$, and then $y=s-x=s(1-v)$. The map is one-to-one from $\{x>0,\ y>0\}$ onto $\{s>0,\ 0<v<1\}$: the total is any positive number and the share is any fraction.

Step 2 — the Jacobian. Differentiate the inverse map: $$J=\begin{pmatrix}\partial x/\partial s&\partial x/\partial v\\ \partial y/\partial s&\partial y/\partial v\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}v&s\\ 1-v&-s\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det J=v(-s)-s(1-v)=-sv-s+sv=-s,$$ so $|\det J|=s$ (positive on the region of interest).

Step 3 — substitute. By independence, $f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\lambda e^{-\lambda y}=\lambda^{2}e^{-\lambda(x+y)}=\lambda^{2}e^{-\lambda s}$, since $x+y=s$ identically. Therefore $$f_{S,V}(s,v)=\lambda^{2}e^{-\lambda s}\cdot s =\underbrace{\big(\lambda^{2}s\,e^{-\lambda s}\big)}_{\text{Erlang order }2}\cdot \underbrace{1}_{\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)},\qquad s>0,\ 0<v<1.$$

Step 4 — read off the conclusion. The density factors over a rectangular region, so $S$ and $V$ are independent; $S$ is Erlang of order $2$ with parameter $\lambda$ — the sum of two independent exponentials, exactly as G4 §3 has it — and $V$ is uniform on $(0,1)$. The last part is the striking one: the share of the total taken by the first job is uniform, and tells you nothing about how long the total was.

Step 5 — verify. At $\lambda=1$ over $1{,}000{,}000$ pairs: $S$ has sample mean $2.001758$ (true $2/\lambda=2$) and sample variance $2.005691$ (true $2/\lambda^{2}=2$), with KS distance $0.000798$ to the Erlang; $V$ has sample mean $0.499550$ and sample variance $0.083351$ (true $1/12$), with KS distance $0.001376$ to $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$; and the sample correlation between $S$ and $V$ is $+0.000083$, consistent with the independence just proved (gv_corr).

Gotcha — the three ways this rule is misapplied. (i) One-to-one is not optional. If two different $(x,y)$ map to the same $(u,v)$, the contributions must be added, one $|\det J|$ term per branch — the two-dimensional version of the $Y=X^{2}$ trap in G3 §4. (ii) The support must be transformed too. The formula gives the density only on the image region; forgetting to work out that region is the single most common error, and it is what makes Example 2.3 land on $0<v<1$ rather than on all of $\mathbb{R}$. (iii) Right matrix, right direction. $|\det J|$ above is that of the map from the new variables to the old; using the forward map instead means dividing rather than multiplying. Check by integrating the answer to $1$, or by testing a case you already know. Finally, when only one new variable is wanted — the density of $X+Y$, say — the honest routes are convolution (G3 §4) or this theorem with an auxiliary second variable (take $U=X+Y$, $V=X$, then integrate $V$ out).
Practice 2.3. $X,Y$ are independent standard normals. Find the joint density of $U=X+Y$ and $V=X-Y$, and say whether they are independent.
Solution

Invert: adding and subtracting the two equations gives $x=(u+v)/2$ and $y=(u-v)/2$. The map is one-to-one from the plane onto the plane.

Jacobian: $$J=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/2\\ 1/2&-1/2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det J=\Big(\frac12\Big)\Big(-\frac12\Big)-\Big(\frac12\Big)\Big(\frac12\Big)=-\frac12, \qquad|\det J|=\frac12 .$$ The map is a rotation by $45^{\circ}$ combined with a stretch by $\sqrt2$, so the inverse shrinks area by $1/2$ — the determinant agrees with the picture.

Substitute: $x^{2}+y^{2}=\dfrac{(u+v)^{2}+(u-v)^{2}}{4}=\dfrac{2u^{2}+2v^{2}}{4} =\dfrac{u^{2}+v^{2}}{2}$ (the cross terms cancel), so $$f_{U,V}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(u^{2}+v^{2})/4}\cdot\frac12 =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\cdot2}}e^{-u^{2}/(2\cdot2)}\cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\cdot2}}e^{-v^{2}/(2\cdot2)} .$$ This is the product of two $N(0,2)$ densities, so $U$ and $V$ are independent, each normal with mean $0$ and variance $2$. Check: $\operatorname{var}(X\pm Y)=1+1=2$ by independence (G3 §4), and $\operatorname{cov}(U,V)=\operatorname{var}(X)-\operatorname{var}(Y)=0$ — but zero covariance alone would not prove independence; the factored density does. Simulation over $10^{6}$ pairs: variances $1.997709$ and $2.002419$, correlation $-0.000306$ (rot_var_sim, rot_corr).

2.3 Order statistics

Definition (order statistics). Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be random variables on the same experiment. Their order statistics $X_{(1)}\le X_{(2)}\le\cdots\le X_{(n)}$ are the same $n$ numbers rearranged into increasing order: $X_{(1)}=\min_i X_i$, $X_{(n)}=\max_i X_i$, and $X_{(k)}$ is the $k$-th smallest. Each $X_{(k)}$ is itself a random variable — a function of the outcome, like any other. When $n$ is odd, $X_{((n+1)/2)}$ is the sample median, and $X_{(n)}-X_{(1)}$ is the sample range.
Gotcha. Even when $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are i.i.d., the order statistics are never independent and never identically distributed: $X_{(1)}\le X_{(3)}$ holds with probability $1$, which is about as dependent as two random variables get. Everything below is about the marginal distribution of a single $X_{(k)}$. Also, for continuous $X_i$ ties have probability zero, so the inequalities are strict almost surely and the sorted list is unambiguous — the formulas below all assume this.

Assume from here that $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are i.i.d. continuous with common CDF $F$ and density $f$.

Property (the CDF of the $k$-th order statistic). For every $x$, $$F_{X_{(k)}}(x)=\mathbf{P}\big(X_{(k)}\le x\big) =\sum_{j=k}^{n}\binom{n}{j}F(x)^{j}\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n-j}.$$

Derivation. Fix $x$ and count how many of the samples fall at or below it: $N(x)=\#\{i:X_i\le x\}$. Each sample independently lands at or below $x$ with the same probability $F(x)$, so $N(x)$ is the count of successes in $n$ independent identical trials — that is, $N(x)\sim\text{Binomial}\big(n,F(x)\big)$, by the indicator decomposition of G2 §4. Now the key translation, and the only idea in the whole subsection: $$\{X_{(k)}\le x\}=\{N(x)\ge k\},$$ in words, "the $k$-th smallest is at or below $x$" says exactly the same thing as "at least $k$ of the samples are at or below $x$". Taking probabilities and summing the binomial PMF over $j=k,\dots,n$ gives the stated formula. $\blacksquare$

Two special cases are already in the notes. For $k=n$ only the $j=n$ term survives and $F_{X_{(n)}}(x)=F(x)^{n}$; for $k=1$ the complementary form is cleaner, $$\mathbf{P}\big(X_{(1)}>x\big)=\mathbf{P}\big(N(x)=0\big)=\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_{X_{(1)}}(x)=1-\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n}.$$ Those are exactly the max and min rules of the recipe in G3 §1 ("multiply the CDFs" and "multiply the tails"), which that section derives directly and applies to exponentials in its Examples 1.7 and 1.8. The theorem above says they are the two ends of one family.

Property (the density of the $k$-th order statistic). If the $X_i$ are i.i.d. with density $f$ and CDF $F$, then $$f_{X_{(k)}}(x)=\frac{n!}{(k-1)!\,(n-k)!}\;F(x)^{k-1}\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n-k}f(x).$$

Derivation (the direct route). For $X_{(k)}$ to lie in the tiny interval $[x,x+\delta]$, three things must happen: one of the samples lands in that interval, exactly $k-1$ of the others land below $x$, and the remaining $n-k$ land above. Count the ways: $n$ choices for which sample sits in the interval, then $\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ choices of which of the remaining $n-1$ go below. Multiply by the probabilities, which are independent across samples: $f(x)\delta$ for the one in the interval, $F(x)^{k-1}$ for those below, $\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n-k}$ for those above. So $$f_{X_{(k)}}(x)\,\delta\approx n\binom{n-1}{k-1}F(x)^{k-1}\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n-k}f(x)\,\delta,$$ and $n\binom{n-1}{k-1}=n\cdot\frac{(n-1)!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!}=\frac{n!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!}$. Dividing by $\delta$ and letting $\delta\to0$ makes it exact. (Differentiating the CDF sum term by term gives the same answer, after a telescoping cancellation.) $\blacksquare$

Interpretation. Read the three factors as a story: $f(x)$ places one observation at $x$, $F(x)^{k-1}$ demands that $k-1$ observations fell to its left, $\big(1-F(x)\big)^{n-k}$ that $n-k$ fell to its right, and the multinomial coefficient counts which observations played which role. Every order-statistic formula in this subsection is that one sentence with different bookkeeping.

The uniform case, and the Beta family

Now specialize to $U_1,\dots,U_n$ i.i.d. $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$, where $F(x)=x$ and $f(x)=1$ on $(0,1)$. The density formula collapses to $$f_{U_{(j)}}(x)=\frac{n!}{(j-1)!\,(n-j)!}\,x^{j-1}(1-x)^{n-j},\qquad 0<x<1,$$ which is the density of a Beta$(j,\,n-j+1)$ random variable: the Beta$(a,b)$ density is $x^{a-1}(1-x)^{b-1}/B(a,b)$, and with $a=j$, $b=n-j+1$ the normalizing constant is $B(a,b)=\frac{(a-1)!(b-1)!}{(a+b-1)!}=\frac{(j-1)!(n-j)!}{n!}$, matching the coefficient exactly. So $$U_{(j)}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(j,\ n-j+1),\qquad \mathbb{E}\big[U_{(j)}\big]=\frac{j}{n+1},\qquad \operatorname{var}\big(U_{(j)}\big)=\frac{j\,(n-j+1)}{(n+1)^{2}(n+2)},$$ using the standard Beta moments $\mathbb{E}=\frac{a}{a+b}$ and $\operatorname{var}=\frac{ab}{(a+b)^{2}(a+b+1)}$ with $a+b=n+1$.

Intuition — the mean without any algebra. The $n$ sample points cut the interval $(0,1)$ into $n+1$ gaps. By symmetry all $n+1$ gaps have the same expected length, and they sum to $1$, so each has expected length $1/(n+1)$. The $j$-th smallest point sits at the end of the $j$-th gap, so $\mathbb{E}[U_{(j)}]=j/(n+1)$ — the sample points are, on average, evenly spaced, but pushed inward: for $n=5$ the largest sits at $5/6=0.833$, not at $1$. This inward bias is the whole content of the estimator bias in G7 §2. The Beta family itself is not new to these notes: G3 §3 meets it as the posterior for a Bernoulli parameter under a Beta prior. The same functional form arises here for a completely different reason, which is a good reminder that a density is a shape, not a story.

Example 2.4 — a tail probability of the sample median, three ways.

Statement. Five points are dropped independently and uniformly on $(0,1)$. What is the probability that the middle one exceeds $0.7$?

Route 1 — the Beta law. $U_{(3)}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(3,3)$ with density $30x^{2}(1-x)^{2}$ (the coefficient is $\frac{5!}{2!\,2!}=\frac{120}{4}=30$; it integrates to $1.000000$, os_f3_norm). Then $$\mathbf{P}\big(U_{(3)}>0.7\big)=\int_{0.7}^{1}30x^{2}(1-x)^{2}\,dx=0.163080$$ (os_p3_beta_tail).

Route 2 — the counting translation, which needs no calculus. By the identity $\{X_{(k)}\le x\}=\{N(x)\ge k\}$, the complement is $\{U_{(3)}>0.7\}=\{N(0.7)\le2\}$: fewer than three of the five points fall below $0.7$. Since $N(0.7)\sim\text{Binomial}(5,0.7)$, $$\mathbf{P}(N\le2)=\sum_{i=0}^{2}\binom{5}{i}(0.7)^{i}(0.3)^{5-i} =0.002430+0.028350+0.132300=0.163080,$$ term by term for $i=0,1,2$ (os_p3_terms) — the same number to six decimals, obtained by arithmetic instead of integration. This equivalence between a Beta tail and a binomial tail is worth remembering; it is the reason the two families keep appearing together.

Route 3 — simulation. Sorting $400{,}000$ samples of five uniforms and counting gives $0.163653$ (os_p3_sim). The standard error of such a fraction is $\sqrt{0.1631\cdot0.8369/400000}\approx0.00058$, so the gap of $0.0006$ from the exact value is about one standard error — a clean agreement, not a discrepancy.

Interpretation. Roughly one time in six the median of five uniform points lands in the top $30\%$ of the interval. Compare a single draw, which exceeds $0.7$ with probability $0.3$: taking a median of five has pulled the distribution sharply toward the center, which is the whole reason sample medians are used.

Exponential order statistics, and the two cross-checks

For $X_1,\dots,X_n$ i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ there is a memoryless shortcut that avoids the density formula entirely. The minimum is the first of $n$ competing exponential clocks, so $X_{(1)}\sim\text{Exp}(n\lambda)$ — "rates add", exactly as G3 §1 derives it — with mean $1/(n\lambda)$. By the memorylessness of the exponential (G4 §3), once the first has fired the remaining $n-1$ clocks start fresh, so the additional wait $X_{(2)}-X_{(1)}$ is $\text{Exp}\big((n-1)\lambda\big)$, independent of the first, and so on. Summing the gaps, $$\mathbb{E}\big[X_{(k)}\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\lambda\,(n-i+1)} =\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\frac1n+\frac{1}{n-1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n-k+1}\right).$$ At $\lambda=1$, $n=5$ this gives $0.200000,\ 0.450000,\ 0.783333,\ 1.283333,\ 2.283333$ against simulated $0.200496,\ 0.449761,\ 0.782750,\ 1.283151,\ 2.283776$ (eo_true, eo_sim). Note the last entry: $\mathbb{E}[X_{(n)}]=\frac1\lambda\big(1+\frac12+\cdots+ \frac1n\big)$, the harmonic number $H_5=2.283333$ — which is exactly the "harmonic pattern" G3 §1 finds for the maximum of three exponentials in its Example 1.7 and its Practice 1.14.

The second cross-check runs into G7 §2. There, $n=5$ observations are drawn uniformly on $[0,\theta]$ and the maximum likelihood estimate of $\theta$ is $\hat\Theta=\max_i X_i=X_{(5)}$. Scaling the uniform result above, $X_{(n)}/\theta\sim\mathrm{Beta}(n,1)$, so $$\mathbb{E}\big[\hat\Theta\big]=\theta\cdot\frac{n}{n+1}=8\cdot\frac56=6.666667$$ (xcheck_max_mean) — the value $6.667$ that G7 §2 obtains, and the source of the bias $-\theta/(n+1)$ it reports. The order-statistic machinery reproduces it in one line, and says at once what the whole distribution of the estimator is, not just its mean.

Practice 2.4. $U_1,\dots,U_n$ are i.i.d. $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$. Find the density and mean of the minimum, two ways.
Solution

Way 1 — the general density with $k=1$. With $F(x)=x$, $f(x)=1$, $$f_{U_{(1)}}(x)=\frac{n!}{0!\,(n-1)!}\,x^{0}(1-x)^{n-1}=n(1-x)^{n-1},\qquad 0<x<1,$$ which is Beta$(1,n)$. Its mean is $\frac{1}{1+n}$ by the Beta formula, or directly: $\int_0^1 x\,n(1-x)^{n-1}dx=\frac{1}{n+1}$ (substitute $w=1-x$ and integrate $n\int_0^1(1-w)w^{n-1}dw=n\big(\frac1n-\frac{1}{n+1}\big)=\frac{1}{n+1}$).

Way 2 — the tail rule of G3 §1. $\mathbf{P}(U_{(1)}>x)=\prod_{i}\mathbf{P}(U_i>x) =(1-x)^{n}$, so $F_{U_{(1)}}(x)=1-(1-x)^{n}$; differentiating gives $n(1-x)^{n-1}$, the same density. And by the tail formula for a nonnegative random variable, $\mathbb{E}[U_{(1)}]=\int_0^1(1-x)^{n}dx=\frac{1}{n+1}$, agreeing. At $n=5$: $1/6=0.166667$ against the simulated $0.166759$ in the table above. It matches the gap picture — the smallest point sits at the end of the first of $n+1$ equal average gaps.

Reading the terms: five clocks race to the first completion (mean wait $1/5$), then four race to the second (mean $1/4$), then three to the third (mean $1/3$).

(b) The last one. The gaps have means $\frac{1}{5},\frac14,\frac13,\frac12,1$ — increasing, because each completion removes one competitor and slows the aggregate rate. The final gap, waiting for the single straggler, averages $1$, five times the first gap; the whole batch takes on average $H_5=2.283333$, more than twice the mean of any single service. This is the standard reason a batch job finishes so much later than its average task: you wait for the maximum, not the mean.

§2 cheatsheet — transformations, uniformity, order statistics (all beyond the 6.041 syllabus)
  • Universality of the uniform. For $F$ continuous and strictly increasing: $F(X)\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$, and $F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$. Both are one line of the CDF method (G3 §4). Fails forward for discrete $X$; the generalized inverse $F^{-1}(u)=\min\{x:F(x)\ge u\}$ keeps the backward direction alive for any CDF at all.
  • Inverse-CDF sampling. $X=F^{-1}(U)$. Exponential: $X=-\ln(1-U)/\lambda$ (verified at $\lambda=1.5$: simulated mean $0.667013$ vs $0.666667$, KS distance $0.000614$). Cauchy: $\tan\big(\pi(U-\tfrac12)\big)$. Logistic: $\ln\frac{U}{1-U}$. Normal: no elementary $\Phi^{-1}$, so use Box–Muller instead.
  • 2-D change of variables. $f_{U,V}(u,v)=f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,|\det J|$ with $J$ the Jacobian of the inverse map, $\det J=x_uy_v-x_vy_u$. Requires one-to-one; the support must be transformed too. One dimension gives back $f_Y(y)=f_X(h(y))|dh/dy|$ of G3 §4.
  • Polar. $\det J=r$, so $f_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)=f_{X,Y}(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\,r$ — the general rule behind "the extra factor $r$" of G3 §1 and G4 §1. For a standard normal pair this gives $R$ Rayleigh, $\Theta$ uniform, independent (Box–Muller). For two i.i.d. exponentials, $(X+Y,\ X/(X+Y))$ is Erlang-$2$ times $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$, independent.
  • Order statistics (i.i.d. continuous, CDF $F$): $\{X_{(k)}\le x\}=\{N(x)\ge k\}$ with $N(x)\sim\text{Bin}(n,F(x))$, hence $F_{X_{(k)}}(x)=\sum_{j\ge k}\binom nj F^j(1-F)^{n-j}$ and $f_{X_{(k)}}(x)=\frac{n!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!}F^{k-1}(1-F)^{n-k}f$. $k=n$ gives $F^n$ and $k=1$ gives $1-(1-F)^n$: the max/min rules of G3 §1.
  • Uniform sample. $U_{(j)}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(j,n-j+1)$, mean $j/(n+1)$, variance $\frac{j(n-j+1)}{(n+1)^2(n+2)}$; the $n$ points cut $(0,1)$ into $n+1$ gaps of equal average length. Range mean $(n-1)/(n+1)$. At $n=5$, $\mathbf{P}(U_{(3)}>0.7)=0.163080$ by Beta tail, binomial tail, or simulation ($0.163653$).
  • Exponential sample. $X_{(1)}\sim\text{Exp}(n\lambda)$ and the gaps are independent exponentials of rates $n\lambda,(n-1)\lambda,\dots$, so $\mathbb{E}[X_{(k)}]=\frac1\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{n-i+1}$; the max has mean $H_n/\lambda$ ($H_5=2.283333$), the harmonic pattern of G3 §1. Cross-check into G7 §2: $\mathbb{E}[\max]=\theta\frac{n}{n+1}=6.666667$ at $n=5$, $\theta=8$.

3.1 The moment generating function

Definition (moment generating function; B&T §4.4). The moment generating function (MGF) of a random variable $X$ is the function of a real variable $t$ $$M_X(t)=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{tX}\big]= \begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_x e^{tx}p_X(x), & X \text{ discrete},\\[6pt] \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{tx}f_X(x)\,dx, & X\text{ continuous},\end{cases}$$ defined for every $t$ for which the sum or integral is finite. Here $t$ is not a random quantity and has no probabilistic meaning at all: it is a bookkeeping variable, a dial you turn to extract information about $X$. The expectation is of the random variable $e^{tX}$, computed by the expected value rule of G2 §2 (discrete) or G3 §1 (continuous) — no new machinery is involved.

Three immediate observations, each one line. First, $M_X(0)=\mathbb{E}[e^0]=\mathbb{E}[1]=1$ for every random variable, which is the cheapest sanity check there is on an MGF you have just computed (all four MGFs differentiated in computes/rf_s3.py return $M(0)=1.000000$). Second, $M_X(t)>0$ wherever it is defined, since $e^{tX}>0$. Third, $M_X$ depends on $X$ only through its distribution — two random variables with the same PMF or PDF have the same MGF, whatever experiment they live on.

Gotcha (existence). Unlike the mean and the variance, an MGF can fail to exist for every $t\ne0$. The sum or integral defining it must converge, and $e^{tx}$ grows fast. Two of the entries in the table below are only defined on part of the line: the exponential MGF needs $t<\lambda$ and the geometric MGF needs $e^t(1-p)<1$, i.e. $t<-\ln(1-p)$ — for $p=0.3$ that is $t<0.356675$ (mgf_geometric_radius). Heavy-tailed distributions such as the lognormal have $M_X(t)=\infty$ for every $t>0$. All the theory below needs $M_X$ finite only on some open interval containing $t=0$, which is why the definition is stated pointwise. (The characteristic function $\mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]$, which is $M_X$ evaluated on the imaginary axis, exists for every random variable and is what a rigorous treatment uses instead; that is a complex analysis course, not this one.)

Why it is called moment generating

Expand $e^{tX}$ in its power series $e^u=\sum_{n\ge0}u^n/n!$ with $u=tX$, and take expectations term by term (legitimate whenever $M_X$ is finite on an interval around $0$; the justification is a convergence theorem outside this course): $$M_X(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^nX^n}{n!}\right] =\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^n}{n!}\,\mathbb{E}[X^n],$$ the second equality using linearity of expectation on each term. So $M_X$ is a power series in $t$ whose $n$th coefficient carries $\mathbb{E}[X^n]$, the $n$th moment of $X$: the MGF packages the whole sequence of moments into one function. Differentiating $n$ times and setting $t=0$ kills every term but one.

Property (moments by differentiation; B&T §4.4). Wherever $M_X$ is finite on an interval around $0$, $$\frac{d}{dt}M_X(t)\Big|_{t=0}=\mathbb{E}[X],\qquad \frac{d^2}{dt^2}M_X(t)\Big|_{t=0}=\mathbb{E}[X^2],\qquad \frac{d^n}{dt^n}M_X(t)\Big|_{t=0}=\mathbb{E}[X^n],$$ and therefore $\operatorname{var}(X)=M_X''(0)-\big(M_X'(0)\big)^2$ by the shortcut formula of G2 §3.

Direct proof of the first one, without the power series: differentiate under the expectation, $$\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{tX}\big]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{d}{dt}e^{tX}\right] =\mathbb{E}\big[Xe^{tX}\big],$$ and set $t=0$ to get $\mathbb{E}[Xe^{0}]=\mathbb{E}[X]$. Differentiating once more gives $\mathbb{E}[X^2e^{tX}]$ and hence $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ at $t=0$, and so on.

Example 3.1 (moments read off three MGFs). Each of the following is differentiated symbolically in computes/rf_s3.py and checked against the mean and variance the notes already derived.
  • Poisson($\lambda=1.5$), $M(t)=e^{1.5(e^t-1)}$: $M'(t)=1.5e^tM(t)$, so $M'(0)=1.5$; $M''(t)=1.5e^tM(t)+\big(1.5e^t\big)^2M(t)$, so $M''(0)=1.5+2.25=3.75$ and $\operatorname{var}=3.75-1.5^2=1.5$ (mom_pois_mean $=1.500000$, mom_pois_EX2 $=3.750000$, mom_pois_var $=1.500000$). Mean $=$ variance $=\lambda$, exactly as G4 §3 has it.
  • Exponential($\lambda=1.5$), $M(t)=\lambda/(\lambda-t)$: $M'(t)=\lambda/(\lambda-t)^2$ gives $M'(0)=1/\lambda=0.666667$, and $M''(t)=2\lambda/(\lambda-t)^3$ gives $M''(0)=2/\lambda^2=0.888889$, so $\operatorname{var}=0.888889-0.666667^2=0.444444=1/\lambda^2$ (mom_expo_mean, mom_expo_EX2, mom_expo_var) — the G3 §1 values.
  • Normal($\mu=1,\sigma^2=4$), $M(t)=e^{t+2t^2}$: $M'(0)=1.000000$, $M''(0)=5.000000$, $\operatorname{var}=5-1=4.000000$ (mom_norm_*).
Notice what did not happen: no integral was evaluated. Once the MGF is known, every moment is calculus on a formula rather than an integration against a density.

3.2 The two properties that make transforms powerful

Moment extraction is a convenience. The following two properties are the reason MGFs exist.

Property 1 (the MGF of a sum is the product of the MGFs; B&T §4.4). If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, then for every $t$ at which both are finite $$M_{X+Y}(t)=M_X(t)\,M_Y(t).$$ Proof, one line. $M_{X+Y}(t)=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{t(X+Y)}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{tX}e^{tY}\big] =\mathbb{E}\big[e^{tX}\big]\mathbb{E}\big[e^{tY}\big]=M_X(t)M_Y(t)$, the first equality by $e^{a+b}=e^ae^b$ and the third by the rule of G3 §2 that expectation is multiplicative for functions of independent random variables — $e^{tX}$ is a function of $X$ alone and $e^{tY}$ a function of $Y$ alone, so if $X\perp Y$ then $e^{tX}\perp e^{tY}$. By induction, $M_{X_1+\cdots+X_n}=\prod_iM_{X_i}$, and for i.i.d. summands $M_{S_n}(t)=\big(M_X(t)\big)^n$.
Property 2 (uniqueness — the MGF determines the distribution). If $M_X(t)$ and $M_Y(t)$ are finite and equal for all $t$ in some open interval containing $0$, then $X$ and $Y$ have the same distribution: the same PMF if discrete, the same PDF if continuous. (The proof is an inversion theorem, well beyond 6.041 and beyond B&T, which states the result and uses it.)
Interpretation. Property 1 converts addition of independent random variables — the operation the whole second half of the course is built on: binomial as a sum of Bernoullis, Erlang as a sum of exponentials, the sample mean of the CLT, random sums in G4 §2 — into multiplication of functions. Property 2 says nothing is lost in the conversion, so you may work entirely in transform space and read the answer off at the end by recognizing the product. That is exactly the role logarithms play for multiplication, or Laplace transforms for differential equations: a hard operation on one side of a dictionary, an easy one on the other.
Gotcha. Property 1 needs independence, not merely uncorrelatedness, and it runs in one direction only. $M_{X+Y}=M_XM_Y$ can hold for dependent $X,Y$ by accident, so observing the factorization does not prove independence — exactly the asymmetry G3 §4 records for $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)=0$.

3.4 The payoff: two theorems the notes prove the long way

G3 §4 establishes that a sum of independent normals is normal by a five-step convolution — expand the exponent, complete the square, do the Gaussian integral, simplify the exponent, simplify the constant. Here is the same theorem in one line, and a second theorem for free.

Fig. 3.1 — The two roads to the distribution of a sum of independent random variables. The upper road is the one G3 §4 takes, because 6.041 never introduces the lower one.
Theorem (sum of independent Poissons is Poisson). If $X\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_1)$ and $Y\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_2)$ are independent, then $X+Y\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$.

Proof. By Property 1 and the table, $$M_{X+Y}(t)=M_X(t)M_Y(t)=e^{\lambda_1(e^t-1)}\cdot e^{\lambda_2(e^t-1)} =e^{(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)(e^t-1)},$$ the last step because $e^ae^b=e^{a+b}$. That is the MGF of a Poisson with parameter $\lambda_1+\lambda_2$, so by Property 2 the sum is that Poisson. $\blacksquare$

The symbolic identity $e^{1.5(e^t-1)}e^{2.5(e^t-1)}=e^{4(e^t-1)}$ is confirmed in computes/rf_s3.py (pois_sum_symbolic_ok $=$ True), and the distributional claim is confirmed independently by brute force: the discrete convolution $\sum_{i=0}^{w}p_X(i)p_Y(w-i)$ for $\lambda_1=1.5,\lambda_2=2.5$ returns $0.018316,\,0.073263,\,0.146525,\,0.195367,\,0.195367,\,0.156293,\ldots$ for $w=0,1,\dots$, matching the Poisson($4$) PMF to $2.8\times10^{-17}$ (pois_conv_pmf, pois_closed_pmf, pois_conv_maxerr). The convolution proof of the same fact requires recognizing $\sum_i\binom{w}{i}\lambda_1^i\lambda_2^{w-i}=(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^w$ after multiplying and dividing by $w!$ — the binomial theorem, hidden inside a factorial manipulation. The transform proof needs no identity at all.

Theorem (sum of independent normals is normal; the G3 §4 result). If $X\sim N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)$ and $Y\sim N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)$ are independent, then $X+Y\sim N(\mu_1+\mu_2,\ \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$.

Proof. $$M_{X+Y}(t)=e^{\mu_1t+\sigma_1^2t^2/2}\cdot e^{\mu_2t+\sigma_2^2t^2/2} =e^{(\mu_1+\mu_2)t+(\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)t^2/2},$$ collecting the coefficients of $t$ and of $t^2/2$ in the exponent. That is the MGF of $N(\mu_1+\mu_2,\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$; apply Property 2. $\blacksquare$

With G3 §4's numbers, $\sigma_1=1$ and $\sigma_2=2$: the product $e^{t^2/2}e^{4t^2/2}=e^{5t^2/2}$ (norm_sum_symbolic_ok), so $X+Y\sim N(0,5)$ with standard deviation $2.236068$, and the $N(0,5)$ density at $w=0,1,3$ equals $0.178412,\,0.161434,\,0.072537$ (norm_sum_density_0_1_3) — the very three numbers G3 §4 obtains by evaluating the convolution integral numerically. The general-parameter check $N(\mu,\sigma^2)+N(2\mu,9\sigma^2)=N(3\mu,10\sigma^2)$ also passes symbolically (norm_sum_general_ok).

Which is less work? The transform route, by a wide margin — and it is not close. The convolution derivation in G3 §4 runs to five labeled steps, needs the completed-square trick and the Gaussian integral $\int e^{-a(x-m)^2}dx=\sqrt{\pi/a}$, and produces two intermediate constants ($a$ and $\gamma$) that exist only to be simplified away. The transform derivation is two applications of $e^ae^b=e^{a+b}$ and a glance at the table. The same asymmetry holds for the Poisson, for the binomial (G3 §4 needs Vandermonde's identity; the MGF needs $(1-p+pe^t)^{n_1}(1-p+pe^t)^{n_2}=(1-p+pe^t)^{n_1+n_2}$), and for the Erlang. What convolution gives you that transforms do not is the shape of the answer when the answer is not a named distribution — the sliding-overlap picture of the triangular density in G3 §4 has no transform analogue. Use convolution to see; use transforms to prove.
Gotcha. Recognition is the whole method, so it only works when the product is in your table. $M_{X+Y}(t)$ for $X,Y$ independent uniforms on $[0,1]$ is $\big((e^t-1)/t\big)^2$, which is a perfectly good MGF and tells you nothing you can name; the triangular density of G3 §4 has to be obtained by convolution (or by inverting the transform, which is harder than the convolution). Transforms shine on closed families, not on arbitrary sums.
Why 6.041 leaves this out. The course states its own boundary: the reading assignment for L12 (random sums) is B&T §4.5 "mean and variance only; no transforms", which is why G4 §2 quotes the transform identity $M_Y(t)=M_N\big(\log M_X(t)\big)$ for a random sum $Y=X_1+\cdots+X_N$ only in a parenthesis and derives the mean and variance by the law of iterated expectations instead. B&T develops transforms properly in §4.4; the lectures skip that section, so G3 §4 routes every sum through convolution and G6 §2 states the central limit theorem without proving it. (The standard proof is a transform argument: the MGF of the standardized sum is $\big(M_{(X-\mu)/\sigma}(t/\sqrt n)\big)^n$ by Property 1, its logarithm expands to $t^2/2+O(n^{-1/2})$, so the MGF converges to $e^{t^2/2}$ — the $N(0,1)$ MGF — and a continuity theorem upgrades that to the CDF convergence G6 §2 states. Every ingredient except the last is on this page.) Knowing that this is the missing tool explains several of the notes' longer derivations at a stroke.
Practice 3.1. $X\sim\text{Exponential}(\lambda)$. Use only the MGF to obtain $\mathbb{E}[X]$, $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ and $\operatorname{var}(X)$, and evaluate them at $\lambda=1.5$.
Solution

From the table, $M_X(t)=\lambda(\lambda-t)^{-1}$ for $t<\lambda$. Differentiate with the power rule and the chain rule (the inner derivative of $\lambda-t$ is $-1$): $$M_X'(t)=\lambda(\lambda-t)^{-2},\qquad M_X''(t)=2\lambda(\lambda-t)^{-3}.$$ At $t=0$: $\mathbb{E}[X]=\lambda/\lambda^2=1/\lambda$ and $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=2\lambda/\lambda^3 =2/\lambda^2$. Hence $$\operatorname{var}(X)=\frac{2}{\lambda^2}-\frac{1}{\lambda^2}=\frac{1}{\lambda^2}.$$ At $\lambda=1.5$: $\mathbb{E}[X]=0.666667$, $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=0.888889$, $\operatorname{var}(X)=0.444444$ (mom_expo_mean, mom_expo_EX2, mom_expo_var) — the values G3 §1 obtains by integrating by parts twice.

Practice 3.2. A random variable $W$ has $M_W(t)=\big(0.7+0.3e^t\big)^5$ for all $t$. Name its distribution, and give $\mathbf{P}(W=0)$.
Solution

The expression matches the table's binomial row $\big(1-p+pe^t\big)^n$ with $p=0.3$ and $n=5$. By Property 2 (uniqueness) no other distribution has this MGF, so $W\sim\text{Bin}(5,0.3)$ and $\mathbf{P}(W=0)=(0.7)^5=0.168070$ (prac32_p0). Check: $M_W(0.4)=(0.7+0.3e^{0.4})^5=1.990000$, and summing $\sum_k\binom5k(0.3)^k(0.7)^{5-k}e^{0.4k}$ directly also gives $1.990000$ (mgf_binom_num_closed, mgf_binom_num_direct). Equivalently, read the MGF as $\big(0.7+0.3e^t\big)^5=\prod_{i=1}^5M_{X_i}(t)$ with each $X_i$ Bernoulli($0.3$): Property 1 says $W$ is a sum of five independent Bernoulli($0.3$) trials, which is the definition of Bin($5,0.3$).

Practice 3.3. $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are i.i.d. Exponential($\lambda$). Find $M_{S_n}(t)$ for $S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n$, and use it to get $\mathbb{E}[S_n]$ and $\operatorname{var}(S_n)$. Evaluate $M_{S_n}(0.5)$ for $n=3,\lambda=2$.
Solution

By Property 1 applied $n-1$ times, and because the summands are identically distributed, $$M_{S_n}(t)=\prod_{i=1}^n M_{X_i}(t)=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-t}\right)^{\!n}, \qquad t<\lambda .$$ This is the MGF of the Erlang of order $n$, whose density $f_{S_n}(s)=\lambda^ns^{n-1}e^{-\lambda s}/(n-1)!$ appears in G4 §3 as the time of the $n$th Poisson arrival; by uniqueness, the two agree. For the moments write $M(t)=\lambda^n(\lambda-t)^{-n}$: $$M'(t)=n\lambda^n(\lambda-t)^{-n-1},\quad M''(t)=n(n+1)\lambda^n(\lambda-t)^{-n-2},$$ so $\mathbb{E}[S_n]=n/\lambda$ and $\mathbb{E}[S_n^2]=n(n+1)/\lambda^2$, giving $$\operatorname{var}(S_n)=\frac{n(n+1)}{\lambda^2}-\frac{n^2}{\lambda^2}=\frac{n}{\lambda^2},$$ which is $n$ times the single-exponential mean and variance, as linearity and independence require. Check at $n=3,\lambda=2,t=0.5$: the closed form gives $(2/1.5)^3=2.370370$, and integrating $\int_0^\infty e^{0.5s}f_{S_3}(s)ds$ numerically gives $2.370370$, difference $4.4\times10^{-16}$ (erlang_mgf_at_0.5, erlang_mgf_numeric, erlang_mgf_abserr).

3.5 Simpson's paradox: when conditioning reverses everything

Now the cautionary tale. G1 §2 taught you to condition and to reassemble conditional answers with the total probability theorem. Simpson's paradox is what happens when someone reassembles without thinking about the weights — and it is not a curiosity, it is the standard failure mode of real-world data analysis.

The paradox (Simpson, 1951). There exist events $A$, $B$, $C$ with all the relevant conditioning events of positive probability such that $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C)<\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C^c)<\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C^c),$$ and yet $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid B)>\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c).$$ In words: $B$ is worse than $B^c$ within the group $C$, worse than $B^c$ within the group $C^c$ — and better than $B^c$ overall. The two strata do not tell you the aggregate, and the aggregate does not tell you the strata.
Example 3.2 (kidney stones — Charig et al., BMJ 1986; a real instance). Two treatments for kidney stones were compared on $700$ patients, $350$ per treatment: open surgery (the older, more invasive operation) and percutaneous nephrolithotomy ("PN", the newer, less invasive one). Why it happens: the weights, not the rates

Nothing paradoxical is going on; the arithmetic is the total probability theorem of G1 §2, applied inside each treatment group. Condition everything on the treatment and partition by stone size: $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid B)=\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C)\,\mathbf{P}(C\mid B) +\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C^c)\,\mathbf{P}(C^c\mid B),$$ which is exactly G1 §2's theorem with every probability conditioned additionally on $B$ (conditional probabilities are probabilities, so all the rules survive — that is the point of G1 §2.1). The aggregate rate for each treatment is therefore a weighted average of its two stratum rates, with weights given by that treatment's case mix. Substituting the numbers: $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid B)=0.866667\times0.771429+0.687500\times0.228571=0.825714,$$ $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c)=0.931034\times0.248571+0.730038\times0.751429=0.780000,$$ and both reproduce the marginal rates exactly as fractions (ks_totprob_PN, ks_totprob_OS, with exactness flags ks_totprob_PN_exact_ok and ks_totprob_OS_exact_ok both True).

Now look at the weights, which is where the whole effect lives. Of the PN patients, $\mathbf{P}(C\mid B)=270/350=0.771429$ had small stones; of the open-surgery patients, only $\mathbf{P}(C\mid B^c)=87/350=0.248571$ did (ks_wsmall_PN, ks_wsmall_OS) — a ratio of $3.103448$ (ks_assoc_ratio). Small stones are the easy cases: both treatments succeed about $0.87$–$0.93$ of the time on them versus $0.69$–$0.73$ on large ones. So PN's aggregate is mostly an average over easy cases and open surgery's is mostly an average over hard ones. PN wins the aggregate not by treating patients better but by treating easier patients — in the real study, surgeons sent the severe cases to open surgery, exactly the sort of association that makes the lurking variable dangerous.

Interpretation (the bottom panel of Fig. 3.2). Write the aggregate as a function of the small-stone share $w=\mathbf{P}(C\mid\text{treatment})$: $$r(w)=r_{\text{large}}+w\big(r_{\text{small}}-r_{\text{large}}\big),$$ a straight line from the large-stone rate at $w=0$ to the small-stone rate at $w=1$. Open surgery's line lies above PN's at every $w$ — that is precisely the two stratum inequalities. The aggregate compares the two lines at different values of $w$: open surgery is read at $w=0.248571$, PN at $w=0.771429$. Comparing points on two curves at different arguments tells you nothing about which curve is higher. That is the entire paradox, in one sentence.

The repair: hold the weights fixed

Recipe (comparing two groups when a lurking variable is present).
  1. Compare within strata first. Report $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C)$ against $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C)$ and $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C^c)$ against $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C^c)$. If both comparisons point the same way, that is the answer, whatever the aggregate says.
  2. Check the case mix. Compute $\mathbf{P}(C\mid B)$ and $\mathbf{P}(C\mid B^c)$. If they differ materially, the aggregate comparison is uninterpretable as it stands.
  3. Standardize. Recombine each group's stratum rates using one common set of weights — for instance the pooled shares $\mathbf{P}(C)$ and $\mathbf{P}(C^c)$ — so that the comparison holds the lurking variable fixed.

For the kidney stones, the pooled shares are $\mathbf{P}(C)=357/700=0.510000$ and $\mathbf{P}(C^c)=343/700=0.490000$ (ks_pool_wsmall, ks_pool_wlarge). Standardizing both treatments to that common mix: $$\text{open surgery: }0.931034\times0.510000+0.730038\times0.490000=0.832546,$$ $$\text{PN: }0.866667\times0.510000+0.687500\times0.490000=0.778875,$$ so open surgery leads by $0.053671$ (ks_std_OS, ks_std_PN, ks_std_gap) — the sign now agrees with both strata, as it must, because with equal weights the difference of the two weighted averages is the weighted average of the two (negative) stratum differences.

Property (no reversal under a common case mix). If $\mathbf{P}(C\mid B)=\mathbf{P}(C\mid B^c)=w$, then $$\mathbf{P}(A\mid B)-\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c) =w\underbrace{\big[\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C)-\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C)\big]}_{\le\,0\text{ by hypothesis}} +(1-w)\underbrace{\big[\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C^c)-\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C^c)\big]}_{\le\,0},$$ a convex combination of two nonpositive numbers, hence nonpositive: no reversal is possible. The paradox needs unequal weights, i.e. it needs $C$ to be associated with $B$. (Check on the kidney-stone rates with the equal weights $w=1-w=\tfrac12$: the aggregate difference PN minus open surgery becomes $-0.053453$ — negative, as the strata demand; noreversal_demo.)
Gotcha (the practical rule). Never aggregate over a variable that is associated with both the treatment and the outcome. Both conditions matter. Stone size is associated with the outcome (easy cases succeed more often) and with the treatment (severe cases were steered to surgery); a variable associated with only one of the two cannot produce a reversal. And notice the direction of the fix: the disaggregated table is the trustworthy one here because stone size is a pre-existing feature of the patient. If the third variable is instead a consequence of the treatment — say, a side effect the treatment causes — then conditioning on it is the mistake and the aggregate is right. Which table to believe is a question about the causal story, not about the arithmetic; the arithmetic is consistent with both.
Where else this bites. The same reversal shows up whenever rates are pooled across unbalanced groups: department-by-department university admissions (each department admits men and women at similar rates, but women apply disproportionately to the competitive departments), hospital mortality rates (the best hospitals receive the sickest patients), and any comparison of "success rates" between two organizations with different intake. The tell is always the same — a striking aggregate difference and an unexamined case mix.

Practice 3.5. A colleague reports: "Across our two clinics combined, drug D cured $60\%$ of patients and drug E cured $70\%$; use E." What two follow-up questions settle whether this conclusion is safe, and what would each answer rule out?
Solution

Question 1: what are the cure rates clinic by clinic? If E beats D in each clinic, the aggregate agrees with the strata and nothing is hidden. If D beats E in each clinic, the aggregate is reversed and the recommendation is backwards.

Question 2: what fraction of each drug's patients came from each clinic? By the no-reversal property above, if the two drugs have the same clinic mix, $\mathbf{P}(\text{clinic 1}\mid D)=\mathbf{P}(\text{clinic 1}\mid E)$, then no reversal is possible and the aggregate is trustworthy. A reversal requires the clinic to be associated with the drug — for instance if the specialist clinic with the harder cases prescribes D more often.

If the mixes differ, ask for the standardized rates: recombine each drug's per-clinic rates with one common set of clinic weights, as in the kidney-stone repair above. And before doing any of this, check the causal direction — the clinic is a pre-existing feature of the patient, so conditioning on it is legitimate; a post-treatment variable would not be.

§3 cheatsheet — transforms and Simpson's paradox
  • MGF. $M_X(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]$, defined where finite; always $M_X(0)=1$. Moments: $\mathbb{E}[X^n]=M_X^{(n)}(0)$; $\operatorname{var}(X)=M''(0)-\big(M'(0)\big)^2$.
  • The two properties. $X\perp Y\Rightarrow M_{X+Y}=M_XM_Y$ (addition becomes multiplication); and the MGF determines the distribution (so recognizing the product finishes the proof). i.i.d. sums: $M_{S_n}=(M_X)^n$.
  • Table. Bernoulli $1-p+pe^t$ · binomial $(1-p+pe^t)^n$ · geometric $pe^t/\big(1-(1-p)e^t\big)$ for $t<-\ln(1-p)$ · Poisson $e^{\lambda(e^t-1)}$ · uniform $(e^{tb}-e^{ta})/\big(t(b-a)\big)$ · exponential $\lambda/(\lambda-t)$ for $t<\lambda$ · normal $e^{\mu t+\sigma^2t^2/2}$.
  • Payoffs in one line each. Poisson$(\lambda_1)+$Poisson$(\lambda_2)=$ Poisson$(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$; $N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)+N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)= N(\mu_1+\mu_2,\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$; Bin$(n_1,p)+$Bin$(n_2,p)=$Bin$(n_1+n_2,p)$; $n$ i.i.d. exponentials $=$ Erlang, $\big(\lambda/(\lambda-t)\big)^n$. G3 §4 proves the first two by convolution, which is strictly more work; transforms are absent from 6.041 by explicit choice (L12: "no transforms"), and B&T §4.4 is the section to read.
  • Simpson's paradox. $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C)<\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C)$ and $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B,C^c)<\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c,C^c)$ are compatible with $\mathbf{P}(A\mid B)>\mathbf{P}(A\mid B^c)$. Kidney stones: open surgery wins $0.931$–$0.867$ on small stones and $0.730$–$0.688$ on large, yet loses $0.780$–$0.826$ overall.
  • Why. Each aggregate is a total-probability weighted average (G1 §2) of that group's own stratum rates, with that group's own weights: $0.771429$ small-stone share for PN against $0.248571$ for open surgery. Equal weights $\Rightarrow$ no reversal possible.
  • Rule. Never aggregate over a variable associated with both the treatment and the outcome; compare within strata, or standardize to a common mix (open surgery $0.832546$ vs PN $0.778875$ at the pooled mix $w=0.510000$). Which table to trust is a causal question: condition on pre-existing covariates, never on post-treatment ones.
