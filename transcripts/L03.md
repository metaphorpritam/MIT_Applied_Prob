**Review**

$$\mathbf{P}(A \mid B) = \frac{\mathbf{P}(A \cap B)}{\mathbf{P}(B)}, \qquad \text{assuming } \mathbf{P}(B) > 0$$

- Multiplication rule:

$$\mathbf{P}(A \cap B) = \mathbf{P}(B) \cdot \mathbf{P}(A \mid B) = \mathbf{P}(A) \cdot \mathbf{P}(B \mid A)$$

- Total probability theorem:

$$\mathbf{P}(B) = \mathbf{P}(A)\mathbf{P}(B \mid A) + \mathbf{P}(A^c)\mathbf{P}(B \mid A^c)$$

- Bayes rule:

$$\mathbf{P}(A_i \mid B) = \frac{\mathbf{P}(A_i)\mathbf{P}(B \mid A_i)}{\mathbf{P}(B)}$$

(The conditional-probability definition, the multiplication rule, the total probability theorem and Bayes rule are all displayed in red on the slide, i.e. highlighted as the key formulas.)

## Slide 3 — Independence of two events (raster p01)

- **"Defn:"** $\mathbf{P}(B \mid A) = \mathbf{P}(B)$
  - "occurrence of $A$ provides no information about $B$'s occurrence"

- Recall that $\mathbf{P}(A \cap B) = \mathbf{P}(A) \cdot \mathbf{P}(B \mid A)$

- **Defn:** $\mathbf{P}(A \cap B) = \mathbf{P}(A) \cdot \mathbf{P}(B)$   *(displayed in red — this is the official definition)*

- Symmetric with respect to $A$ and $B$
  - applies even if $\mathbf{P}(A) = 0$
  - implies $\mathbf{P}(A \mid B) = \mathbf{P}(A)$

## Slide 4 — Conditioning may affect independence (raster p01)

- Conditional independence, given $C$, is defined as independence under probability law $\mathbf{P}(\,\cdot \mid C)$

- Assume $A$ and $B$ are independent

- $A$ — a roughly circular/egg-shaped curve occupying the upper-left of the rectangle; its label "A" sits in its left portion.
- $B$ — a larger, wider oval whose top edge cuts through the lower half of $A$ and which extends down and to the right; its label "B" sits at its lower-middle, below $A$'s boundary. $A$ and $B$ overlap in a lens-shaped region at the lower-right of $A$.

Its label "C" sits in the wide part of the sliver, just to the right of $A$'s right boundary.
The picture is used to argue visually that conditioning on $C$ restricts the sample space to the thin $C$ sliver, inside which the proportions of $A$ and $B$ need no longer factor. | raster/L03_p01.png]

[UNSOLVED EXAMPLE] If we are told that $C$ occurred, are $A$ and $B$ independent?

[DERIVATION GAP: No answer printed on the slide. ## Slide 6 — Independence of a collection of events (raster p02)

- Intuitive definition:
  Information on some of the events tells us nothing about probabilities related to the remaining events
  - E.g.:

$$\mathbf{P}\big(A_1 \cap (A_2^c \cup A_3) \mid A_5 \cap A_6^c\big) = \mathbf{P}\big(A_1 \cap (A_2^c \cup A_3)\big)$$

- Mathematical definition:
  Events $A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_n$ are called **independent** if:

$$\mathbf{P}(A_i \cap A_j \cap \cdots \cap A_q) = \mathbf{P}(A_i)\mathbf{P}(A_j)\cdots \mathbf{P}(A_q)$$

  for any distinct indices $i, j, \ldots, q$, (chosen from $\{1, \ldots, n\}$)

(The "E.g." conditional-probability display and the product formula are shown in red; in the E.g. line the events $A_1$, $A_2^c$, $A_3$ on the left of the conditioning bar are colored to distinguish them from the conditioning events $A_5$, $A_6^c$.)
