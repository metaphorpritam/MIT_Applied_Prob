## Slide 2 — The Bayes variations / Continuous counterpart (raster p01)

**The Bayes variations**

$$\textcolor{red}{p_{X|Y}(x \mid y)} = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x,y)}{p_Y(y)} = \textcolor{red}{\frac{p_X(x)\,p_{Y|X}(y \mid x)}{p_Y(y)}}$$

$$p_Y(y) = \sum_x p_X(x)\,p_{Y|X}(y \mid x)$$

**Example:**

- $X = 1, 0$: airplane present/not present
- $Y = 1, 0$: something did/did not register on radar

---

**Continuous counterpart**

$$\textcolor{red}{f_{X|Y}(x \mid y)} = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)} = \textcolor{red}{\frac{f_X(x)\,f_{Y|X}(y \mid x)}{f_Y(y)}}$$

$$f_Y(y) = \int_x f_X(x)\, f_{Y|X}(y \mid x)\,dx$$

**Example:** $X$: some signal; "prior" $f_X(x)$
$Y$: noisy version of $X$
$f_{Y|X}(y \mid x)$: model of the noise

## Slide 3 — Discrete X, Continuous Y / Continuous X, Discrete Y (raster p01)

**Discrete $X$, Continuous $Y$**

$$\textcolor{red}{p_{X|Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{p_X(x)\, f_{Y|X}(y \mid x)}{f_Y(y)}}$$

$$f_Y(y) = \sum_x p_X(x)\, f_{Y|X}(y \mid x)$$

**Example:**

- $X$: a discrete signal; "prior" $p_X(x)$
- $Y$: noisy version of $X$
- $f_{Y|X}(y \mid x)$: continuous noise model

---

**Continuous $X$, Discrete $Y$**

$$\textcolor{red}{f_{X|Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{f_X(x)\, p_{Y|X}(y \mid x)}{p_Y(y)}}$$

$$p_Y(y) = \int_x f_X(x)\, p_{Y|X}(y \mid x)\,dx$$

**Example:**

- $X$: a continuous signal; "prior" $f_X(x)$ (e.g., intensity of light beam);
- $Y$: discrete r.v. affected by $X$ (e.g., photon count)
- $p_{Y|X}(y \mid x)$: model of the discrete r.v.

[DERIVATION GAP: the slides state these two "mixed" Bayes formulas without deriving them; the underlying limiting argument (conditioning on $\{y \le Y \le y+\delta\}$ and dividing by $\delta$) is not shown.]

## Slide 4 — What is a derived distribution / When not to find them (raster p01)

**What is a derived distribution**

- It is a PMF or PDF of a function of one or more random variables with known probability law. E.g.:

[FIGURE: Small 2-D sketch of a joint density on the unit square. Horizontal axis labeled x, vertical axis labeled y. A square region is drawn with corners at (0,0), (1,0), (1,1), (0,1); tick "1" marked on the y-axis and tick "1" marked on the x-axis. An arrow points into the interior of the square from a label reading "f_{X,Y}(y,x)=1", i.e. the joint PDF is uniform with value 1 on the unit square. | raster/L10_p01.png]

- Obtaining the PDF for
  $$g(X,Y) = Y/X$$
  involves deriving a distribution.
  Note: $g(X,Y)$ is a random variable

**When not to find them**

- Don't need PDF for $g(X,Y)$ if only want to compute expected value:

$$\mathbf{E}[g(X,Y)] = \int\!\!\int g(x,y)\, f_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\,dy$$

Given $X, Y$ uniform on the unit square (joint PDF $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = 1$ on $0 \le x \le 1$, $0 \le y \le 1$), obtain the PDF of $g(X,Y) = Y/X$. The slide poses this as the motivating derived-distribution problem and does not carry out the derivation.

## Slide 5 — How to find them (discrete case) (raster p02)

**How to find them**

- **Discrete case**

  - Obtain probability mass for each possible value of $Y = g(X)$

$$\textcolor{red}{p_Y(y)} \;=\; \mathbf{P}(g(X) = y)$$
$$\;=\; \textcolor{red}{\sum_{x:\, g(x) = y} p_X(x)}$$

[FIGURE: Mapping diagram with two parallel vertical arrow-tipped axes. The left vertical axis is labeled x at its top and carries seven filled dots (possible values of X, numbered 1..7 from top to bottom); the right vertical axis is labeled y at its top and also carries seven filled dots (possible values of Y, numbered 1..7 top to bottom). The label "g(x)" sits in the empty space between the two axes near the top and names the whole family of arrows. Arrows run left-to-right from x-dots to y-dots as follows:
  - x1 → y1 (horizontal)
  - x2 → y2 (horizontal)
  - x3 → y1 (long upward-slanting arrow, crossing the x2→y2 arrow)
  - x4 → y4 (horizontal)
  - x5 → y5 (horizontal)
  - x6 → y5 (upward-slanting)
  - x7 → y5 (longer upward-slanting)
  Thus two arrows converge on y1 and three arrows converge on y5, while y3, y6 and y7 receive no arrow (they are drawn as dots but are not in the range shown). The many-to-one convergence illustrates that $p_Y(y)$ sums $p_X(x)$ over all $x$ with $g(x)=y$. | raster/L10_p02.png]

## Slide 6 — The continuous case (raster p02)

**The continuous case**

- **Two-step procedure:** *(in red)*

  - Get CDF of $Y$: $\;F_Y(y) = \mathbf{P}(Y \le y)$

  - Differentiate to get
    $$f_Y(y) = \frac{dF_Y}{dy}(y)$$

**Example**

- $X$: uniform on $[0,2]$

- Find PDF of $Y = X^3$

- **Solution:**

$$\begin{aligned}
F_Y(y) &= \mathbf{P}(Y \le y) = \mathbf{P}(X^3 \le y) \\
&= \mathbf{P}(X \le y^{1/3}) = \tfrac{1}{2} y^{1/3}
\end{aligned}$$

$$f_Y(y) = \frac{dF_Y}{dy}(y) = \frac{1}{6 y^{2/3}}$$

[DERIVATION GAP: the range of validity is not stated on the slide — the expressions hold for $0 \le y \le 8$ (since $X \in [0,2]$ gives $Y \in [0,8]$), with $F_Y(y)=0$ for $y<0$ and $F_Y(y)=1$ for $y>8$. Also, the step $\mathbf{P}(X \le y^{1/3}) = \tfrac12 y^{1/3}$ uses the uniform CDF $F_X(x)=x/2$ on $[0,2]$ without comment.]
