**Recurrent and transient states**

- State $i$ is **recurrent** if:
  starting from $i$,
  and from wherever you can go,
  there is a way of returning to $i$

- If not recurrent, called **transient**

- **Recurrent class**:
  collection of recurrent states that
  "communicate" to each other
  and to no other state

## Slide 4 — Periodic states (raster p01)

- The states in a recurrent class are **periodic** if they can be grouped into $d > 1$ groups so that all transitions from one group lead to the next group

[FIGURE: Two schematic "group" diagrams illustrating periodicity, drawn side by side.
(a) LEFT — period $d = 2$: two tall, narrow, vertically-oriented rounded rectangles ("pill" shapes, the groups) stacked one above the other with a gap between them. Two long arcs sweep around the left side from the *lower* group up into the *upper* group (both arrowheads entering the upper rectangle from the left), and two long arcs sweep around the right side from the *upper* group down into the *lower* group (both arrowheads entering the lower rectangle from the right). So: group 1 → group 2 → group 1, alternating with period 2.
(b) RIGHT — period $d = 3$: three rounded rectangles arranged in a triangle (top-left, top-right, bottom-center). Two parallel horizontal arrows go from the top-left rectangle to the top-right rectangle; two arrows go diagonally down-left from the top-right rectangle into the bottom-center rectangle; two arrows go diagonally up-left from the bottom-center rectangle into the top-left rectangle. Cyclic flow: top-left → top-right → bottom → top-left, period 3. | raster/L17_p01.png]

[FIGURE: A second, state-level example drawn below: a directed graph of 9 unlabeled circles forming **two directed cycles that share one common node**. The shared node sits at the bottom center of the picture. The LEFT cycle is a 6-step directed loop: from the shared bottom-center node the arc goes left to a bottom-left node, then up-left to a far-left node, then up-right to an upper-left node, then right to a top node, then down-right to a mid-right node, and then down (vertical arrow) back into the shared bottom-center node. The RIGHT cycle is a 4-step directed loop: from the shared bottom-center node a horizontal arrow goes right to a bottom-right node, then a vertical arrow up to a right node, then up-left to a top-right node, then a long diagonal arrow down-left back into the shared bottom-center node. ## Slide 5 — Steady-State Probabilities (raster p02)

- Do the $r_{ij}(n)$ converge to some $\pi_j$?
  (independent of the initial state $i$)

- Yes, if:
  - recurrent states are all in a single class, and
  - single recurrent class is not periodic

- Assuming "yes," start from key recursion
$$r_{ij}(n) = \sum_k r_{ik}(n-1)\, p_{kj}$$
  - take the limit as $n \to \infty$
$$\pi_j = \sum_k \pi_k p_{kj}, \qquad \text{for all } j$$
  - Additional equation:
$$\sum_j \pi_j = 1$$

[DERIVATION GAP: the passage to the limit is done formally — the slide does not justify interchanging the limit with the (possibly infinite) sum, nor does it prove the steady-state convergence theorem it invokes. It also does not show why the normalization equation $\sum_j \pi_j = 1$ is needed (the balance equations alone are singular / determine $\pi$ only up to a scale factor).]

## Slide 6 — Visit frequency interpretation (raster p02)

$$\pi_j = \sum_k \pi_k p_{kj}$$

- (Long run) frequency of being in $j$: $\pi_j$

- Frequency of transitions $k \to j$: $\pi_k p_{kj}$

- Frequency of transitions into $j$: $\displaystyle\sum_k \pi_k p_{kj}$

From each of the three left circles a straight arrow points into the central circle (from top-left going down-right, from middle-left going horizontally right, from bottom-left going up-right). The central circle also has a **self-loop** drawn as a narrow vertical ellipse above it, with the arrowhead pointing back down into the circle. Three arrows leave the central circle and point outward to the right (one up-and-right toward the top-right corner, one horizontally right, one down-and-right toward the bottom-right corner), with no destination circles drawn. The picture illustrates that the long-run frequency of transitions *into* $j$ is $\sum_k \pi_k p_{kj}$. | raster/L17_p02.png]

| raster/L17_p02.png]

Cut (detailed balance) equation across the dashed line:
$$\pi_i p_i = \pi_{i+1} q_{i+1}$$

- Special case: $p_i = p$ and $q_i = q$ for all $i$
  $\rho = p/q =$ load factor
$$\pi_{i+1} = \pi_i \frac{p}{q} = \pi_i \rho$$
$$\pi_i = \pi_0 \rho^{\,i}, \qquad i = 0, 1, \ldots, m$$

- Assume $p < q$ and $m \approx \infty$

$$\pi_0 = 1 - \rho$$

$$\mathbf{E}[X_n] = \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho} \qquad \text{(in steady-state)}$$

[DERIVATION GAP: the cut equation $\pi_i p_i = \pi_{i+1} q_{i+1}$ is asserted from the "frequency of transitions across the cut must balance" argument, without deriving it from the general balance equations $\pi_j = \sum_k \pi_k p_{kj}$.]

[DERIVATION GAP: $\pi_0 = 1-\rho$ is stated without showing the normalization step $\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \pi_0 \rho^i = \pi_0/(1-\rho) = 1$.]

[DERIVATION GAP: $\mathbf{E}[X_n] = \rho/(1-\rho)$ is stated without the computation — it follows from recognizing $\pi_i = (1-\rho)\rho^i$ as a geometric PMF on $\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ whose mean is $\rho/(1-\rho)$.]
